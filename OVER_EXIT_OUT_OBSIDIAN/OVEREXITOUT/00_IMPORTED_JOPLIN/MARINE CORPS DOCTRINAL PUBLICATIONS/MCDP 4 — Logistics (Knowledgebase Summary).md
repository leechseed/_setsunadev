# MCDP 4 — Logistics (Knowledgebase Summary)

## Abstract
MCDP 4, *Logistics* (21 March 2023), revises the 1997 edition to establish the Marine Corps' theory and philosophy of military logistics in a globally contested, multi-domain environment across the competition continuum. It argues that logistics is the engine of strategy and the bridge between the Nation's economy and its warfighting forces, that it is both an enabling and a limiting factor setting the parameters of what is strategically achievable, operationally feasible, and tactically possible, and that it must be conducted according to the philosophy of maneuver warfare. The publication defines logistics and its characteristics, sets out seven principles, the logistics process, six logistics functions, and the logistics system, describes the joint logistics enterprise across the tactical, operational, and strategic levels, addresses how Marines, training, education, and equipment are prepared for logistics, and closes with leadership, command and control, planning, shaping actions, visibility, and risk. It demands that all Marines understand logistics, that commanders base concepts of operations on what they can do logistically, and that the force be able to operate when logistics is contested.

## Section-by-Section Breakdown

### Foreword / Preface
- The publication describes the theory and philosophy of military logistics as practiced by the Marine Corps and revises the 1997 MCDP 4, placing its time-tested principles in an updated warfighting context grounded in MCDP 1.
- It addresses logistics in a globally contested, multi-domain environment across the competition continuum, requiring Marines to operate when logistics is contested and to leverage strategic- and operational-level logistics.
- It is intended for all Marines, is constructed like MCDP 1 to be read cover to cover, offers ideas rather than techniques or procedures, and uses historical lessons and realistic fictional illustrations (two scenarios, "The Great Pacific War" and "Welcome to the Theater," precede Chapters 1 and 4).

### Chapter 1 — The Nature of Logistics
This chapter defines logistics as the engine of strategy and the bridge between the Nation's economy and its forces, traces its evolution, situates it in a contested operational context, and establishes it as a warfighting function conducted under maneuver warfare.

#### What is Logistics?
- Logistics is the aspect of military operations dealing with the procurement, transportation, and maintenance of military materiel, facilities, and personnel; it provides for the physical needs of a force.
- Logistics converts national resources into combat power, transforming people, natural resources, and industrial capacity into units, weapons, equipment, and supplies.
- Logistics can be decisive; battles, allies, and popular opinion have turned on how a force is moved and supplied.
- Logistics is both an enabling and a limiting factor, setting the parameters for what is strategically achievable, operationally feasible, and tactically possible.

#### The Evolution of Logistics
- The industrial revolution enlarged the scale of warfare and made logistics an essential prerequisite for war, shaping decisions on whether, where, and when to campaign.
- Information systems accelerate real-time planning and execution and increase demand for responsive sustainment; logistics information must yield actionable knowledge, not merely data.
- Technology will change the character of war but will not eliminate the need to move large quantities of fuel, water, and ammunition.
- "Hybrid logistics" blends old, new, and emerging technologies and is best suited to support Marines across the competition continuum and a distributed force design.

#### Operational Context
- Logistics capabilities are critical requirements and potential vulnerabilities that become focal points for enemy attack; the logistics system must be designed for a lethal, distributed, multi-domain threat environment.
- Threats exist in all domains, including espionage, information, space, and cyberspace; the cumulative effect makes the entire logistics network a target, and the system must survive destructive and disruptive action.
- Logistics is integral to deterrence and assurance below the threshold of violence through forward presence, forward-positioned stocks, and in-theater capabilities that convey commitment and credibility.
- When the threshold of violence is crossed, logistics provides the physical means for conflict; offensives cannot be sustained indefinitely, and plans that ignore logistics capacity cause the force to culminate before achieving objectives.

#### Logistics Characteristics
- Logistics is both art (planning that balances efficiency and effectiveness) and science (calculating requirements, managing inventories, developing detailed plans); it is defined by results rather than activities.
- Logistics is complex; military organizations are interdependent adaptive systems in which disruption in one part has disproportionate effects, mitigated by understanding critical connections and making anticipatory plans.
- Teamwork is essential; the commander is ultimately responsible, but unity of effort driven by teamwork and trust is the key.

#### Logistics as a Warfighting Function
- Logistics is one of the seven warfighting functions and must be viewed as part of an interdependent whole with command and control, intelligence, fires, maneuver, force protection, and information.
- Logistics sustains the people and materiel of command and control, is both provider and consumer of intelligence, feeds the fuel, ammunition, and engineering that fires and maneuver require, provides the mobility and regenerative capacity of force protection, and depends on secure and resilient information.

#### Logistics and Maneuver Warfare
- Maneuver warfare guides the logistics approach: flexibility and agility are built into plans so forces can exploit opportunities, while logistics is prevented from becoming a critical vulnerability.
- Logistics aligns to the main effort according to the commander's priorities and generates tempo by delivering supplies, repositioning forces, and repairing or replacing equipment.
- Marines apply the maneuver warfare philosophy to logistics all the time, not just in war.
- Chapter conclusion: logistics provides, positions, and sustains the materiel needs of military action; it is globally contested in every domain; treated as art and science and conducted under maneuver warfare, it extends the reach and resiliency of the force.

### Chapter 2 — The Theory of Logistics
This chapter sets out Marine Corps logistics theory as principles, processes, and functions put into action by a logistics system, and describes how sourcing and distribution are designed and how the theory is tested.

#### Logistics Principles
- Seven principles guide effective logistics: responsiveness, economy, flexibility, attainability, sustainability, simplicity, and survivability.
- Responsiveness, the keystone, is providing the right support in the right place at the right time, achieved by early identification of requirements, reserves as a hedge against uncertainty, webs rather than single chains, cross-training, and staging before need.
- Economy is employing the minimum resources required within acceptable risk; the art of logistics balances effectiveness (outcome-based) against efficiency.
- Flexibility is the ability to adapt logistics structure and plans to evolving situations, with branches and sequels; attainability is the point at which the commander judges sufficient supplies and support exist to initiate operations at acceptable risk, enabled by demand and supply visibility.
- Sustainability is maintaining necessary support levels for long-term objectives, balancing self-sufficiency and dependency; simplicity minimizes complexity through clear, standardized, interoperable processes and reduces friction.
- Survivability is the capacity to prevail despite adverse impacts, achieved by avoidance, dispersion, hardening, mass, deception, defensive lethality, and the means to regenerate; resilient logistics underpins every principle.

#### The Logistics Process
- The process consists of four essential actions: sourcing, distribution, sustainment, and disposition.
- Sourcing obtains goods and services; distribution moves people and materiel from origin to point of need, including lift, staging, packaging, and tracking.
- Sustainment provides logistics and personnel services to maintain and prolong operations; disposition consumes, returns, or disposes of weapons, equipment, and supplies.

#### Logistics Functions
- The six logistics functions organize what needs to be done: supply, maintenance, transportation, general engineering, health service support, and services.
- Supply covers procurement, distribution, storage maintenance, and salvage of supplies; maintenance keeps or restores equipment to serviceable condition; transportation moves people and materiel by any mode with enabling activities.
- General engineering creates infrastructure and modifies, maintains, or protects the physical environment; health service support minimizes the effect of injury and disease; services include religious ministries, financial management, billeting, messing, contracting, postal, legal, civil affairs, and mortuary affairs.
- Each function is integrated into a concept of support; the functions are interdependent and their scale, scope, and character differ at each level of warfare.

#### The Logistics System
- A logistics system produces, positions, and sustains combat power and has three fundamental parts: an organizational construct, sourcing methods, and a distribution model.
- The organizational construct comprises command relationships (responsibility and authority within a unit) and support relationships (purpose, scope, and responsibilities among units).
- Units may be organized functionally, by task (multifunctional), or geographically; a mix provides commanders a range of options and a hedge against uncertainty.

#### Sourcing Methods
- Sourcing methods include acquisition, procurement, contracting, foraging, capturing, and manufacturing, with overlapping responsibilities among units and agencies.
- Beyond large-scale acquisition, Marines source through capture, on-site manufacture or rebuild, local purchase, host-nation contracts, cross-service agreements, pre-staging, and forward production such as additive manufacturing.
- All units are empowered to identify resource and capability gaps to inform institutional sourcing.

#### Distribution
- A distribution model is a template for how people, equipment, and supplies travel from origin to receiving point, tailored to the mission and warfighting style, and built from places, platforms, and procedures.
- A supply chain is efficient but breaks when a single link fails; a supply web provides multiple sourcing and distribution options and resiliency at greater cost and coordination.
- Distribution nodes range from caches and combat outposts to advanced bases and sea bases; seabasing is a Marine Corps hallmark, and forward locations balance security against responsiveness through nodal analysis.
- Platforms provide tactical battlespace distribution, operational intratheater lift, and strategic intertheater lift; innovations in signature, autonomy, and space may generate tempo.
- Procedures identify requirements by push (scheduled, forecast) or pull (requested) methods, usually in combination, with discipline against the "logistics snowball effect" of inflated requirements.
- Distribution methods are point (receiving unit collects), unit (issuing unit delivers), and blended (shared responsibility), chosen with regard to consumption, communications reliability, signature, and threat.

#### Testing the Theory
- Logistics theories require periodic analysis; concepts are ideas awaiting validation, and operational concepts are validated through logistics feasibility analysis.
- Wargaming, modeling, and simulation test logistics feasibility and refine theory as technologies and ideas emerge.
- Chapter conclusion: theory shapes planning and execution; the logistics system turns theory into actionable parts consisting of command and control, processes and procedures, places, and platforms, executed by people in various organizational configurations.

### Chapter 3 — The Logistics Enterprise
This chapter describes how the Marine Corps logistics system interacts with the joint logistics enterprise across the tactical, operational, and strategic levels and identifies who performs logistics at each level.

#### Logistics and the Levels of Warfare
- The joint logistics enterprise is a multitiered matrix of global logistics providers structured through agreements, contracts, policy, legislation, and treaties, encompassing all Services, the combatant commands, and the defense industrial base.
- Effective military activity depends on integrating logistics at all three levels; raising forces is easier than concentrating and supplying them in theater.
- Tactical logistics sustains forces, operational logistics sets the theater, and strategic logistics provides the materiel means of war; expeditionary operations depend on operational and strategic logistics.

#### Tactical Logistics
- Tactical logistics sustains forces directly involved in deterrence and assurance, crisis response, or war, supporting the concept of operations while preventing premature culmination.
- It is conducted primarily within the Marine air-ground task force, whose elements share logistics responsibilities and none of which is fully independent.
- The supporting-supported relationship between the Marine Corps and Navy shifts over time, and the task force routinely requires augmentation from Service and joint enterprise sources.

#### Operational Logistics
- Operational logistics links strategic means to tactical employment by positioning and sustaining forces in a geographic area; its goal is to enable campaigns to accomplish national objectives.
- It focuses on setting the theater: a theater distribution system of bases, intratheater lift, campaign sourcing through joint enterprise coordination and regional contract support, and campaign-level planning.
- Combatant commanders integrate and synchronize limited resources; Marine Corps component commanders set conditions for employing Marine forces and may establish a logistics support organization.

#### Strategic Logistics
- Strategic logistics produces the means of war by leveraging the Nation's materiel resources; it focuses on infrastructure (bases and stations), acquisition and procurement, enterprise inventory management, and strategic lift.
- Enterprise inventory management covers materiel readiness, prepositioning, and war reserves; strategic lift is transportation within the United States and between theaters.
- Strategic logistics is conducted by Service headquarters, the Office of the Secretary of Defense, Joint Staff, US Transportation Command, Defense Health Agency, Defense Logistics Agency, interagency, allies, partners, industry, and academia.

#### Roles and Responsibilities
- The Commandant organizes, trains, and equips forces; the Service acquires and procures materiel, administers land and infrastructure, allots resources, and exercises institutional command and control of logistics, integrating logistics into force design, posture, mobilization, training, and talent management.
- Department of Defense and interagency organizations serve as leads for functional responsibilities and classes of supply, such as US Transportation Command, the Under Secretary for Acquisition and Sustainment, and the Defense Logistics Agency.
- The defense industrial base, including the organic industrial base for depot maintenance, and academia supply capabilities, technologies, and techniques, with the caution that commercially efficient methods may not succeed in war.
- Allies and partners are force multipliers providing access, basing, overflight, transportation, security, and local supplies; trust earned through forward presence, exercises, and interoperable systems is the catalyst for combined logistics.
- Chapter conclusion: the joint logistics enterprise is the bridge between the Nation's economy and its forces; the Marine air-ground task force focuses on tactical logistics, Service headquarters on strategic logistics, and the component commander links them at the operational level.

### Chapter 4 — Logistics Preparation
This chapter addresses the traits individual Marines need to conduct logistics, how training and education develop them, the logistics considerations of equipping the force, and how logistics prepares forces for deployment over the short and long term.

#### Logistics and the Individual Marine
- People are essential to logistics; organizational models and procedures work only when used by committed, competent, well-led Marines, and logistics is a matter of attitude and cooperative team effort.
- Initiative generates tempo, enabled by familiarity with the commander's intent and the supported unit's requirements.
- Proficiency, multidisciplinary skill, attention to detail, and teamwork built on trust before contact with the enemy are required by the technical and multi-organizational nature of logistics.
- Adaptation and innovation overcome fog and friction, fostered by diverse teams, an innovative culture, and testing against threats; human-machine teaming and talent management aligning Marines' skills and interests with billets are logistics readiness issues.

#### Logistics Training and Education
- Training is continuous, challenging, integrated, and progressive, woven realistically into every opportunity; integrated training between logistics and supported units builds trust and refines support concepts.
- Training must interject friction, uncertainty, and hardship and force Marines to perform above their experience; failure is part of learning.
- Education develops understanding of how logistics enables and limits warfighting, of the joint logistics enterprise, and of history, theory, organization, and technology, as part of lifelong cross-discipline learning.

#### Logistics Considerations for Equipment
- Expeditionary equipment must be scalable, resilient, reliable, easy to maintain, and logistically sustainable; operational benefits must be weighed against logistics costs and the "soldier's load."
- Modular and multifunctional equipment lets Marines aggregate or disaggregate force packages; equipment must withstand austere environments, and light, self-sufficient forces reduce footprint and signature.
- Compatibility with joint logistics enterprise transportation and especially naval integration is crucial; equipment should be purpose-built for operations with the Navy and easy to load, offload, and reconfigure at sea.
- Logistics units require adaptable, scalable, redundant, and survivable equipment matched to the supported force; logistics information systems must be interoperable, protected, few, simple, and capable of predictive analysis.

#### Logistics Preparation for Deploying Forces
- Logistics sets conditions for rapid deployment through force generation: generating the force (sourcing personnel and equipment), readying the force (training support), and deploying the force.
- In permissive environments units use an efficient force closure model; in contested environments they adopt a power projection approach, prepared to fight on arrival; a purely permissive approach is unlikely in a globally contested environment.
- A resilient distribution system combines sustainability and survivability; installations serving as nodes must prepare to be attacked, and deception and signature management support deployment in contested conditions.

#### Logistics Preparations over Time
- Many support requirements, such as installations and acquisition programs, take years or decades to develop and require foresight, diligence, and many stakeholders.
- Logistics includes quality-of-life considerations for Marines and families that form resilient communities and let Marines focus on the mission.
- Chapter conclusion: Marines are the heart of the expeditionary force; equipment and supplies must account for sustainment in austere environments; training and education reward initiative and build cohesion; investing in Marines yields readiness dividends now and over time.

### Chapter 5 — Logistics Planning and Execution
This chapter explains how leaders use command and control to integrate logistics with the other warfighting functions through planning, shaping actions, visibility, adjustment, and risk management, and closes the publication with a call for continued debate and development.

#### Leadership
- Commanders lead the logistics effort by stating clear intent, ensuring logistics is considered throughout planning, determining feasibility, and setting the tone for logistics discipline, accountability, and prioritization.
- Leaders forge teams from diverse specialists, teach, mentor, and train, and create a culture of ingenuity; leadership failures underlie logistics failures.

#### Command and Control
- Command and control establishes what will be done, how, and by whom, while logistics sets the limits on what a unit can do; logistics is the engine and command and control the gas pedal and steering wheel.
- Logistics informs decision making on how quickly forces can be positioned, how long they can be sustained, and what resources are required.
- Priorities drive resource allocation; processes and procedures govern the system and enable decentralized problem solving; information technology systems automate routine functions but are vulnerable, so alternatives without connectivity must be developed and rehearsed.

#### Logistics Planning
- Planning anticipates requirements, sequences resources, integrates each warfighting function, and matches movement and sustainment to force requirements while evaluating feasibility.
- Understanding the logistics environment includes threats to and from the logistics footprint, the physical network of nodes, throughput, and sourcing and storage options, and the resources available across the force and joint logistics enterprise.
- Developing options uses the quantifiable nature of logistics to predict requirements, maximizes the ways requirements can be met, addresses each logistics function and its relation to each warfighting function, and incorporates threat mitigation; flexibility and simplicity are particularly significant.

#### Logistics Shaping Actions
- Shaping actions occur before the threshold of violence to deter adversaries and set sustainment conditions; logistics is most relevant to operational-level shaping, or setting the theater.
- Setting the theater maintains or prepares a distribution system, identifies facility shortfalls, lift gaps, bottlenecks, and node vulnerabilities, and invests in places and platforms before conflict.
- Forward-positioning increases responsiveness and survivability, but over-reliance creates risk from enemy targeting or host-nation political shifts, mitigated through force protection and diplomacy.
- Force posture provides the nodes of theater distribution and depends on host-nation agreements for bases, contract support, and overflight; where posture is unavailable, the naval expeditionary force must be as self-sufficient as possible until strategic and operational logistics arrive.

#### Logistics Visibility and Adjustments
- Logistics visibility enables responsive support by monitoring sourcing options, infrastructure, throughput velocity, materiel readiness, casualties, and supply levels; information must be gathered, processed, analyzed, and shared.
- Visibility and analysis shape decisions to adjust tasks, priorities, and distribution; feedback from units tells decision makers when change is required.
- Technology multiplies visibility and facilitates reallocation, but the enemy will attempt to compromise logistics information networks, so systems must be hardened, alternatives prepared, and signatures managed or manipulated to deceive.

#### Risk
- Every operational decision must include an understanding of logistics risk, the probability and consequence of harm to something valued; the commander avoids, accepts, mitigates, or transfers risk and communicates the decision.
- The reach of a force is a logistics factor tied to munitions, fuel, maintenance, and transportation; commanders with a keen sense for logistics make better risk-informed decisions.
- Risk exists across the entire enterprise, including budgetary pressures and espionage against the joint logistics enterprise, industry, and academia; adherence to the logistics principles limits risk, and wargames, rehearsals, and assessments uncover it.
- Publication conclusion: MCDP 4 is the beginning, not the end, of logistics discussion; logistics is essential to warfighting, is the heartbeat of the Corps, and is like oxygen, transparent to the user unless in short supply; Marines are called to read, think, and write about logistics and to uphold the relentless spirit exemplified by First Lieutenant Alexander Bonnyman, Jr. at Tarawa.

## Mental Models

### Model 1 — Engine and Bridge
- **Model:** Logistics is the engine of strategy and the bridge that converts the Nation's economy into combat power at the point of need.
- **Use:** Judge any concept of operations first by whether the bridge can carry it; commanders base concepts on what they can do logistically.

### Model 2 — Enabler and Limiter
- **Model:** Logistics simultaneously enables and limits, setting the parameters of the strategically achievable, operationally feasible, and tactically possible.
- **Use:** Identify the culminating point implied by lift, sustainment, and reach before committing to an offensive.

### Model 3 — Chain versus Web
- **Model:** A supply chain is efficient but breaks at a single link; a supply web is costlier but resilient through multiple sourcing and distribution options.
- **Use:** In contested environments, design webs and caches over optimized chains, and accept redundancy as the price of effectiveness.

### Model 4 — Effectiveness over Efficiency
- **Model:** The art of logistics balances outcome-based effectiveness against resource efficiency, accepting inefficiency to ensure the mission.
- **Use:** When priorities conflict, resolve toward the commander's main effort rather than toward minimum cost.

### Model 5 — Push and Pull
- **Model:** Requirements are met by scheduled forecast (push) or by request (pull), usually in combination, and discipline prevents the logistics snowball.
- **Use:** Push routine sustainment, pull emergent needs, and train disciplined requirement generation before crisis.

### Model 6 — Three Levels, One Enterprise
- **Model:** Tactical logistics sustains forces, operational logistics sets the theater, and strategic logistics provides the materiel means, all linked through the joint logistics enterprise.
- **Use:** Locate every requirement at its level and identify who owns it before assuming the Marine air-ground task force must solve it alone.

### Model 7 — Survivable by Design
- **Model:** Logistics nodes are high-value targets; survivability comes from avoidance, dispersion, hardening, mass, deception, defensive lethality, and regeneration.
- **Use:** Plan for the logistics system to be hit and rehearse how it regenerates.

## Argument Map
- **Claim 1:** Logistics converts national resources into combat power and sets the parameters of what is achievable, feasible, and possible.
  - **Therefore:** Commanders must base concepts of operations on what they can do logistically, and all Marines must understand logistics.
- **Claim 2:** The operating environment is globally contested in every domain across the competition continuum, and the entire logistics network is a target.
  - **Therefore:** The logistics system must be designed for survivability and resiliency, with webs, dispersion, deception, and the means to regenerate.
- **Claim 3:** Logistics is both art and science and is governed by seven principles, a four-step process, and six functions, put into action by a system of organization, sourcing, and distribution.
  - **Therefore:** Support concepts must be built from these elements and tested through wargaming and feasibility analysis rather than improvised.
- **Claim 4:** Expeditionary operations depend on operational and strategic logistics provided through the joint logistics enterprise, allies, and partners.
  - **Therefore:** Marines must be proficient at higher-level logistics and build trust and interoperability with enterprise partners during competition.
- **Claim 5:** People, training, education, equipment choices, leadership, and command and control determine whether logistics generates tempo or friction.
  - **Therefore:** The force must be prepared deliberately over time, and commanders must lead logistics and manage its risk as part of every decision.
- **Conclusion:** Marine Corps logistics, conducted under the philosophy of maneuver warfare, extends the reach, endurance, and survivability of the force in a contested world and is essential to fighting and winning the Nation's battles.

## Key Concepts & Definitions
- **Logistics**: The aspect of military operations that deals with the procurement, transportation, and maintenance of military materiel, facilities, and personnel, providing for the physical needs of a force.
- **Hybrid Logistics**: A timeless blend of old, new, and emerging technologies providing a menu of support options for a distributed force.
- **Competition Continuum**: The range of actions below and above the threshold of violence, from deterrence and assurance through crisis to conflict and war.
- **Culminating Point**: The point at which a force can no longer sustain the attack and reverts to the defense.
- **Responsiveness**: Providing the right support in the right place at the right time; the keystone principle.
- **Economy**: Employing the minimum resources required, within acceptable risk, to create a specific effect.
- **Flexibility**: The ability to adapt logistics structure and plans to evolving situations, missions, and concepts of operation.
- **Attainability**: The point at which the commander judges that sufficient supplies and support capabilities exist to initiate operations at acceptable risk.
- **Sustainability**: The ability to maintain the necessary levels of logistics support to achieve long-term military objectives.
- **Simplicity**: Minimizing logistics complexity through clear, standardized, interoperable processes and clearly defined command relationships.
- **Survivability**: The capacity of an organization to prevail despite adverse impacts or potential threats.
- **Logistics Process**: The four essential actions of sourcing, distribution, sustainment, and disposition.
- **Logistics Functions**: Supply, maintenance, transportation, general engineering, health service support, and services.
- **Logistics System**: The organizational construct, sourcing methods, and distribution model required to produce, position, and sustain combat power.
- **Supply Chain**: A network that transforms raw materials into positioned capabilities efficiently but is degraded when a single link breaks.
- **Supply Web**: A network of interrelated connections providing flexibility and resiliency through multiple sourcing and distribution options.
- **Push Method**: Providing resources on a forecast, predetermined schedule.
- **Pull Method**: Providing resources upon request from the requiring unit.
- **Logistics Snowball Effect**: The cumulative burden created when units overstate support requests.
- **Joint Logistics Enterprise**: A multitiered matrix of global logistics providers structured through agreements, contracts, policy, legislation, and treaties to support the joint force commander.
- **Setting the Theater**: Operational-level shaping that establishes a theater distribution network, supports force posture, and forward-provisions equipment and supplies.
- **Force Generation**: The systematic, Service-wide approach to selecting, resourcing, and preparing units for deployment.
- **Force Closure and Power Projection**: Deploying through multiple, often commercial, platforms and locations in a permissive environment, versus deploying in combat formations prepared to fight on arrival in a contested one.
- **Logistics Visibility**: Insight into sourcing options, infrastructure condition, throughput velocity, materiel readiness, casualties, and supply levels that enables responsive support and adjustment.
- **Risk**: The probability and consequence of an event potentially causing harm to something valued, which the commander avoids, accepts, mitigates, or transfers.
