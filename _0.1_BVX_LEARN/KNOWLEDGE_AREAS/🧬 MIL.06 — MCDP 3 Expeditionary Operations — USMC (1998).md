## CORE THESIS
In a post-Cold-War world of crises that are smaller, more frequent, more varied, and concentrated in the urbanized littorals of the developing world, national interest requires a force that is expeditionary first as a mindset — bags packed, austere, adaptable, self-sustaining — and that projects power into a foreign setting quickly by sea, no larger or heavier than the mission requires, sets the conditions for decisive action, and withdraws or reconstitutes for the next mission; the Marine Corps is organized as that force, and its capstone operating concept is Operational Maneuver from the Sea.

---

## INVARIANTS
*These are the unchanging principles — true regardless of context.*

1. **Expeditionary is a mindset before it is an organization, equipment, or tactic.** "Bags packed" — ready and willing to deploy on a moment's notice, any time, any place, any mission — with a Spartan pride in enduring austerity. The mindset is a product of training and institutional culture and must be continuously imparted.

2. **An expedition is temporary and austere by definition.** Temporary: the intent is to withdraw after the specified mission; a permanent or indefinite presence is a station, not an expedition. Austere: no larger or heavier than necessary; supplies and infrastructure limited to operational necessity; operational considerations prevail over quality of life.

3. **Threats have decreased in magnitude and increased in number, frequency, and variety.** The environment is disorder — disasters, disruptions, disputes — in the developing world's littorals, driven increasingly by nonstate actors using asymmetric and cheap lethal means. A crisis-response force is designed for range, not against one threat.

4. **There is no alternative to the sea for moving and sustaining sizable forces, or to naval power for global projection.** The sea is at once an avenue for friendly movement, a barrier to the enemy, a secure and retrievable base, and a means of avoiding disadvantageous engagement.

5. **What matters is how fast capable, sustainable forces close — not how fast the first forces arrive.** The initial force at a crisis is rarely the decisive force, so the organization must expand in theater without losing continuity of operational capability.

6. **Forcible-entry capability is a permanent requirement.** Environments fluctuate between permissive and hostile, the level of hostility changes quickly, and entry is the period of greatest risk.

7. **Exit is part of the entry decision.** Every expedition redeploys; departure must maintain the desired political situation; the transition to a longer-lasting presence is often the hardest phase.

8. **Cost-effectiveness is economy of force, not cheapness.** Too small a commitment early leads at best to a larger commitment later and at worst to a failed mission; the standard is no greater cost or commitment than is necessary to accomplish the mission properly.

9. **Adaptability requires a learning organization.** Self-critical, changing quickly on experience, with innovation bubbling up from operating units as well as descending from programs. Leaders exercise judgment on situational awareness rather than applying techniques by rote.

## HEURISTICS
*Rules of thumb for real-time decision-making.*

| Situation | Do This | Not This |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| Sizing the force | Task-organize to the mission from modular building blocks; no larger or heavier than necessary; bring everything needed | Deploy the standard package; build the garrison |
| A crisis is developing | Close the nearest capable, sustainable force; loiter offshore if needed; expand by echelon behind it | Wait until the decisive force is assembled; send first-arrivers who cannot sustain themselves |
| Deciding to intervene | Decide the exit and the redeployment at the same time as the entry | Enter now and work out the departure later |
| Choosing how to enter | Use the sea as an avenue; land where the enemy is weak at the time and place of your choosing; maneuver ship-to-objective | Seize a beachhead, build up supplies, then fight a pitched battle to break out |
| Basing the force | Sea-base what can be sea-based — aviation, logistics, command and control — to shrink the footprint, the rear-area security bill, and the redeployment burden | Establish large supply dumps ashore that dictate and limit operational direction and range |
| Mission complete, another emerging | Reconstitute — regenerate, reorganize, replenish, reorient from the forward position | Return to home base to reset |
| Operating short of war | Expect restrictive rules of engagement, host-nation coordination, and daily media; measure success by the political condition | Apply combat-optimized methods and search for a clean military objective that is not there |
| An improvisation works | Turn it into innovation — adopt, refine, institutionalize, pass on | Treat it as a one-off and wait for a formal program |
| Designing the organization | Balanced, general-purpose, routinely reorganized | Specialized single-mission units whose effectiveness reorganization degrades |
| Infrastructure is inadequate | Offload pierside or at sea; operate from austere sites; bring the support with you | Wait for a deep-draft port and a large airfield |

## CORE CONCEPTS

**1. The Landscape — Disaster, Disruption, Dispute**
Crises fall into three classes: disasters (calamities causing mass suffering, natural or manmade), disruptions (intentional disorder — insurgency, terrorism, crime — sufficient to interfere with government function), and disputes (open clash between political groups, internal or external). They escalate: a disaster becomes a disruption when the response fails; a disruption becomes a dispute when the disruptive element can openly challenge authority. Drivers: simultaneous fragmentation and integration ("fragmegration") producing a tightly coupled, sensitive global system; population growth, urbanization, and the youth bulge in the developing world; 60 percent of humanity within 100 kilometers of the sea; cheap, lethal, unregulable weapons; nonstate actors who will not fight on our terms.

**2. Expeditionary Operations Defined**
An expedition is a military operation to accomplish a specific objective in a foreign country; its defining characteristic is the projection of force into a foreign setting — deployment to the scene with the support to sustain it, some distance from home. Power projection is necessary but not sufficient; operations without actual deployment are not expeditionary. Presence works only because of demonstrated capability to land. Logistics — mounting the expedition — is central. Composition follows mission: aviation with a security force, combat service support for relief, a battalion on two ships for an embassy evacuation, two million troops for a world war.

**3. The Sequence of Projection**
Predeployment (organize the deployment so forces arrive in logical sequence with the right equipment and sustainment) → deployment (airlift fastest but needs a secure airhead; sealift the only way to move significant sustainable force; prepositioning combines both) → entry (introduction onto foreign soil — greatest risk; needs an entry point; forcible entry the hardest case) → enabling actions (seize the port or airfield, establish logistics, restore initial order — set the stage) → decisive actions (create the conditions that accomplish the political objective) → redeployment (depart or transition to a longer-lasting presence in a way that maintains the political result). A framework for thinking, not prescribed phases.

**4. The Expeditionary Mindset**
Bags packed. Every Marine is part of an organization designed to project force overseas, and all operating forces — not selected ready units — stay deployable. Spartan attitude: embarkation boxes for bookcases even in garrison. Versatility and adaptability to respond without much preparation time to a broad range of circumstances, and a global perspective oriented to diverse threats rather than one region.

**5. Requirements of an Expeditionary Force**
Naval character (the sea for mobility, sea basing, forcible entry — amphibious landing is the Marine Corps' specialty). Strategic mobility — closure rate and reach — through forward-deployed MAGTFs afloat (continuous movement, indefinite loiter), maritime prepositioning, and rapid deployability by sea and air. Operational mobility along the coast, using the sea as an avenue of approach (Inchon). Competence — the consistent ability to get things done, built on experience above all. Sustainability, including self-contained sustainment until a theater system exists, and the advantage of a force that can operate austerely. Adaptability through improvisation (an untested response to the unexpected) and innovation (systematic adoption of what works). Reconstitution — regenerate and reorient for a new mission from forward without returning home. Cost-effectiveness through forward deployment, routine proficiency at deploying, and versatile equipment.

**6. The MAGTF**
The Marine air-ground task force is the principal organization for every mission: a balanced combined-arms force with four elements — command, ground combat, aviation combat, combat service support — task-organized from building blocks so that reorganization is routine rather than degrading. Sizes: the Marine expeditionary force (division, wing, force service support group; 60 days' sustainment; deploys by echelon behind a MEF (Forward)); the MEU(SOC) (reinforced battalion, composite squadron, 15 days; the standing forward-deployed force, often first on scene, conducting enabling actions for what follows); the special purpose MAGTF for a narrow mission. Expandability without loss of continuity is the key design feature; sea-based MAGTFs give decision-makers a rheostat of options for how much to put ashore.

**7. Operational Maneuver from the Sea**
The capstone concept: an amphibious operation that uses the sea as an avenue for maneuvering against an operational-level objective — a center of gravity or critical vulnerability — at the time and place of our choosing, avoiding strengths and striking weakness. Two aspects: operational maneuver (turning the flank, threatening lines of operation — Inchon, the Marianas) and from the sea (move by sea, deploy near the crisis, project and sustain from the sea, redeploy to it). Implementing concepts: ship-to-objective maneuver, which fuses the ship-to-shore movement and "subsequent operations ashore" into one decisive maneuver, eliminating the beachhead buildup that gave defenders time; and sea basing of aviation, logistics, and most command and control, which frees the landing force from protecting dumps ashore and enlarges its freedom of action. Applies across the range of operations, not only high-end combat.

**8. Supporting Concepts**
Sustained operations ashore: MAGTFs fighting essentially as land forces within a joint force, best employed as independent, self-contained combined-arms formations (Desert Storm). Military operations other than war: political constraints dominate, objectives are limited and hard to measure, host-nation and media coordination is daily, and most still apply operational maneuver from the sea principles (Eastern Exit: 466 nautical miles, two refuelings, less than 24 hours). Maritime prepositioning force operations: a deployment concept — fly-in echelon linked with prepositioned ships (one squadron equals more than 3,000 airlift sorties; a MEF lead echelon ready within days); no forcible-entry capability alone; future pillars are at-sea force closure, integration with the amphibious task force, indefinite sea-based sustainment, and in-theater reconstitution.

## WHAT DOESN'T WORK
Preparing against a single known enemy, garrison assumptions and permanent-station habits, heavy footprints ashore, beachhead buildup followed by breakout, measuring response by the arrival of the first units, undersizing the early commitment to save cost, deciding entry without exit, single-mission organizations that reorganization degrades, innovation only from formal top-down programs, waiting for deep-draft ports and large airfields, combat-optimized methods in operations short of war, expeditionary as equipment rather than mindset, land-based staging dependent on host-nation permission, quality-of-life over operational considerations, selected ready units instead of a fully deployable force, judging readiness by the scale of what can be brought rather than by what the mission needs.

## APPLICATION — THE OPERATION
*Mapping this publication onto a solo creator-operator running projects with an AI partner.*

- **Expeditionary mindset → launch light with what you have.** Doctrine: expeditionary is a state of mind — bags packed, Spartan, versatile, global; embarkation boxes for bookcases. Translation: a project starts with the sources on hand and the kit already in the repo; the standard project flow (BOLO 25) must be runnable from a cold start with no assembly period; the working set stays portable.

- **Sequence of projection → the project lifecycle.** Doctrine: predeployment → deployment → entry → enabling → decisive → redeployment, a framework not phases; entry is the riskiest moment; enabling sets the stage, decisive accomplishes the objective. Translation: read STATE.md (predeployment), open the working set (deployment), first artifact committed (entry), format contract and scaffolding (enabling), the deliverable (decisive), cache note + STATE update + `_LOG/` move (redeployment). Naming the phase exposes the stall: most stalls are enabling actions mistaken for decisive ones.

- **Exit strategy → define done at "how copy."** Doctrine: the exit is part of the entry decision and departure must preserve the political result. Translation: every line in STATE.md carries its exit condition; *how copy?* is the moment the exit condition is read back before execution starts.

- **Closure of capable, sustainable force → the first deliverable must stand on its own.** Doctrine: first arrival is not the metric; capable, sustainable force closing is; the organization expands without losing continuity. Translation: a fast draft nobody can build on is first-arrivers without sustainment; the first artifact is the one later work expands — the template, the frontmatter schema, the `feeds:` wiring.

- **Reconstitution → "Oscar Mike" and "sweep."** Doctrine: regenerate, reorganize, replenish, and reorient for a new mission from the forward position without returning home. Translation: *Oscar Mike* parks the current work in place and moves to the next mission with the same kit; *sweep* is the redeployment that promotes and clears. The operation never returns to zero to reset.

- **Sea basing and small footprint → retrievable support.** Doctrine: keep logistics, aviation, and command and control afloat; reduce what must be protected ashore; simplify redeployment. Translation: scratchpad for working files, repo for the deliverable, nothing accumulating in the project that is not the mission; what is easily retrieved is easily left behind.

- **Enabling vs decisive → sit rep Block IV.** Doctrine: the initial force sets conditions; decisive actions accomplish the objective; a force that only enables has not finished. Translation: the PMCS/BOLO block separates maintenance (enabling) from the main effort (decisive); a sit rep that shows only enabling actions for several sessions is the warning the doctrine gives.

- **Disaster → disruption → dispute → escalation ladder.** Doctrine: a disaster becomes a disruption when the response fails; a disruption becomes a dispute when the disruptive element can openly challenge authority. Translation: for the collision engine, a three-rung model of how a story world's instability becomes open contest; for the operation, a triage for incoming problems — accident, sabotage, or contest — each with a different response.
