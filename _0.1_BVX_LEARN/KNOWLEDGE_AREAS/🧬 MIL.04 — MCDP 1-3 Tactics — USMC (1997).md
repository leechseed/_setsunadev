## CORE THESIS
Tactics is the art and science of achieving a decision — a result that reaches beyond the engagement itself — by focusing combat power through a single main effort against a critical vulnerability, acting consistently faster than the opponent can react, and ruthlessly exploiting every advantage until the issue is settled; no formula produces this, only military judgment built through study, drill, and free-play practice.

---

## INVARIANTS
*These are the unchanging principles — true regardless of context.*

1. **Combat is probabilistic, not deterministic.** Battle is the collision of animate, interactive, unpredictable wills; outcomes are anticipated only as probabilities. Treating combat as a closed mechanical system produces centralized control, prescriptive orders, and units that are overwhelmed the moment events depart from the script.

2. **There are no fixed rules — but there are bounds.** No formula, process, acronym, or buzzword answers the tactical question; every situation is different. History is studied to build one's own judgment, never to retrieve someone else's scheme.

3. **A decision must lead to a result beyond itself.** An engagement is decisive only if it feeds directly into campaign or war success. Incremental gains that connect to nothing waste every life, hour, and round spent on them.

4. **Decisiveness is never sought for its own sake.** The deck is stacked before the cards are laid on the table — the situation is shaped so the battle goes your way. Seeking decision without that preparation is an irresponsible gamble.

5. **Speed is relative and must be continuous.** Speed matters only if you act faster than the opponent, and intermittent bursts hand the initiative back during every halt. Time is a weapon; idleness is a cost paid to the enemy.

6. **Advantage does not finish the enemy; exploitation does.** The enemy does not surrender for being placed at a disadvantage. Victory is the product of small advantages exploited immediately and repeatedly until one becomes decisive and the action turns into a rout.

7. **Control in combat is a delusion; cooperation is what harmonizes.** Chaos breaks control down, and attempts to impose it kill the initiative tactics depends on. What keeps dispersed units working toward one result is cooperation — the union of self-discipline and initiative in pursuit of a common goal.

8. **Failure to act is worse than an honest error.** In situations clearly requiring independent decision the leader has a duty to make one. Inaction from a failure of moral courage breaks faith in the chain of command; a judgment error from sincere effort does not.

## HEURISTICS
*Rules of thumb for real-time decision-making.*

| Situation | Do This | Not This |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| Any new situation | Ask "In this situation, what efforts will be decisive?" — and keep asking as the situation changes | Retrieve what someone else did in a slightly similar situation |
| Choosing where to strike | Hit a *critical* vulnerability — the capability without which the enemy cannot function — strength against weakness, where attention is not focused | Attack a target because it is vulnerable, or attack the front because that is where the enemy is |
| Many actions competing for resources | Designate one main effort; every other unit asks "How can I best support the main effort?"; when a neighbor breaks through, shift the designation and move the support with it | Spread effort evenly; leave the original main effort in place while the breakthrough goes unsupported |
| Time available before engagement | Analytical decisionmaking — generate and compare options | Rush an intuitive call when there is time to deliberate |
| Engaged, time short | Intuitive decisionmaking — recognize the pattern, act on it | Stall for a complete comparison of options or for more information |
| Enemy has a workable response | Build a dilemma (combined arms, fix-and-flank): every move to avoid one threat exposes them to another | Present a single problem they can solve by moving |
| You have just succeeded | Exploit — pour through the gap, pursue, disrupt in depth; consolidate only when the gain is worth surrendering the initiative | Stop to eat, consolidate by reflex, "possess the field" |
| Situation still forming | Set the stage and wait — let the ambush fill, let the diversion take effect | Counterattack too soon and merely push back what could have been encircled |
| Planning under uncertainty | A few branches and sequels; modular, loosely coupled, simple plans; a reserve sized to the uncertainty | Many tightly coupled branches no one can rehearse; everything committed at once |
| Pressure to move now | Speed: reconnaissance done, support arranged, every available person brought up, then launch on a predetermined plan | Haste: commit early without recon or support and finish very slowly |
| Approaching a transition | Drill the friction point — assembly-to-attack, patrol-to-ambush, defense-to-counterattack; rehearse task-organization changes | Treat transitions as pauses and improvise the reorganization under fire |

## CORE CONCEPTS

**1. Achieving a Decision — Military Judgment**
Judgment is understanding the situation plus acting decisively. Understanding: work METT-T (mission, enemy, terrain and weather, troops and support, time), assume the enemy's role, and ask what will be decisive — repeatedly, as the situation changes. Acting: identify critical vulnerabilities, shape the operating area to keep several options open, designate a main effort, and pursue the goal with boldness (dare to win big) and ruthlessness (increase the pressure once ahead), both tempered by judgment. The core skill is pattern recognition — filling in the picture correctly from a few pieces (coup d'oeil).

**2. Main Effort**
Of every action under way, one is most critical to success at this moment; the unit assigned it is the main effort, the focal point on which the force's combat power converges. It receives priority for every kind of support and harmonizes subordinate initiative — faced with a decision, everyone asks how best to support it. Main effort implies economy of force: not using as little as you can get away with, but wasting nothing — uncommitted forces support indirectly through feints, demonstrations, and tying down enemy reserves. The main effort shifts as events unfold, and a shift that does not move the support is merely nominal.

**3. Leverage over Muscle**
Advantage comes from using the opponent's strength and momentum against him, not from overpowering him. The tools: combined arms (a no-win dilemma across arms), maneuver in space and in time, exploiting terrain, weather, and darkness, complementary forces (the cheng that fixes and the ch'i that bids for decision — interchangeable), surprise through deception, stealth, and ambiguity, trapping rather than pushing (a pushed enemy returns tomorrow; a trapped one is destroyed), and asymmetry — fighting on your terms with dissimilar means.

**4. The Ambush Mentality**
Turn every situation into an ambush. Five features: surprise is the rule, not the exception; the enemy is drawn into the trap, usually by making one course of action look inviting; the ambush is invisible until it is too late to react (a visible ambush is a target); shock — hold fire until everything is in range, then open all at once; and the focus is the enemy, not the terrain. The reverse-slope defense, the artillery raid, the fastbreak, the trap block — all are ambushes.

**5. Speed, Time, and Tempo**
Speed is more than miles per hour. It is physical speed on the approach (cuts reaction time), through the enemy (changes the situation faster than he can react), and past him (makes reaction irrelevant); it is using every minute even while stationary; it is timing — knowing when not to act; it is relative to the enemy; it must be continuous; and it is fast transitions, because transitions are where friction eats tempo. Boyd's OODA loop names the mechanism: while the enemy observes and orients on your first action you are acting on your second; the gap widens with each cycle; everything he does is too late.

**6. Becoming Faster**
Five levers. Simplicity in plans, staffs, command and control, and action — large staffs are among war's greatest consumers of time. Decentralization through mission tactics (assign the task and its purpose, not the how) and commander's intent (so subordinates adapt without losing unity of effort). Experience — real or synthetic through decision games, sand tables, and rehearsals — which also yields implicit communication (shared understanding needing few words) and lateral communication (leaders talking to leaders, not up and down the chain). The commander positioned at the point of friction. Fitness, so speed is sustained.

**7. Adapting**
Adaptability is shortening the time it takes to adjust to each new situation. Two modes: anticipation (planning, immediate-action drills, standing operating procedures — pre-designed responses that seize initiative until considered action is possible) and improvisation (creative, intuitive adjustment on the spot). Enablers: flexible plans with a few branches (options within the current operation) and sequels (what follows victory, defeat, or stalemate); modular, loosely coupled plans, so changing one part does not force changing all; and decentralized authority to the commander closest to events, always within the higher intent.

**8. Exploiting Success and the Reserve**
Casualty rates stay roughly even until one side tries to flee; the rout is where decisive results come from. Three modes of exploiting an advantage: consolidation (protect the gain, knowingly surrender initiative), exploitation (disorganize the enemy system in depth), pursuit (catch and destroy a force that has lost cohesion). The reserve is combat power withheld to influence future action — the tool for both opportunity and crisis. The more uncertain the situation, the larger the reserve; give it the best unit or the mobility assets; reconstitute it as soon as it is spent; once committed it usually becomes the main effort. Intangibles — fatigue, morale, sortie rates, low-density munitions, fuel — are reserves too.

## KEY VOCABULARY

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Tactics | The art and science of winning engagements and battles — creative employment of combat power (art) resting on mastered techniques and procedures (science) |
| Combat power | The total destructive and disruptive force a unit can apply against the opponent at a given time — physical, moral, and mental factors combined |
| Decision (decisive) | A battle or engagement whose result leads directly to larger success in the campaign or war; the opposite of incremental gain |
| Military judgment | The developed ability to understand a situation and act decisively; honed by experience, education, and practice |
| Pattern recognition / coup d'oeil | Grasping the true significance of a situation from limited information — "stroke of the eye," tactical sense |
| Critical vulnerability | A vulnerability whose exploitation destroys a capability without which the enemy cannot function effectively |
| Shaping | Lethal and nonlethal actions that make the enemy vulnerable, dictate the time and place of battle, and preserve multiple friendly options |
| Main effort | The unit assigned the mission most critical to success at that moment; the focal point of the force's combat power and support |
| Economy of force | Making effective use of every asset — forces not directly supporting the main effort support it indirectly |
| Combined arms | Integrating arms so that any action the enemy takes to avoid one threat increases vulnerability to another — a dilemma, not a problem |
| Cheng / Ch'i | Sun Tzu: the direct, fixing action / the unexpected, extraordinary action that bids for decision; interchangeable in battle |
| Trapping | Destroying the enemy's ability to think through a dilemma, as opposed to pushing him off ground he retakes tomorrow |
| Ambush mentality | Turning every situation into an ambush: surprise, lure, invisibility, shock, focus on the enemy |
| Tempo | Speed plus timing — generating and maintaining a fast pace when it counts and recovering when it does not hurt |
| Friction point | A transition in time and space — formation to formation, posture to posture — where tempo is lost or preserved |
| OODA loop | Boyd's observation–orientation–decision–action cycle; conflict as time-competitive cycles, maneuver as being consistently faster |
| Mission tactics | Assigning a mission and its purpose without specifying how it is to be accomplished |
| Commander's intent | The purpose behind the task, enabling subordinates to adapt without diffusion of effort |
| Implicit communication | Mutual understanding requiring little or no talk — faster and more reliable than explicit communication |
| Branch / Sequel | Branch: an option within the current operation for changing conditions. Sequel: the course of action following a probable outcome |
| Coupling | The degree to which parts of a plan depend on each other; loosely coupled plans change without cascading |
| Reserve | Combat power initially withheld to influence future action — the tool for clinching victory or averting crisis |
| Consolidation / Exploitation / Pursuit | Protect the gain / disorganize the enemy in depth / catch and destroy a force attempting to escape |
| Cooperation | The union of self-discipline and initiative in pursuit of a common goal; the component of control that survives chaos |

## WHAT DOESN'T WORK
Deterministic force-ratio prediction, centralized control and micromanagement, overly prescriptive orders and plans, incrementalism and attrition (a hill here, a town there, body counts), pushing contests that let the enemy come back tomorrow, one-handed punching (one arm at a time), symmetrical fighting (tanks against tanks), seeking decision before the deck is stacked, boldness without judgment (recklessness) and ruthlessness without judgment (cruelty), haste mistaken for speed, intermittent bursts of speed with long halts, large staffs, detailed tightly coupled plans, more branches and sequels than can be rehearsed, counterattacking too soon, consolidating by reflex, throwing in the last reserve early, discipline that is imposed only, school solutions in critiques, damaging a leader's self-esteem in public, waiting for war to begin one's education.

## APPLICATION — THE OPERATION
*Mapping this publication onto a solo creator-operator running projects with an AI partner.*

- **Main effort → the one live line.** Doctrine: the unit whose mission is most critical right now gets priority for all support, and a redesignation that does not move the support is nominal. Translation: at any moment STATE.md names one main effort; every other thread answers "how does this support it?"; when a side project breaks through, the AI partner's attention moves with the redesignation. The proword *main effort* is this doctrine verbatim.

- **Achieving a decision → the stall pattern's antidote.** Doctrine: a result is decisive only if it produces something beyond the engagement; incremental gains that connect to nothing are waste. Translation: a finished design blocked on one unanswered decision is an indecisive battle. The standard project flow (BOLO 25) needs a decision gate — "what result beyond this task does it produce, and what decision does it wait on?" — and the decision gets made, not deferred and rebuilt.

- **OODA / tempo → the sit rep cadence.** Doctrine: conflict is time-competitive observe-orient-decide-act cycles; orientation is where the plan is built. Translation: *sit rep* is the observe-orient phase run on demand; the board it triggers (Block 0 cache, Block IV BOLO/PMCS) is the orientation product. A sit rep that does not end in a decision has not closed the loop.

- **Mission tactics + commander's intent → "how copy."** Doctrine: assign the mission and its purpose, leave the how to the subordinate; implicit communication built on shared SOP is faster than explicit. Translation: *how copy?* ends orders; "Good copy" plus readback confirms the intent landed; execution is then decentralized to the AI partner without step-by-step approval. SOP.md is the shared experience that makes implicit communication possible.

- **Critical vulnerability + shaping → "boresight."** Doctrine: a target is not worth attacking because it is vulnerable; find the capability whose loss stops the enemy from functioning, and shape so options stay open. Translation: *boresight* re-aims the current effort at the blocker that actually gates the project — usually the unanswered decision — rather than the most visible or most comfortable task.

- **Speed vs haste → the launch gate.** Doctrine: haste commits without reconnaissance or support and finishes slowly; speed does the reconnaissance, brings up every available asset, then launches on a predetermined plan. Translation: the project flow's start gate is Patton's list — sources gathered, format contract read, prowords set — after which the run does not pause for approvals.

- **Reserve and friction points → uncommitted capacity and drilled transitions.** Doctrine: withhold capacity to exploit opportunity or meet crisis, larger when uncertainty is higher; drill the transitions, because that is where tempo is lost. Translation: never plan a session to 100 percent; session open (read STATE.md, challenge/countersign) and session close (cache note, STATE update, BOLO capture, `_LOG/` move) are the operation's battle drills.
