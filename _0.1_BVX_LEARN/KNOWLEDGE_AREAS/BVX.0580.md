# BVX.0580 — Genre and Hollywood — Steve Neale (2000)
### Knowledge Entry — Distill

The L7 shelf's institutional counter-weight: an empirically driven takedown of genre-as-textual-essence that relocates genre in the industry's inter-textual relay, the critic's corpus, and the audience's regime of verisimilitude. It is the Command's source for treating "audience" and "market" as genre fields in their own right, not afterthoughts to "genre" and "medium."

## 1 · CORE THESIS

Genre is a multi-dimensional process, not a fixed set of textual traits: it runs between Hollywood's industry and inter-textual relay, the films themselves, and audience expectation held in regimes of verisimilitude. Industrial, marketing, critical, and audience uses of a genre label routinely diverge, so no single corpus or definition can serve all four at once.

## 3 · FRAMEWORK / STRUCTURE

Three parts, each correcting the excesses of the one that would follow if left unchecked.

- **Part I (ch. 1-2), definitions and concepts.** Traces genre theory's history (Alloway's iconography, Buscombe/McArthur, Ryall's audience-and-industry triangle, Tudor's "empiricist dilemma," Hirsch/Derrida/speech-act theory) to one conclusion: genre is ubiquitous, multi-dimensional, and not exclusive to Hollywood. Chapter 2 adds verisimilitude and the inter-textual relay as the mechanisms that actually produce genre-in-use.
- **Part II (ch. 3-5), genres surveyed.** A stock-taking of fourteen "major genres" at summary level, then two extended case studies, film noir and melodrama, chosen because they break the assumption built up in Part I that critical and industrial genre terms coincide.
- **Part III (ch. 6-7), theory, industry and history.** Chapter 6 audits aesthetic theories (Schatz's "predetermined" genre-film, Russian Formalist stage theory, Altman's semantic/syntactic split, Jauss and Cohen's genre-as-process) and socio-cultural theories (ritual, ideological, Kapsis's "production of culture" alternative). Chapter 7 tests all of it against actual Variety output in the mid-1930s and mid-1980s, and concludes that cycles, production trends, budget tiers, star-genre formulations, and hybrids outnumber and outrank "genre" as Hollywood's own planning unit in both eras.

The whole book argues by demonstrated exception. Every chapter produces a case (the western's atypical iconography, noir's non-existence as an industry term, melodrama's opposite trade meaning, 1930s Variety's sixty-one-category "Genre Index") that a tidier theory would have to suppress.

## 4 · KEY CONCEPTS

| Concept | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Genre as multi-dimensional** | Expectations, categories, labels, discourses, texts, corpuses, and the conventions governing all of them, never one dimension alone | Founding move. Every later argument refuses to let one dimension, usually "the text," stand in for the whole |
| **Iconography** (Panofsky via Alloway, Buscombe, McArthur) | Recurring visual motifs (guns and cars in gangster films, castles and coffins in horror) read as a genre's visual conventions | Fits the western and gangster film well but fails for the biopic, social problem film, and romantic drama. Genres are heterogeneous in what defines them |
| **Two verisimilitudes** (Todorov, Culler) | Generic verisimilitude is plausible by the genre's own rules (singing in a musical); cultural verisimilitude is plausible by everyday belief | Every genre balances the two. Non-realist genres carry a heavier expositional burden since they cannot borrow real-world norms |
| **Inter-textual relay** (Lukow and Ricci) | Posters, trailers, stills, reviews, credit sequences, and programming, the industry's own machinery for a film's "narrative image" | The primary evidence for what a genre actually was at a given moment, not what critics later say it was |
| **Genre film vs generically marked film** (Altman, refined by Neale) | "Genre film" conforms self-consciously to a model; Neale separates the moment of production (modelled) from reception (marked) | Keeps genre-as-category distinct from genre-as-corpus, and lets parody be theorized without assuming all genre use is self-conscious |
| **Genre as process** (Jauss, Cohen) | Genres are not fixed essences but a continual founding and revising of "horizons of expectation" as each new text joins the corpus | The book's load-bearing claim. It makes melodrama's and noir's shifting meanings normal genre behavior, not definitional failure |
| **Semantic / syntactic approach** (Altman) | Semantic elements are a genre's building blocks; syntax is the structural relations they are arranged into | Useful but porous. Neale shows the frontier is unclear, since a shoot-out can be either depending on the genre's stage |
| **Ritual vs ideological theories** (Cawelti/Schatz vs Wright/Screen) | Ritual holds genres are audience-authored cultural expression validated by box office; ideological holds genres manipulate audiences for capital | Neale rejects both as unfalsifiable. Box-office success indexes popularity, not ideological consent, and popularity is never culturally uniform |
| **Production of culture perspective** (Kapsis) | Genre and cycle output explained by the interorganizational network of studios, distributors, gatekeepers, censors, and markets | Preferred alternative. The early-1980s horror cycle ended over foreign-market saturation fears, not declining domestic interest |
| **Film noir as a critical category** | Coined by French critics (Nino Frank, 1946) after the fact, never used by the contemporary American industry or press | Central case study. A term with no consistent criteria that has nonetheless since become genuinely generic via neo-noir and relabeling |
| **Melodrama's double meaning** | Hollywood's trade term meant action and suspense, a synonym for "thriller"; Film Studies redirected the word to the woman's film | A second demonstration that critical and industrial vocabularies can point at nearly opposite corpora under one word |
| **Studio-era output diversity** | Budget tiers, series, cycles, production trends, and star-formula combinations, not "genre," were the industry's real planning units | Even at the studio system's height, Variety shows dozens of ad hoc categories alongside canonic genre terms |
| **New Hollywood continuities** | Sequels and hybridity read as distinctively "New Hollywood," but 1940s films recycled scripts at roughly six times the 1970s rate | Undercuts both hybridity and sequelitis accounts of New Hollywood as historically naive comparisons |

## 5 · HEURISTICS & DECISION RULES

| Situation | Do this | Not this |
|---|---|---|
| Assessing whether a category is a "real" genre | Check whether the term appears in the industry's own inter-textual relay at the time | Assume a critic-coined label (noir) has industrial or historical priority |
| Comparing Hollywood eras for genre practice | Sample actual trade-press reviews and output for the period | Rely on canonic film lists or a genre's classic-era reputation |
| Explaining a film or cycle's cultural significance | Check exhibition patterns, market segmentation, and audience-survey data first | Assume box-office success equals audience ideological approval |
| Building a genre definition or model | Track industry label, marketing label, critical label, and audience expectation as separate variables | Assume the four converge on one stable definition |
| Judging whether an on-screen event is "unrealistic" | Ask whether it's being judged by the genre's own rules or by everyday cultural belief | Treat "unrealistic" as one undifferentiated failure mode |
| Theorizing why a genre changed | Treat each new film as adding to or revising the genre's horizon of expectation | Assume genres pass through fixed stages (experimental to classical to self-conscious) |
| Explaining why a cycle ended | Look for interorganizational and market-side causes (foreign sales, censorship, saturation fears) | Assume the cycle exhausted itself creatively or that audiences simply lost interest |

## 6 · INVARIANTS

1. Genre is always multi-dimensional: expectation, category, label, text, corpus, convention. Never reducible to any one of them.
2. Industrial, marketing, critical, and audience uses of a genre term can diverge sharply at the same historical moment.
3. Every genre balances generic verisimilitude against cultural verisimilitude. The balance is never fixed and never absent.
4. A critical category never used by the industry at the time (film noir) is a legitimate object of study only when explicit, consistent criteria are applied to a broad corpus, not one assembled backward from an already-canonized core.
5. Hollywood's actual output is always more diverse than any genre census suggests. Budget tier, series, cycle, production trend, star vehicle, and hybrid combination operate alongside, and often instead of, genre as the planning unit.
6. Generic repertoires always exceed any single film. Hybridity and overlap are the rule in every era of Hollywood, not a late or postmodern development.
7. A genre's history is as much the history of a term, its shifting boundaries, uses, and disputes, as it is of the films the term gets applied to.

## 7 · PITFALLS / MYTHS

- Treating a retrospective critical label (film noir) as though it were the industry's own contemporary category.
- Taking the western's unusually rigorous iconography as the general model for how genre works. Most genres (biopic, social problem film, romantic drama) lack a stable iconography altogether.
- Assuming box-office popularity proves ideological approval or cultural consensus. Westerns were mass-produced in the studio era despite being disliked by most of the viewing population.
- Assuming studio-era companies specialized in single genres for extended periods. Most studios diversified across many trends within any given year.
- Treating hybridity, allusion, and sequels as distinctively "New Hollywood." 1940s Hollywood recycled scripts at a far higher rate than the 1970s did, and studio-era films (*Tripoli*, 1950) were routinely multi-generic.
- Confusing melodrama the trade term (action and suspense, a synonym for thriller) with melodrama the Film Studies term (the woman's film). The two name nearly opposite corpora.
- Confusing "genre" as category (a type) with "genre" as corpus (an actual group of films), a slippage Neale flags even in Altman's own terminology.

## 8 · APPLICATION

- **Spine level:** `L7` (genre, medium, audience, market, the outermost ring). Neale's "genre as process" claim (Jauss/Cohen) describes how a *category* revises its boundaries over time, not what a story fundamentally is, so `L0` is not added here.
- **12-layer character stack:** none, `feeds: []`. Neale's unit of analysis is the industry-critic-audience triangle around a category, not a person. Nothing here maps onto CORE, WOUND, DRIVE, or FUNCTION without inventing a connection he never makes.
- **plot_systems:** n/a directly. Neale treats genre as a reader/industry contract layered over plot, not a plot-events source.
- **Setting:** contextual only. The SSOT's existing "genre-setting contract" touchpoint (Southern Gothic, western, space opera as genres that *are* setting promises) is a live thread, and Neale's iconography discussion is the craft-side evidence for why that touchpoint holds for some genres (western, gangster film) and not others (biopic, social problem film).

Where Dramatica treats genre as a thin appreciation-layer and Coyne's clover treats it as a reader-contract of conventions, Neale supplies the institutional machinery underneath both. A genre label is simultaneously an industry production category, a marketing claim, a critical construct, and an audience expectation, and these four routinely disagree about the same film. The shelf's existing holdings have treated audience and market mostly as reception-effects of a textual genre decision. Neale reverses that for two of the four: market conditions (budget tier, foreign-sales anxiety, censorship pressure) and industry categories can produce a genre label independent of any stable textual pattern, and critical or audience uses can then diverge from that origin without either side being wrong.

**For the genre system:**
- Adds a documented institutional mechanism, the inter-textual relay, for how a genre label actually forms and circulates, which the L7 ring currently names but does not model as a process with its own evidence trail.
- Feeds `market` hardest. Neale's industrial history (studio-era budget tiers, cycles, star-genre formulations, New Hollywood's blockbuster economics) is a direct source for what "market" should mean as a genre field: production risk-management, not just box-office reception.
- As fields in a genre model: `industry label` is the term production actually used at the time; `marketing label` is the looser term circulated in posters and reviews; `audience expectation` is the verisimilitude regime and generic knowledge a viewer brings. These, plus `critical label`, are independent variables, not synonyms for one "genre" slot.
- The rule the book insists on: a genre as the trade uses it is a planning convenience keyed to risk and cost; a genre as critics use it is a retrospective, criteria-dependent corpus. The two can name opposite sets of films under one word, as melodrama shows.
- OPEN candidate: the model has no field yet for a genre's contemporaneous industrial absence, a critically coherent category (like noir) with no trade-press name when its films were made. Neale's answer, study it only via explicit criteria over a full corpus, never canon-backward, is a rule the Command's library-keying should adopt whenever a BVX-LEARN genre tag is critic-coined rather than industry-attested.
