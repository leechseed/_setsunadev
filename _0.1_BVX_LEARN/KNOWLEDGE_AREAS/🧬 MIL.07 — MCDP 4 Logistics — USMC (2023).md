## CORE THESIS
Logistics is both the engine and the governor of every operation — it sets the outer boundary of what is strategically achievable, operationally feasible, and tactically possible — so the commander who builds the concept of operations on what the force can actually be moved and sustained to do, and who designs that sustainment to be responsive, simple, and survivable under attack, reaches the objective before the force culminates.

---

## INVARIANTS
*These are the unchanging principles — true regardless of context.*

1. **Logistics enables and limits.** It gives a force reach, freedom of action, and endurance, and it sets the parameters on all three. A force attacking loses strength as it goes; every plan has a culminating point, and a plan that ignores logistics capacity reaches it before the objective.

2. **Logistics is defined by results, not activities.** The measure is the supported force's ability to achieve advantage against a thinking opponent, not tonnage moved or reports filed. Right support, right place, right time — or it did not happen.

3. **Accrual is slower than expenditure.** It takes longer and more effort to build supplies, relationships, places, and platforms than to consume them. Responsiveness is therefore bought before the need, never at the moment of need.

4. **Every logistics node is a target.** Logistics is a critical requirement and therefore a focal point for attack. Disruption is not an anomaly to be handled if it occurs; it is the planning assumption. Survivability — avoidance, dispersion, hardening, mass, deception, regeneration — is designed in.

5. **Effectiveness and efficiency trade against each other.** Effectiveness is outcome-based regardless of cost; efficiency is minimum resources at acceptable risk. Every design (chain vs. web, push vs. pull, organic vs. outsourced) buys one with the other. The balance is a judgment, and it is the art.

6. **Demand discipline is a system property.** One unit's "little extra just in case" is trivial; repeated across the force it becomes the logistics snowball effect — cumulative false demand that undercuts prioritization and diverts support from real need. Discipline is trained in peace so it holds in crisis.

7. **The commander owns logistics; teams execute it on trust.** Responsibility cannot be delegated to the logistician. Unity of effort across many organizations with no single chain of command runs on trust — the conviction that those requesting and providing support are capable, reliable, and dependable.

8. **Logistics is science inside art.** The science calculates requirements from usage data and densities. The art anticipates how enemy action changes the calculation and keeps multiple paths open. Calculation without anticipation is a plan for a war that will not happen.

## HEURISTICS
*Rules of thumb for real-time decision-making.*

| Situation | Do This | Not This |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| Building a concept of operations | Base it on what you know you can do logistically; run feasibility before committing | Design the operation, then hand it to logistics to make work |
| Deciding whether to initiate | Judge attainability — sufficient supplies and support at acceptable risk — as an explicit, communicated commander's risk decision | Launch on enthusiasm and assume resupply catches up |
| Designing the distribution model | Build a web with multiple sourcing and transport options; accept the coordination and oversight cost | Optimize a single efficient chain that halts entirely when one link breaks |
| Choosing push vs. pull | Push routine, predictable consumption on a schedule; pull the unanticipated, rare, or specific | Pull everything (Kut, 1916) or push everything blind to actual consumption |
| Stating requirements | State actual need; validate against usage data and modeling | Pad "just in case" and feed the snowball |
| Allocating scarce support | Flow resources to the main effort per the commander's priorities; balance current operations against future needs | Spread evenly; serve whoever asks loudest |
| Writing the support concept | Simple, compelling concept built around the intent; branches and sequels for likely futures | Detailed, explicit instructions covering every eventuality |
| Timing preparation | Stage equipment, supplies, places, and relationships before they are required | Begin acquiring when the requirement materializes |
| Adopting new equipment or capability | Weigh operational benefit against the full support tail — training, maintenance, power, transport | Adopt the capability and discover its tail in the field |
| The system takes a hit | Execute rehearsed contingency plans: alternate methods, reserves, succession of command; regenerate | Treat disruption as an exception with no rehearsed alternate |
| Reading logistics information | Process and analyze into shared understanding of demand against supply over time | Report a snapshot of current stock and call it visibility |
| Choosing organic vs. outsourced | Blend by mission, area, and guidance; know which end of the spectrum each class of need sits on | Assume full self-sufficiency or full dependency |

## CORE CONCEPTS

**1. The Seven Logistics Principles**
Responsiveness (the keystone — right support, right place, right time), economy (minimum resources at acceptable risk), flexibility (adapt structure and plans to evolving situations), attainability (sufficient support to initiate at acceptable risk), sustainability (maintain support for long-term objectives), simplicity (clear, standardized processes and relationships; fewest required actions), survivability (prevail despite attack). They are integrated into plans and consulted during execution. Resilience underpins all seven.

**2. The Logistics Process and Functions**
Four actions support any force at any level: sourcing (obtain), distribution (move origin to need), sustainment (maintain and prolong until mission accomplished and redeployed), disposition (consume, return, dispose). Six functions organize the work: supply, maintenance, transportation, general engineering, health service support, services. Process says what must be done; functions organize it; a concept of support integrates every function against every warfighting function.

**3. Attainability and Logistics Visibility**
Attainability is the commander's judgment that enough exists to start or continue. It rests on visibility, split into demand visibility (friendly need relative to the concept of operations) and supply visibility (available units, equipment, infrastructure, supplies). Information is gathered, then processed and analyzed into a shared understanding of anticipated demand against expected ability to meet it. A data snapshot is not visibility.

**4. Chain vs. Web; Places, Platforms, Procedures**
A distribution model is places (nodes), platforms (lift), and procedures (how requirements are identified and delivered). A supply chain is efficient and breaks like a blocked road. A supply web has redundant sourcing and routes, resists blockage, costs more to maintain, and requires decision-making throughout. Expeditionary forces in contested space need webs.

**5. Push/Pull and Point/Unit/Blended Distribution**
Push forecasts requirements and delivers on schedule: predictable, slow to adjust. Pull responds to requests: efficient, dependent on communications and on the requester's discipline. Point distribution makes the supported unit come get it; unit distribution delivers to them; blended shares the movement. Routine favors push; emergent favors pull; most forces run both.

**6. The Culminating Point**
A force loses strength as it attacks and eventually can no longer sustain the attack. Logistics determines where that point falls. Plans that do not account for logistics capability and capacity culminate before the objective. Tactical logistics exists to keep the force from a premature culminating point.

**7. Engine, Steering Wheel, Gas Pedal**
Command and control establishes what will be done, how, and by whom; logistics sets what can and cannot be done. Logistics is the engine (how fast, how far, how long); C2 is the steering wheel and gas pedal (where, how quickly). C2 shapes logistics through priorities (order of need by mission), processes and procedures (stock levels, reorder thresholds, resupply means), and information systems — with alternatives rehearsed for when connectivity fails.

**8. Logistics Planning as a Sequence**
Understand the environment through a movement-and-sustainment lens (threats, physical network, resources) → develop feasible options that maximize the ways a requirement can be met → shape before violence (set the theater, forward-position, build relationships) → maintain visibility and adjust from unit feedback → make and communicate explicit risk decisions (avoid, accept, mitigate, transfer). Wargaming, modeling, rehearsal, and periodic deliberate assessment expose risk before the enemy does.

## KEY VOCABULARY

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Logistics | Planning and executing the movement and support of forces; the bridge between a nation's economy and its combat forces |
| Sustainment | The broader category: provision of logistics and personnel services to maintain and prolong operations until mission accomplishment and redeployment |
| Culminating point | The point at which a force can no longer sustain the attack and reverts to defense; set by logistics |
| Responsiveness | Right support, right place, right time; the keystone principle |
| Attainability | The commander's judgment that sufficient supplies and support exist to initiate operations at acceptable risk |
| Logistics visibility | Processed, analyzed awareness of demand (need vs. concept of operations) and supply (available resources) |
| Supply chain / supply web | Single efficient linear network that fails when blocked / redundant multi-path network that resists blockage at higher cost |
| Push / pull | Scheduled delivery from forecast / delivery on request from actual consumption |
| Logistics snowball effect | Cumulative false demand from padded requirements that undercuts prioritization |
| Hybrid logistics | Blending old, new, and emerging means; modular capability tailored to a distributed force |
| Setting the theater | Operational-level shaping: distribution network, force posture, forward provisioning before conflict |
| Concept of support | The integrated plan for how every logistics function supports every warfighting function |
| Distribution node | Any place where people or materiel are stored, reconfigured, or await movement; caches to sea bases |
| Self-sufficiency / dependency | Ends of the sustainment spectrum; forces blend them by mission and area |
| Warfighting functions | Command and control, intelligence, fires, maneuver, force protection, information, logistics |

## WHAT DOESN'T WORK
Designing the operation first and asking logistics to catch up, single-chain distribution optimized for efficiency, pure pull with severed communications, pure push blind to consumption, padded requirements ("a little extra just in case"), stock snapshots mistaken for visibility, detailed support instructions covering every eventuality, treating disruption as an exception, adopting capability without costing its support tail, mountains of steel at fixed forward locations in contested space, commercial-efficient methods assumed to survive combat, top-down directive culture that discourages initiative in the supply system, total dependence on connectivity with no rehearsed offline alternate, ignoring the force's own logistics signature.

## APPLICATION — THE OPERATION

- **Sustainment → runway.** For a solo creator-operator the classes of supply are cash, energy, attention, and tooling. MCDP 4's rule that the concept of operations is based "on what they know they can do logistically" means no project brief enters `project_flow` without a sustainment line: what it consumes, how long the runway holds, where the culminating point falls.
- **Attainability → the gate.** The venture `gates_sheet` is a series of attainability decisions. Doctrine defines the gate precisely: sufficient supplies and support capabilities exist to initiate at an acceptable level of risk. The operator makes it explicitly, names the risk posture (avoid / accept / mitigate / transfer), and communicates it — to the AI partner in the brief, to the record in STATE.md.
- **Logistics visibility → sit rep Block IV.** The PMCS table in BOLO.md is supply visibility (operator readiness, tool state, maintenance due); the BOLO watchlist is demand visibility (what upcoming work will consume). Block IV must present both as a processed picture, not a stock count — "shared understanding of the demands of anticipated operations and expected ability to meet those demands."
- **Responsiveness → stage before need.** Templates, caches, reference decks, and canonical docs are pre-positioned stores. Build them in the quiet phase. The AI partner's pre-session preparation is forward provisioning; accrual is slower than expenditure, so the sprint is never the time to build the tooling the sprint needs.
- **Chain vs. web → single points of failure.** One income source, one tool, one file that everything depends on is a supply chain. Doctrine says webs cost more and survive. Audit critical dependencies for a second path; accept the coordination cost.
- **Push/pull → PMCS cadence.** Scheduled maintenance (weekly reviews, backups, STATE.md updates at session end) is push. Emergent needs surfaced mid-session go to BOLO the same turn (pull). The snowball effect is the warning: padded "nice to have" items in BOLO are false demand that dilutes prioritization.
- **Priorities drive allocation → main effort.** "Aligning logistics resources to the main effort according to the commander's priorities makes the greatest contribution." The proword *main effort* is the allocation rule: runway goes there first, and everything else is economy of force.
- **Simplicity → the support concept in one sentence.** Concept-of-support plans are "built around simple, compelling concepts, with focus on the underlying intent," not instructions for every eventuality. The sustainment line in a brief is one sentence plus branches for the two most likely disruptions.
- **Survivability → assume the hit.** Doctrine plans for disruption as normal. For the operation: offline alternates for every connected tool, a rehearsed recovery path when the AI partner or the workspace is unavailable, and session notes to `_CACHE/` so the system regenerates from the last known state rather than from scratch.
- **Risk closes the publication → risk closes the brief.** "Every operational decision must include an understanding of logistics risk." ENDEX on any phase includes a periodic deliberate assessment: did we perform as anticipated, what changed in the environment, what is the runway now.
