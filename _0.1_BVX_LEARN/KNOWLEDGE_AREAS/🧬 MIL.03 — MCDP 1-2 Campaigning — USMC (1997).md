## CORE THESIS
Tactical success does not produce strategic success; the campaign — a series of related operations connected by time, space, and purpose — is the operational-level discipline that converts tactical actions into a strategic result, and its design begins with the military strategic aim, targets the enemy's centers of gravity through critical vulnerabilities, phases by events rather than schedule, plans termination from the start, and stays concise enough to adapt while it is being executed.

---

## INVARIANTS
*These are the unchanging principles — true regardless of context.*

1. **Tactical success does not guarantee strategic success.** Battles won that do not lead to the strategic objective are wasted effort. Combat is a means to a larger end, never the end.

2. **The levels of war form a continuum, and higher levels overpower lower ones.** Strategic incompetence squanders operational and tactical success far more often than tactical brilliance overcomes strategic disadvantage. Tactics that win the fight can defeat the strategy.

3. **Campaign design begins with the military strategic aim.** Focus on that aim is the single most important element of design; without knowing whether the strategy is annihilation or erosion, no coherent campaign is possible and instruments of power work at cross-purposes.

4. **Combat is costly; economy governs its use.** Fight when it is strategically advantageous or necessary — something to gain by fighting or lose by not — and refuse battle otherwise. Tactical advantage alone is not sufficient reason.

5. **Design and execution are continuous and interdependent.** There is no point at which design ceases and execution begins; results of execution modify the design mid-stride.

6. **Uncertainty grows with distance; detail must shrink with it.** The initial phase may be planned with certainty; later phases become increasingly general — an intent and a set of options.

7. **Logistics dictates what is operationally possible.** A campaign plan that cannot be sustained is not a plan.

8. **Every campaign ends, and the end must be designed from the start.** The acceptable end state, the transition to what follows, and reconstitution are campaign-design questions, not afterthoughts.

## HEURISTICS
*Rules of thumb for real-time decision-making.*

| Situation | Do This | Not This |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| Handed a strategic aim | Determine annihilation or erosion; identify centers of gravity and critical vulnerabilities; then build the campaign concept | Jump to operations and sequencing |
| Running an erosion campaign | Ask what the enemy leadership values and how to threaten it credibly | Inflict costs on the population or on units the leadership does not care about |
| Critical vulnerability is not open to attack | Design a progressive sequence of actions to expose or isolate it, creating the opportunity over time | Attack the center of gravity head-on |
| Phasing the campaign | Event-driven, natural subdivisions, each with its own intent; plan forward from now and backward from the end state and make the two mesh | Schedule-driven arbitrary chunks that produce a plodding, incremental approach and sacrifice tempo |
| Deciding how much detail to plan | Detail the initial phase; leave later phases conceptual — general intent plus contingencies and options | Equal detail across every phase |
| Writing the plan | Concise: end state, intent and concept, tentative phases and objectives, logistics concept, milestones as a measure of progress | Tactical detail for every phase; a schedule treated as immutable |
| Deciding whether to give battle | Fight when strategically advantageous or necessary, and accept battle even at tactical disadvantage if the result serves strategy | Fight because the engagement is winnable |
| A tactical result arrives — win, lose, or draw | Exploit whatever the result to strategic advantage | Reset to the original plan; treat a defeat as the end |
| Strategic aim is unclear, or imposed conditions prevent attaining it | Seek clarification; convey the impact of ambiguity; request relaxation of either the aims or the limitations | Proceed silently; cite the constraints as an excuse afterward |
| Resources are insufficient | Seek additional resources or request modification of the aim | Absorb the shortfall and fail on schedule |
| Need to generate tempo | Undertake multiple actions simultaneously; prepare sequels in advance; decentralize decisions inside a unifying intent; avoid unnecessary combat | Serial actions; centralized decisions; fighting every available engagement |
| Assessing the enemy | Understand their true character and capabilities; see your own actions from their viewpoint | A cardboard strawman, or an enemy ten feet tall; mirror-imaging |
| Approaching termination | Define the acceptable end state; check that it removes the seeds of further conflict; plan disengagement, force protection, transition, reconstitution | Stop abruptly; "declare victory" with no transition plan |
| Higher authority over-prescribes your concept | The concept "should be born in the mind" of the commander who directs it — push back | Become a mere executor of tactical tasks |

## CORE CONCEPTS (8)
*The essential theories, solutions, and mechanics.*

**1. The Operational Level and the Campaign**
The operational level is the link between strategy and tactics: its aim is to get strategically meaningful results from tactical efforts by deciding when, where, for what purpose, and under what conditions to give or refuse battle. The campaign is its principal tool — related operations connected by time, space, and purpose toward a strategic objective. The level is not defined by echelon; if a tactical action directly achieves a strategic objective, it is operational, however small.

**2. How Strategy Guides Operations**
Strategy shapes the campaign in three ways: it establishes the military strategic aim, it allocates resources (tangible and intangible, including political support), and it imposes restraints (prohibitions) and constraints (obligations). The relationship runs both ways — strategy must adapt to operational events, and the commander must communicate risk, seek clarification of ambiguous aims, and request relief when conditions prevent success. No commander may use imposed conditions as an excuse for failure.

**3. Campaigning Under Annihilation or Erosion**
Under annihilation the focus is the enemy's armed forces, the military is the main effort, other instruments support it, and victory is easily measured. Under erosion there are many more variables: the main effort may be diplomatic, economic, or informational; the target is whatever the leadership values; victory is flexibly defined and may be implicit. Political and military aims need not match — a limited political aim can be served by annihilation within the theater.

**4. Strategic vs. Operational Centers of Gravity; Critical Vulnerabilities**
A strategic center of gravity is an objective whose loss profoundly affects the enemy leadership's will or ability to continue. An operational center of gravity is normally the concentration of enemy military power most dangerous to us or standing between us and the mission. The two may coincide or differ. A critical vulnerability must undermine a key strength and be within our means to attack; often it must be created by a progressive sequence of actions. Expect the enemy to attack ours.

**5. The Campaign Concept**
A single unifying idea expressed in clear, concise, conceptual language: what we plan to accomplish and how, with intent explicitly stated and a general sense of when, where, and under what conditions battle will be given or refused. It should be bold, creative, free of discernible patterns, rich in options so the enemy cannot read intentions, as simple as the situation allows, and built for speed. A simple superior idea has often been the basis of success.

**6. Phasing, Branches, and Sequels**
Phases organize dispersed activity into natural, event-driven subdivisions, each with an intermediate goal and its own intent. Each phase contains decision points with branches (options at the point) and sequels (follow-ups to each outcome). Planning runs forward from the present and backward from the end state; the two sets must mesh. Logistical culmination and time-oriented resource availability must be reconciled with event-driven phasing — logistics may dictate the purpose and sequence of phases.

**7. Conceptual, Functional, and Detailed Planning**
Conceptual planning produces the broad scheme; functional planning designs the supporting concepts for the six warfighting functions (command and control, maneuver, fires, intelligence, logistics, force protection) and tests feasibility; detailed planning coordinates specifics without inhibiting flexibility. The process runs both ways — the concept must adapt to functional realities. No amount of detail substitutes for a concept.

**8. Conflict Termination and the Campaign Plan**
Define the acceptable political and military end state early and update it; ask whether it ends the fundamental problem or leaves the seeds of further conflict, and whether the termination permits unilateral resumption. Plan disengagement, transition to post-conflict operations, and reconstitution (reorganization or regeneration). The campaign plan itself is the concise statement of design: end state, intent and concept, tentative phases and objectives, key functional concepts (above all logistics), milestones as a measure of progress but never an immutable schedule, with the final decisive action clearly envisioned.

## WHAT DOESN'T WORK
Accumulating tactical victories without an operational design, defining the operational level by echelon, schedule-driven phasing, arbitrary incremental chunks that sacrifice tempo, equal detail across every phase, long campaign plans, fighting because it is tactically advantageous, viewing any tactical action in isolation, higher authority over-prescribing the concept of operations, using imposed constraints as an excuse, the strawman enemy and the ten-foot-tall enemy, mirror-imaging, plans without a logistics concept, ignoring conflict termination until the end, abrupt disengagement without transition, the operational commander absorbed in tactics, unnecessary combat that saps tempo, tactics that alienate the population the strategy needs, methodical deliberate battle against a faster opponent.

## APPLICATION — THE OPERATION

- **Campaign = project; operation = session; engagement = task.** Doctrine: without an operational design that synthesizes tactical results, what passes for operations is the accumulation of tactical victories. Translation: the operational level is the discipline missing from the recurring stall pattern — sessions accumulate finished designs (tactical victories) with no campaign connecting them to the strategic aim. BOLO 25's standard flow is the campaign plan template, and its first line is the aim.

- **Event-driven phasing, planned forward and backward → the gate structure.** Doctrine: phases are natural subdivisions ending on events, each with its own intent; plan from now toward the end state and from the end state back to now, and mesh the two. Translation: a phase ends when a decision is made or an artifact ships, never on a date. The backward pass from the end state exposes the phase that has no event closing it — that is where the stall lives.

- **Near-detailed, far-conceptual → planning cadence.** Doctrine: plan the initial phase with certainty; later phases become increasingly general, "perhaps consisting of no more than a general intent and several contingencies and options." Translation: detail only the current phase in the plan; carry intent and options for the rest. This stops the pattern of designing every phase in full, then discarding the lot when phase one changes the situation.

- **Conflict termination and end state → definition of done, written first.** Doctrine: define the acceptable end state from the earliest moment; check it removes the seeds of further conflict; plan disengagement, transition, and reconstitution. Translation: before the last push, the plan states what justifies stopping, what hand-off or archive follows, and how the operator reconstitutes — Oscar Mike park-and-sweep and the `_CACHE/` session note are the reconstitution and transition steps.

- **Logistics concept → the resource line in every plan.** Doctrine: a plan that cannot be logistically supported is fanciful wishes; at the operational level logistics dictates what is possible, and failure to anticipate costs weeks, not hours. Translation: every project plan carries a line for time, attention, tokens, and money through to the end state, reconciled against the event-driven phases. The gate checks logistical culmination before it checks progress.

- **Aims, resources, constraints → the sit rep's blocker block.** Doctrine: when imposed conditions prevent the aim, the commander must request relaxation of the aims or the limitations — and may never use those conditions as an excuse. Translation: the sit rep surfaces a blocker as a formal request — relax the aim, or relax the constraint, or add resources — rather than working around it silently. Two-way flow: how copy pushes intent down to the AI partner; the sit rep pushes ground truth back up. "The fingers have to know what the brain is feeling for, and the brain has to know what the fingers are actually touching."

- **The four tempo generators → session design; boresight as the tempo check.** Doctrine: tempo comes from simultaneous actions, prepared sequels, decentralized decisions inside a unifying intent, and avoiding unnecessary combat. Translation: parallel subagents, the next step already queued before the current one lands, mission-type orders to the partner, and refusal to do work that does not move the aim. "Boresight" is the check on the fourth generator — is this engagement strategically necessary, or merely winnable?

- **Main effort location depends on strategy → know when the build is supporting.** Doctrine: under annihilation the armed forces are the main effort; under erosion the main effort may be diplomatic, economic, or informational and the military supports. Translation: in a build-it project the artifact is main effort and writing supports it; in a change-the-process project, the writing, comms, and cadence are main effort and the build supports them. Naming which prevents the AI partner from optimizing the supporting effort.
