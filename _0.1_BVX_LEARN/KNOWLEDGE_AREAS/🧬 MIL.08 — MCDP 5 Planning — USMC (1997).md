## CORE THESIS
Planning is a continuous learning process, subordinate to execution, whose object is not to predict or control the future but to let the organization decide and act effectively inside uncertainty — so a plan is an interim product that is always being revised, the commander is the single most important factor in it, and the measure of any plan is never whether it was executed as written but whether it provided the basis for faster and more effective action than the opponent's.

---

## INVARIANTS
*These are the unchanging principles — true regardless of context.*

1. **Planning is a process, not a procedure, and it does not stop.** It is a dynamic system of iterative, interdependent activities, too situation-dependent to be a routine sequence of steps. Planning for an action stops only with execution, and adaptation continues after that. Much of planning is replanning.

2. **A plan is an interim product.** It is the best configuration of actions in time and space given what is known at the moment, always subject to revision as information and understanding change. A static plan is of no value to an adaptive organization in a fluid situation.

3. **Uncertainty and time define the problem.** Uncertainty grows with the planning horizon and with the rate of change. All planning takes time, and the situation changes while you plan. The object of planning is not to eliminate uncertainty but to decide and act effectively in the midst of it.

4. **The measure of a plan is action, not accuracy.** How accurately a plan forecasts the future is not a measure of its effectiveness. The measure is whether it provides the basis for effective action and adaptation. Few plans are executed as designed; they are not meant to be.

5. **The commander is the single most important factor.** Planning is a fundamental responsibility of command. The commander disciplines the process (time, horizon, detail, mode, simplicity) and the product (relevant to the moment, suitable to the subordinate), and personally drafts the intent.

6. **Detail trades against flexibility.** The less certain the situation, the less detail in which you can plan. Anxiety under uncertainty drives more detail, which drives more anxiety, which produces a plan that does not survive friction and constricts action. Plans contain only as much detail as subordinates need.

7. **Synthesis cannot be proceduralized.** Establishing aims, formulating intent, and devising a course of action are creative judgments. Analysis structures and evaluates; it cannot make the creative decisions. Attempts to reduce synthesis to procedure restrict the creativity the process depends on.

8. **Planning must be done by the organization, not for it.** The main benefit of planning is the learning and shared understanding produced by engaging in it. Those who execute understand a plan only if they helped build it. Planners and executors are ideally the same people.

9. **Every plan carries the means of its own change.** A plan's control process — coordination measures, feedback mechanisms, decision points — anticipates the need for change and the decisions to be made in execution. Plans that omit the signals of success or dysfunction are incomplete.

## HEURISTICS
*Rules of thumb for real-time decision-making.*

| Situation | Do This | Not This |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| Entering a new, unfamiliar situation | Orientation planning: assess, learn, identify feasible objectives, design flexibility, commit only limited resources | Commit to a detailed plan before understanding the situation |
| Uncertainty is high | Broad, loosely coupled, modular plan; near commitment horizon; more time in contingency and orientation modes | A detailed, integrated plan built to cover every possibility |
| Deciding how much detail | Only as much as gives subordinates necessary guidance while allowing maximum freedom of action | Planning one echelon below your own level of detail |
| Setting goals | General but not vague — few defining criteria, usable measures of success; act on unclear goals rather than wait | Vague goals with no criteria; or paralysis waiting for perfectly clear goals |
| Time is short | Rapid planning from current conditions; accept a workable plan over an optimal one; issue warning orders and partial instructions as they are ready | Compress the deliberate process; withhold everything until the complete order is done |
| Time is ample and speed buys nothing | Plan deliberately; there is no excuse not to | An ill-conceived hasty plan because "we move fast here" |
| About to add a coordination or control measure | Ask: do these actions need to be coupled? Prefer directing local coordination over designing it | Couple unrelated elements to feel in control |
| Evaluating the plan | Embed evaluation throughout development; scrutinize on merits; surface decision points, contingencies, feedback signals; bring in an outsider for a post-hoc critique | A formal critique step after the plan is "finished," which the team cannot honestly perform on its own work |
| Plan meets reality and departs | Adapt from the plan as the point of departure; stay inside the intent; inform others of the departure | Scrap and rebuild from scratch; or force events back to the plan |
| Fixed deadline | Reverse-plan from the end state to allocate preparation time; forward-plan the next feasible step | Forward-only planning that discovers the deadline is unreachable |
| Writing the directive | Minimum instruction; intent brief enough to hold in mind under stress; oral between principals when possible; format serves content | Elaborate detail, restated SOP, many contingencies, slavish format |
| Adopting a planning routine | Use it for repetitive tasks with little variation; protect the creative parts from it | Reduce intuition and creativity to steps; let the routine rigidify |
| Deciding how many contingencies | Branches and sequels for a few likely ones; preserve the ability to pursue unplanned options | So many contingencies that none is adequately prepared |
| Decision planning vs. execution planning under time pressure | Ask whether another course of action is worth the time it takes from preparing to execute the one you have | Generate options past the point where execution suffers |

## CORE CONCEPTS

**1. The Planning Process**
Assess the situation (orient, identify centers of gravity and critical vulnerabilities, project developments, define the problem) → establish goals, objectives, and the underlying intent (the commander's greatest contribution; resolve competing goals) → conceptualize a course of action (salient features and their interactions) → detail the course of action (execution planning, often deferred or passed down) → evaluate (embedded throughout, not a separate gate) → issue a directive → cycle again. Phases run in parallel, feed back to one another, and are distributed across echelons. It is a schematic, not a sequence.

**2. The Planning Hierarchy: Conceptual / Functional / Detailed**
Conceptual planning establishes aims, objectives, intent, and broad concepts; it is creative synthesis supported by analysis — the art of war — and the commander personally directs it. Detailed planning translates the concept into a practicable plan: scheduling, coordination, technical specifics — the science of war, analytical, decomposing concept into tasks, working to objectives assigned from above. Functional planning sits between: supporting plans for discrete functions (maneuver, fires, logistics, intelligence). Planning progresses general to specific, but functional and detailed constraints feed back and reshape the concept.

**3. Planning Modes and Horizons: Commitment / Contingency / Orientation**
Uncertainty sets the mode. Commitment planning: confident forecast, commit to one plan and its resources; most preparation, least flexibility. Contingency planning: several plausible futures, plan for each without committing; balances preparation and flexibility. Orientation planning: uncertainty too high to commit or even branch; assess, design responsiveness, cultivate conditions; most flexibility, least preparation. The modes are also the sequence (orient → contingency → commit as execution nears) and correspond to horizons — commitment horizon near, contingency beyond it, orientation beyond that — planned simultaneously for different phases.

**4. Analysis and Synthesis**
Analysis decomposes a whole into parts and handles each: processing information, testing feasibility, breaking a concept into tasks. Synthesis integrates elements into a cohesive whole through judgment and creativity. The key planning judgments — aims, intent, course of action — are synthesis. Analysis precedes (structuring the elements) and follows (scrutinizing and detailing the product) but cannot replace it.

**5. Decision Planning and Execution Planning**
Planning before the decision develops the estimate and generates, evaluates, and modifies courses of action — largely conceptual. Planning after the decision translates the approved course into an executable directive — largely functional and detailed. Execution planning at one echelon becomes decision planning at the next down. Planning is anticipatory decision-making: a plan is a system of interrelated tentative decisions, and decisions are plans put into effect.

**6. Components of a Plan**
Every plan has a desired outcome with the intent behind it (the basis for everything else; general is good, vague is not); actions arranged in time and space, usually as tasks to subordinates; resources — type, amount, allocation, and provision; and a control process — coordination measures plus feedback mechanisms and decision points that identify the need for change and provide for making it. The last is the most neglected.

**7. Coupling, Modularity, and Simplicity**
Coupling is how closely actions in a plan interact. Tightly coupled (integrated, synchronized) plans are efficient and near-optimal under stable conditions but brittle: one disruption reverberates through the whole, and they are hard to repair. Loosely coupled (modular, asynchronous) plans tolerate friction, allow latitude, and can be repaired part by part. Most tactical situations favor loose coupling. Simplicity comes from fewer actions, grouping related actions under one subordinate, planning broad but not vague, and embodying the plan in one compelling concept. Simple is not simplistic; the plan should be as simple as the situation allows.

**8. The Five Functions of Planning**
Plans direct and coordinate action; planning builds shared situational awareness; planning generates expectations against which failure is recognized; planning supports initiative by giving a point of departure for adaptation; planning shapes the thinking of planners. The functions conflict: over-weighting direction breeds micromanagement; many contingencies add flexibility at the cost of initiative and shared awareness. The commander decides which function the situation demands.

**9. Planning in Time and Planning as Shaping**
Forward planning asks "where can we get to next?" and works from present feasibility; reverse planning asks "where do we eventually want to get?" and works back from an end state — possible only when the goal is stable, and used together. Deliberate planning runs on assumptions well ahead of execution; rapid planning runs on current conditions and revises it. Shaping is taking action now for future advantage while preserving multiple options and avoiding irreversible commitment. The end state is an aiming point, not a fixed destination.

**10. Planning Misused**
Four pitfalls, one cause — refusal to accept uncertainty. Forecasting and dictating too far into the future (plans are overcome by events sooner than planners expect; studies show plans last about 65 percent of their intended life). Planning in more detail than conditions warrant. Planning as scripting that prescribes friendly and enemy actions and turns subordinates toward meeting the plan rather than deciding effectively. Institutionalized rigidity — routines that harden and reduce judgment to procedure, "one of the gravest pathological characteristics of planning and of plans."

## KEY VOCABULARY

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Planning | The art and science of envisioning a desired future and laying out effective ways of bringing it about; thinking before doing |
| Plan | Any product of planning; an interim configuration of actions in time and space, always subject to revision; the basis for action, cooperation, and adaptation |
| Process vs. procedure | A dynamic system of related activities vs. a prescribed sequence of steps; planning is the former |
| Conceptual / functional / detailed planning | Aims, intent, and broad concepts (art) / supporting plans by function / specifics of execution (science) |
| Commitment / contingency / orientation planning | Modes by uncertainty: commit resources to one plan / prepare several without committing / assess and design flexibility only |
| Planning horizon | How far into the future planning tries to shape events; commitment, contingency, and orientation horizons run concurrently |
| Decision planning / execution planning | Planning before the decision (generate and evaluate courses) / after it (translate into an executable directive) |
| Deliberate / rapid planning | Well in advance on assumptions / time-sensitive on current conditions; rapid revises deliberate |
| Forward / reverse planning | From present toward next feasible step / from end state back toward present |
| Tight / loose coupling | Close, time-dependent interaction between plan elements / independent, asynchronous elements; integrated vs. modular |
| Control process | The plan component that supervises execution: coordination measures, feedback mechanisms, decision points |
| Decision point | A designed point in execution requiring a decision about how to proceed |
| Branches and sequels | Planned alternatives within a phase / planned follow-on phases for likely contingencies |
| Directive | Any communication establishing policy or ordering action; plans (conditional) and orders (obligation to execute) |
| Warning order / fragmentary order | Preliminary notice to begin preparation / abbreviated order that avoids restating the base order |
| Commander's intent | The purpose behind the mission; brief, unambiguous, compelling; drafted by the commander personally |
| Anticipatory adaptation / improvisation | Adapting in advance through planning / adapting in real time from the plan as point of departure |
| General vs. vague goal | Few criteria with broad latitude, good / no usable criteria for success, bad |
| Simple vs. simplistic | As simple as the situation allows / made simple by ignoring the situation's real complications |

## WHAT DOESN'T WORK
Treating planning as a procedure, forecasting and dictating too far into the future, planning in more detail than the situation warrants, adding detail to relieve the anxiety of uncertainty, scripting friendly and enemy actions, judging a plan by forecast accuracy or by effort spent, institutionalized routines that reduce intuition to steps and harden over time, tightly coupling actions that do not need coupling, plans without a control process or feedback signals, vague goals with no success criteria, waiting for clear goals before acting, planning for every eventuality until none is prepared, an isolated planning staff that plans for executors instead of with them, a commander who delegates planning and misses the learning, directives that repeat SOP and restate higher's order, slavish order formats, withholding partial instructions until the whole order is complete, treating the plan as a solution to implement rather than a point of departure, rebuilding from scratch when the plan meets reality.

## APPLICATION — THE OPERATION

- **Planning hierarchy → the phases of `project_flow`.** Conceptual planning is the brief: end state, intent, one compelling concept — drafted by the operator personally, because that is the commander's job and cannot be delegated to the AI partner. Functional planning is the workstream layer (content, tooling, runway, distribution). Detailed planning is the task list, and it belongs to whoever executes — the AI partner drafts it, the operator approves. Flow runs general to specific, but functional constraints (runway, time) feed back and reshape the concept.
- **Planning modes → the horizon structure and the gates.** Commit resources only inside the commitment horizon where forecasts hold. Later phases sit as contingencies (two or three branches, not detailed) and beyond that as orientation (assess, keep options open). Each venture gate on the `gates_sheet` is the moment a phase moves from contingency to commitment — and doctrine says the horizon should be nearer the more uncertain the situation is.
- **"Much of planning is actually replanning" → STATE.md is the living plan.** A plan is an interim product. STATE.md updated at session end is the doctrinal replan; the `_CACHE/` note is the record of what changed. A plan that is not updated after contact is a static document, "of no value to an adaptive organization."
- **Components of a plan → the brief template.** Every brief carries four things or it is not a plan: desired outcome with intent, actions, resources, and a control process. The control process is the piece operators skip and doctrine says is most neglected — for the operation it is the sit rep cadence, the decision points, and the BOLO watchlist that tells you the plan is failing before the deadline does.
- **The recurring stall pattern is Planning Misused, pitfalls two and four.** Finished designs blocked on one unanswered decision, then rebuilt from scratch, is detail-under-anxiety followed by solution-by-engineering. Doctrine's counter: "Unclear goals are generally better than no goals, and waiting for clear goals before acting can paralyze an organization." Make the decision at acceptable risk and adapt from the plan as the point of departure.
- **Tight vs. loose coupling → modular workstreams.** Before wiring two workstreams together, ask "do these actions need to be coupled?" Loosely coupled projects can be parked (Oscar Mike) and resumed without breaking the rest. Tightly coupled ones fail together and repair slowly.
- **Commander's intent → the line the AI partner acts on when the task is overcome by events.** Slim: the intent "should be worded by the commander himself." The operator writes one brief, unambiguous, compelling sentence the partner can hold in mind; everything else in the brief is subordinate to it. Mission-type orders to the partner state task and intent, not method.
- **Plans and orders → how copy and boresight.** Directives carry the minimum instruction, are issued orally between principals where possible, and go out as warning orders and partial instructions early. *How copy* is the readback that closes the directive and confirms the shared image; *boresight* is the shared situational awareness the planning process exists to produce before execution — "the common understanding provides a basis for unity of effort."
- **Participatory planning → the AI partner cannot plan for the operator.** "Planning cannot be done to or for an organization; it must be done by it." A plan the partner generates and the operator consumes fails the doctrine on its face; the operator's understanding comes from building it. The partner facilitates: guidance, context, resources, options, analysis — and the operator synthesizes.
- **Planning in time → sit rep cadence and ENDEX.** All planning is time-sensitive: plan deliberately when time allows, rapidly when it does not, and know when to stop planning and execute. The session is the planning-execution cycle; ENDEX is the point at which the latest version of the plan becomes the basis for action and the measure is applied — did it produce effective action, faster than the alternative.
