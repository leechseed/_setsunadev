# Markdown marking: one ink per hand

## Two people mark one document

A writer and a reader both mark the same document. The writer marks structure and the first instance of each technical term. The reader marks the terms the argument rests on.

## Shared ink hides who marked what

Before this standard, both used bold. One one-sheet carried 26 template bolds and 63 reader bolds. Neither a person nor a script could tell them apart.

The fix gives each party its own ink. Ink identifies the hand. A reader never bolds, and a writer never highlights.

## Four marks carry four meanings

Bold is the writer's structure and first-instance term. Highlight is the reader's capture of a load-bearing term. Italic is the writer's caption or aside. A wikilink is a term that has earned its own note.

A highlight names a load-bearing term: one the argument cannot stand without in this source. It does not name an unknown word. The reader's job is to capture, not to define.

## Meaning comes later, at harvest

The reader does not stop to define. A script reads the marks later and writes the sense into a vocabulary page. It writes the meaning the term has in that source. This keeps reading fast and keeps definitions in one place.

## Marks must survive the editor

Every mark survives the Command's editors. Prettier rewrites `*x*` to `_x_` and pads tables. It leaves `**`, `==` and `[[ ]]` alone. So the writer types italic with underscores from the start.

## Marks have a life cycle

A highlight leaves in exactly two cases. The term is promoted to a wikilink, or review finds it is not load-bearing. Promotion keeps the register rows, which now point at a note.

## The long read

The standard gives two parties two inks and gives one command the job of harvesting. The end-state renderer is the MCDP browser (BOLO 33). Until then the source view shows highlights as double equals signs.
