# A Comprehensive Glossary of D/s, Kink, Somatic Sexology, and Clinical Psychology Terminology

## TL;DR

- This glossary defines ~110 terms across four interlocking domains — Dominance/submission (with focus on Daddy Dom/little sub dynamics), broader BDSM, somatic sexology, and clinical psychology — drawing on peer-reviewed research (Sagarin, Ambler, Williams), DSM-5-TR criteria, and community-authoritative authors (Easton & Hardy, Kaldera, Califia, Midori, Harrington), as well as somatic/clinical figures (Porges, Levine, van der Kolk, Siegel, Schwartz, Martin, Jaiya).
- The single most important conceptual move in this field is the **DSM-5/DSM-5-TR distinction between paraphilias (atypical sexual interests) and paraphilic disorders (those causing distress, impairment, or non-consensual harm)** — which means consensual BDSM/kink is *not*, in itself, a mental disorder.
- For Daddy Dom/little sub practitioners, the most consequential distinctions to internalize are: (a) **age regression (involuntary, clinical, non-sexual coping) vs. ageplay (consensual adult roleplay, may be sexual)**; (b) **CGL/DDlg (a power-exchange dynamic) vs. ABDL/paraphilic infantilism (an arousal pattern centered on infancy/diapers)**; and (c) **discipline vs. punishment vs. funishment**, each of which serves a distinct function within a caregiver/little dynamic.

---

## Key Findings

1. **Consent frameworks have evolved hierarchically**: SSC (1980s, David Stein for GMSMA) → RACK (Gary Switch) → PRICK (~2002) → 4Cs (Williams, Thomas, Prior & Christensen, 2014, *Electronic Journal of Human Sexuality*) → FRIES (Planned Parenthood) → EPP (NCSF). Each successor critiques the prior framework's blind spots; none is universally adopted.
2. **The "regression" question is critical**: clinical/IFS-style regression and kink ageplay are categorically distinct, and conflating them causes real harm to age-regressors who use the practice as trauma coping.
3. **Subspace and topspace are empirically measurable altered states.** Ambler, Lee, Klement, Loewald, Comber, Hanson, Cutler, Cutler & Sagarin (2017), "Consensual BDSM facilitates role-specific altered states of consciousness: A preliminary study," *Psychology of Consciousness: Theory, Research, and Practice*, 4, 75–91 — found topspace correlates with Csikszentmihalyi's flow state and subspace with Dietrich's transient hypofrontality.
4. **Polyvagal theory remains influential but contested**: Porges' three-state hierarchy (ventral vagal, sympathetic, dorsal vagal) is foundational to trauma-informed kink work, though anatomical critiques exist (Grossman & Taylor; Neuhuber & Berthoud).
5. **Kink-aware professionals matter**: the NCSF Kink and Polyamory Aware Professionals (KAP) directory, maintained since 2006, is the field standard for finding clinicians who will not pathologize BDSM identity.

## Details

The glossary is organized by category for learning. Within categories, related terms are clustered.

### A. D/s and Power Exchange Terminology

**Dominance (D-type, Dom/Domme).** The role of the partner who consensually exercises authority, control, or direction within an erotic or relational power exchange. Dominance is a *function within a negotiated dynamic*, not a personality trait or a license to coerce; community-authoritative sources (Easton & Hardy, *The New Topping Book*, 2003) frame the dominant as bearing primary responsibility for the bottom's welfare during a scene.

**Submission (s-type, sub).** The role of the partner who consensually yields authority, autonomy, or direction. Submission is voluntary and revocable; Easton & Hardy (*The New Bottoming Book*, 2001) emphasize that "the bottom has all the power" because consent is theirs to withdraw.

**Switch.** A person who fluidly occupies either dominant or submissive roles, often depending on partner, mood, or activity. Switches sometimes face stigma from both sides of the community for being seen as "uncommitted" — a misconception challenged by community educators including Mollena Williams-Haas.

**Top / Bottom.** The person performing an action (top) and the person receiving it (bottom). Top/bottom describes *what one does* in a scene; D/s describes *who has authority*. A service top, for example, may perform whipping while remaining functionally subordinate to the bottom's desires.

**Vers (versatile).** Most often used in gay male sexual contexts, but in BDSM borrowed to indicate flexibility between top/bottom roles, similar to switch.

**Power Exchange (PE).** The negotiated, consensual transfer of authority or decision-making from one partner to another, scoped to specific activities, times, or domains.

**Total Power Exchange (TPE).** A relationship structure in which one partner cedes virtually all decision-making authority to the other across most or all life domains (finances, schedule, attire, sexuality). Often associated with Master/slave (M/s) dynamics; Raven Kaldera and Joshua Tenpenny's *Real Service* (Alfred Press, 2014) is a canonical text.

**Master/slave (M/s).** A deeper, more totalizing form of power exchange than session-based D/s, typically involving 24/7 commitment and internalized ownership. Kaldera & Tenpenny describe it as a relational identity rather than a kink activity; capitalization conventions ("Master" capitalized, "slave" lowercase) signal hierarchy.

**24/7 dynamic.** A power exchange that operates continuously rather than only during scenes; can be high or low protocol.

**Scene-only / Bedroom-only.** A dynamic confined to negotiated scenes or sexual encounters, with vanilla equality at all other times.

**Lifestyle (vs. "kinky").** Community shorthand for those whose BDSM identity is an ongoing relational orientation rather than a recreational activity.

**Daddy Dom (DD).** A masculine-coded caregiver dominant who combines authority with nurturing, protection, and structure for a "little" partner. The role explicitly does not require biological paternity or age difference and is not pedophilic — the "little" is an adult.

**Mommy Domme.** The feminine-coded equivalent of Daddy Dom.

**Caregiver (CG).** The gender-neutral umbrella term for the dominant/nurturing partner in CGL dynamics.

**Little.** An adult who, within a consensual dynamic, accesses a younger-feeling psychological state ("little space") and engages with caregiver-style nurturance. Littles vary widely in expressed "age" — from infant/toddler to school-age — and not all littles age-regress; some simply identify with childlike playfulness within a nurturing power exchange.

**Middle.** A self-described identity between "little" and adult — often a teenage or pre-teen headspace; sometimes used by those who don't fully regress.

**Big.** Adult headspace; the state to which a little "returns" after little space.

**DDlg (Daddy Dom / little girl).** The most widely known configuration of the CGL umbrella. DDlg explicitly involves consenting adults; mainstream confusion with pedophilia is a persistent and unfounded misreading (KYNK 101; per forensic psychologist Anil Aggrawal as cited in Wikipedia's "Ageplay" entry, ageplay is unrelated to pedophilia).

**DDlb (Daddy Dom / little boy), MDlg (Mommy Domme / little girl), MDlb (Mommy Domme / little boy).** Gendered variants within CGL.

**CGL (Caregiver/little).** The gender-neutral umbrella covering DDlg, DDlb, MDlg, MDlb, and non-binary configurations. CGL distinguishes itself from ABDL (see below) by centering the *power-exchange caregiving relationship* rather than infantile regression or diaper-specific arousal.

**CGLre (Caregiver / little regressor).** A non-kink, explicitly non-sexual subset of CGL terminology used by age-regressors and their caregivers; community consensus is that CGLre is "not a kink" and should not be cross-tagged with sexual ageplay (Fanlore "Age Regression").

**Service submission.** A submission orientation centered on tasks of care, devotion, and competent performance for the Dominant; canonical text is Kaldera & Tenpenny's *Real Service* (2014): "Real Service is a handbook for service-oriented submissives and the people they serve."

**Service top.** A top who performs activities primarily because the bottom desires them, not because the top inherently craves topping. Patrick Califia discusses the dynamic in *Sensuous Magic* (1993); the concept is closely associated with dyke leather culture.

**Service bottom.** The inverse — a bottom who receives activities primarily to satisfy the top's desire to give them.

**Brat.** A submissive whose submission is expressed through playful, consensual disobedience, teasing, and rule-pushing. Bratting is "fundamentally about the consensual and playful violation of a rules-based order."

**Brat tamer.** A dominant who specifically enjoys and skillfully handles bratty submissives — neither crushing the brat nor letting them run the dynamic.

**Primal.** A style of play emphasizing instinctual, animalistic behavior — biting, scratching, growling, chasing, wrestling — usually without formal protocol. Lee Harrington and Mollena Williams discuss primal dynamics in *Playing Well With Others* (2012).

**Owner / property.** Common honorifics in M/s dynamics; framing varies by individual relationship and is not synonymous with dehumanization in the abusive sense.

**Collaring.** The act of placing a collar on a submissive as a marker of dynamic, commitment, or ownership. The progression of collars is community-codified out of Old Guard leather tradition (Larry Townsend, *The Leatherman's Handbook*, 1972) and formalized in writings by Joseph Bean and Guy Baldwin, with later articulation in Kaldera & Tenpenny's M/s work:

- **Collar of Consideration.** A revocable early-stage collar indicating the submissive is being considered for a deeper dynamic, analogous to engagement courtship.
- **Training Collar.** Indicates the submissive is in active formal training under the Dominant.
- **Formal / Ownership Collar.** "The equivalent of giving/receiving a wedding ring" — a long-term commitment marker.

**Protocol.** The codified rules of behavior, speech, and posture within a D/s relationship. **High protocol** is reserved for ceremonies, formal events, or designated time, with strict honorifics, limited speech, and prescribed posture; **low protocol** governs ordinary daily life with looser etiquette. Discussed in Robert Rubel's *Protocols: A Variety of Foundational Concepts* and Guy Baldwin's *SlaveCraft*.

### B. Kink/BDSM General Terminology

**BDSM.** An acronym layering three overlapping practices: Bondage/Discipline (B/D), Dominance/submission (D/s), and Sadism/Masochism (S/M). Coined in the 1990s as community shorthand.

**Kink.** Umbrella term for non-normative ("non-vanilla") consensual sexual or relational practices.

**Fetish vs. kink.** Clinically, a *fetish* historically referred to arousal contingent on a specific object or body part (DSM lineage); colloquially, the term is used more loosely. *Kink* is broader, encompassing power dynamics, sensation, role-play, and fetish. In DSM-5-TR, **fetishistic disorder** requires arousal to nonliving objects or a highly specific focus on nongenital body part(s) *plus* distress or impairment.

**Vanilla.** Non-kinky sexual or relational practice. Used descriptively, not pejoratively, by most kinksters.

**Kinkster.** Community-internal term for a person who practices kink.

**Scene.** A bounded episode of kink play with a beginning, middle, and end; usually negotiated in advance.

**Play.** General term for kink activity; "playing" with a partner means engaging in BDSM together.

**Aftercare.** The structured post-scene care addressing physical and emotional needs of all participants. Aftercare typically includes hydration, warmth, physical closeness, verbal reassurance, and processing; its absence is the leading preventable cause of sub drop.

**Sub drop / Top drop / Dom drop.** A post-scene physical and emotional crash, typically 1–72 hours after a scene, caused by the dissipation of stress and reward hormones (cortisol, endorphins, adrenaline). Sub drop is widely discussed; *dom drop* and *top drop* are under-discussed but real, often misattributed to "stress" or "guilt" by the dominant.

**Subspace.** A trance-like altered state of consciousness experienced by some submissives during intense scenes, characterized by floatiness, time distortion, reduced verbal capacity, elevated pain tolerance, and "out-of-body" sensation. Ambler et al. (2017, *Psychology of Consciousness*) operationalized subspace as aligning with Dietrich's "transient hypofrontality" — temporary impairment of the brain's executive function, supporting the felt experience of cognitive shutdown.

**Topspace / Domspace.** The complementary altered state experienced by tops/dominants — sharpened focus, decreased self-consciousness, sense of control. Sagarin's lab (Ambler et al., 2017) randomly assigned 14 experienced BDSM practitioners to top or bottom roles and found topspace aligns with Csikszentmihalyi's flow state.

**Headspace.** General term for any altered cognitive/emotional state induced by play.

**Little space.** The specific headspace a little enters when accessing their younger-feeling state. Can be entered through ritual cues (stuffies, paci, specific clothing), specific nicknames, or simply being told "it's little time."

**Big space.** Adult/everyday headspace.

**Middle space.** Intermediate headspace, neither fully little nor fully adult.

**Hard limit.** An activity a participant absolutely will not do; non-negotiable.

**Soft limit.** An activity a participant is hesitant about, willing to try only under specific conditions, or willing to approach gradually.

**Negotiation.** The pre-scene (and ideally ongoing) communication establishing what will and will not happen, safewords, aftercare needs, and contingencies. Considered the ethical foundation of BDSM.

**Yes/No/Maybe list (checklist).** A structured tool for negotiation in which each partner marks specific activities as yes (want), no (won't), or maybe (interested under conditions).

**Safeword.** A pre-agreed word, often unrelated to the scene's content, that signals the bottom (or top) wants to stop or slow down. The most common system is the **traffic light**: "red" (stop now), "yellow" (slow / check in), "green" (all good / more please).

**Safe signal.** A non-verbal safeword used when speech is impossible (gags, deep subspace) — e.g., dropping a held object, hand squeeze sequences, finger snaps.

**SSC (Safe, Sane, Consensual).** The first widely adopted ethical framework, coined by **David Stein** for the Gay Male S/M Activists (GMSMA) in the 1980s. Requires activities be safe, participants of sound mind, and all parties consenting.

**RACK (Risk-Aware Consensual Kink).** Coined by **Gary Switch**; reframes "safe" as "risk-aware," acknowledging no activity is truly without risk and that informed risk acceptance is more honest than the claim of safety.

**PRICK (Personal Responsibility Informed Consensual Kink).** Reportedly coined around 2002; amplifies RACK by emphasizing each participant's active personal responsibility to inform themselves of risks.

**4Cs (Caring, Communication, Consent, Caution).** Proposed by **D.J. Williams, Jeremy N. Thomas, Emily E. Prior, and M. Candace Christensen** in the *Electronic Journal of Human Sexuality*, Vol. 17 (2014). The 4Cs framework adds the relational/affective dimensions (caring, communication) absent from SSC and RACK.

**CCCC / CCC (Committed, Compassionate, Consensual).** Variants more common in M/s communities, emphasizing the relational dimension.

**FRIES (Freely given, Reversible, Informed, Enthusiastic, Specific).** Popularized by Planned Parenthood; an accessible consent mnemonic widely adopted in college sex education and increasingly in kink communities.

**EPP (Explicit Prior Permission).** Developed by the NCSF as a framework for crafting clear, advance consent — particularly for edge play and CNC where in-scene safewords may be partly suspended.

**Consent (informed, enthusiastic, ongoing, revocable).** The bedrock ethical concept: agreement must be made with full information, freely given without coercion, actively wanted (not merely permitted), continuously reaffirmed, and withdrawable at any moment.

**Edge play.** Activities involving heightened physical, psychological, or emotional risk — fire play, breath play, knife play, CNC, fear play. Typically reserved for experienced practitioners with strong trust.

**Fear play.** Negotiated play that intentionally evokes fear as part of the erotic experience.

**CNC (Consensual Non-Consent).** An edge play category in which a pre-negotiated scene simulates non-consent. Both parties consent extensively in advance to scenarios (often rape-play, abduction, interrogation) that, *within the bounded scene*, suspend in-the-moment verbal consent or limit safeword availability. CNC requires high trust, deep negotiation, and robust aftercare; NCSF's EPP framework was developed in part for such scenarios (Psychology Today, 2025, "Rising Interest in Consensual Non-Consent").

**Impact play.** Striking the body with hands, paddles, floggers, canes, whips, etc. for erotic sensation.

**Sensation play.** Activities focused on stimulating the senses — temperature (ice, wax), texture (fur, sandpaper), electrical, sharp objects — without necessarily involving impact.

**Breath play.** Erotic restriction of breath; considered very high-risk edge play due to mortality potential.

**Knife play.** Use of blades (often dulled) for sensation, fear, or aesthetic effect.

**Bondage.** Physical restraint using rope, cuffs, tape, leather, or other materials.

**Rope (shibari / kinbaku).** Japanese-derived rope bondage. **Shibari** (縛り) means "binding" in Japanese generally; **kinbaku** (緊縛, "tight binding") was developed as a term for erotic rope bondage by bakushi Minomura Kou and Tsujimura Takashi in the August 1952 issue of *Kitan Club*. Itoh Seiu is widely considered the "father of modern kinbaku." A common modern Western distinction (attributed to Osada Steve) is that shibari refers to the artistic/aesthetic practice and kinbaku to the connective/sensual/erotic practice — though Japanese practitioners largely use the terms interchangeably.

**Suspension.** Bondage in which the bottom is partially or fully suspended off the ground; considered very high-risk and requires advanced anatomy and rigging knowledge.

**Sadism.** Deriving pleasure (often erotic) from causing pain or psychological distress to a consenting partner. DSM-5-TR distinguishes **sexual sadism (paraphilia)** from **sexual sadism disorder** (which requires either distress/impairment or acting on urges with a non-consenting person).

**Masochism.** Deriving pleasure from receiving pain, humiliation, or psychological distress. Parallel DSM-5-TR distinction applies.

**Algolagnia.** Older clinical term for sexual pleasure from pain (Greek: *algos*, pain + *lagneia*, lust); largely supplanted by "masochism."

**Mono / Poly / Ethical non-monogamy.** Monogamous (one partner), polyamorous (multiple loving relationships with consent of all), and the broader category of consensual non-monogamy. Foundational text: Easton & Hardy, *The Ethical Slut* (3rd ed., 2017).

**Munch.** A casual social gathering of kinksters in a public, vanilla venue (restaurant, bar) — no play, no fetish attire. The term derives from "burger munch" — community oral history attributes the first to a "Kirk's Burger Munch" in Palo Alto, CA, organized via the Usenet newsgroup **alt.sex.bondage** in the early 1990s; documented use by Boston's "Burger Munch" appears in an alt.sex.bondage post dated September 4, 1993 (per Wikipedia, "Munch (BDSM)").

**Dungeon.** A space (private or club-owned) equipped for BDSM play — typically with crosses, suspension points, benches, etc.

**Play party.** A social event where BDSM play occurs, with house rules around consent, photography, and aftercare.

**Fetish night.** A bar/club night themed around fetish wear and/or kink-friendly socializing; usually less play-focused than a dungeon.

**Debrief / Check-in.** Post-scene conversation processing what worked, what didn't, and emotional residue; an essential aftercare component.

### C. Somatic Sexology and Embodiment Terminology

**Somatic sexology.** A field of sexology that centers the body, sensation, and the nervous system in understanding and educating about sexuality. Practitioners often integrate breath, movement, touch, and trauma-informed frameworks.

**Sexological bodywork.** A professional discipline founded by **Joseph Kramer, PhD**, formalized through the Institute for the Advanced Study of Human Sexuality and the Association of Certified Sexological Bodyworkers (ACSB). Sexological bodyworkers offer hands-on educational sessions including body mapping, breath work, and erotic anatomy education within a clear professional scope. **Caffyn Jesse** has been a key figure in developing the curriculum.

**Somatic Experiencing (SE).** A trauma therapy developed by **Peter A. Levine, PhD** (*Waking the Tiger*, 1997; *In an Unspoken Voice*, 2010) that approaches trauma as incomplete physiological survival responses stored in the body. SE works through body sensation rather than narrative.

**Titration (somatic experiencing).** The practice of working with very small, manageable amounts of trauma activation at a time to avoid overwhelm and retraumatization (Levine).

**Pendulation.** Levine's term for the natural oscillation between sensations of activation/contraction and sensations of safety/expansion. Therapists deliberately move clients back and forth between resourced and activated states to build nervous-system flexibility.

**Embodiment.** The lived, felt sense of being in one's body; the capacity to perceive and inhabit physical experience without dissociation.

**Interoception.** The neural sense of the internal state of the body — hunger, heart rate, breath, gut sensations, emotional "felt sense." Central to Levine's SE and to current trauma neuroscience.

**Proprioception.** The sense of body position and movement in space — where one's limbs are without looking.

**Window of Tolerance.** Coined by **Daniel J. Siegel, MD** in *The Developing Mind* (Guilford, 1999). The optimal zone of nervous-system arousal in which a person can process experience, regulate emotion, and remain socially engaged. Above the window is **hyperarousal** (panic, irritability, fight/flight); below is **hypoarousal** (numbness, dissociation, shutdown). Trauma narrows the window; therapeutic work and somatic practices widen it.

**Polyvagal Theory.** Developed by **Stephen W. Porges, PhD**, first formally proposed in Porges, S. W. (1995), "Orienting in a defensive world: Mammalian modifications of our evolutionary heritage. A polyvagal theory," *Psychophysiology*, 32, 301–318. Proposes a three-state hierarchical model of the autonomic nervous system grounded in vagal anatomy:

- **Ventral vagal state.** The mammalian "social engagement system" supporting calm, connection, and felt safety.
- **Sympathetic state.** Mobilization for fight or flight.
- **Dorsal vagal state.** Immobilization, shutdown, collapse, dissociation — the most evolutionarily primitive defensive state.

(Note: polyvagal theory has received methodological critiques from Grossman & Taylor and from Neuhuber & Berthoud regarding its phylogenetic and anatomical claims; Porges responded in *Clinical Neuropsychiatry*, 2025.)

**Social Engagement System (SES).** The Porges-defined network of cranial nerves (especially ventral vagal) supporting facial expression, vocal prosody, listening, and reciprocal social cueing — the neurobiological substrate of co-regulation.

**Neuroception.** Porges' term for the nervous system's continuous, sub-conscious scanning of internal and external cues for safety, danger, or life threat — operating below conscious perception.

**Co-regulation.** Defined by **Deb Dana, LCSW** (*The Polyvagal Theory in Therapy*, Norton, 2018) as "the biological imperative to be connected with others to survive. We hope to meet others who are regulated and welcoming." The nervous-system synchronization between two people — first established in infant-caregiver dyads and necessary lifelong.

**Self-regulation.** The capacity to return one's own nervous system to a regulated state without external support; built on a foundation of earlier co-regulation experiences.

**Dysregulation.** Loss of nervous-system regulation — sympathetic activation, dorsal shutdown, or oscillation between them.

**Freeze / Fight / Flight / Fawn.** The four primary trauma responses. *Freeze* is the inability to move despite mobilization energy; *fight* and *flight* are sympathetic mobilization; *fawn* (popularized by Pete Walker, *Complex PTSD*, 2013) is appeasement/people-pleasing as a survival strategy.

**Arousal nonconcordance.** Coined and popularized by **Emily Nagoski, PhD** in *Come As You Are* (Simon & Schuster, 2015). The well-established phenomenon of a lack of overlap between physiological genital response and subjective experience of arousal/desire — about 50% overlap for people with penises and approximately 10% for people with vulvas. Crucial implication: bodily response is *not* a valid indicator of consent or desire.

**Dual Control Model.** The model of sexual response (Bancroft & Janssen, Kinsey Institute) Nagoski uses in *Come As You Are*, framing arousal as the balance between a "sexual excitation system" (accelerator) and "sexual inhibition system" (brake).

**Erotic Blueprints.** A typology of erotic arousal developed by somatic sexologist **Jaiya** identifying five "blueprint types": **Energetic** (aroused by space, tease, anticipation), **Sensual** (aroused by full-sensory experience), **Sexual** (aroused by direct, recognizably sexual stimuli), **Kinky** (aroused by taboo and psychological/sensation play), and **Shapeshifter** (aroused by all four). Jaiya, *Your Blueprint for Pleasure* (Union Square, 2023).

**Wheel of Consent.** Developed by **Dr. Betty Martin** (somatic sex educator; *The Art of Receiving and Giving: The Wheel of Consent*, 2021). A model that splits any touch interaction by two axes — *who is doing* and *who is it for* — yielding four quadrants:

- **Serve** (I am doing; it's for you).
- **Accept** (you are doing; it's for me).
- **Take** (I am doing; it's for me — with your permission).
- **Allow** (you are doing; it's for you — with my permission).

Each quadrant has a "shadow" (the same action without consent: martyrdom, doormat, stealing, freeloading).

**Three Minute Game / May I, Will You Game.** An embodied practice (developed by Harry Faddis and adapted by Betty Martin) for experientially learning the Wheel of Consent.

**Pleasure mapping / Body mapping.** A somatic practice (taught by Jaiya, drawing on Joseph Kramer's training) of systematically exploring the body's response to different qualities of touch, often rated on a scale.

**Sensate Focus.** A psychosexual therapy technique developed by **William H. Masters and Virginia E. Johnson** (*Human Sexual Inadequacy*, 1970). Structured, non-demand touching exercises that defer genital contact to reduce performance anxiety and rebuild attentional presence to sensation. Weiner & Avery-Clark (2014, *Sexual and Relationship Therapy* 29:3) emphasize the original intent was "non-demand touching for one's own interest" rather than non-demand pleasuring of the partner.

**Containment / Holding space.** Therapeutic and somatic terms for providing a steady, non-reactive presence within which another person can experience and process difficult material; relevant in both clinical sessions and Caregiver/little dynamics.

**Embodied consent.** Consent expressed through bodily congruence — voice, posture, breath, facial expression, eye contact — not merely verbal yes/no. Central to Betty Martin's work and to somatic sexology generally.

### D. Clinical Psychology Terminology

**Attachment theory.** Originated by **John Bowlby** (mid-20th century) and operationalized by **Mary Ainsworth** (the "Strange Situation," 1970s). The framework that early caregiver-infant bonds shape lifelong relational patterns. Extended to adult romantic relationships in the 1980s.

**Secure attachment.** Comfortable with intimacy and autonomy; trusts that needs will be met; can self-soothe and seek support.

**Anxious (preoccupied) attachment.** Fear of abandonment, hyperactivation of attachment needs, seeking high closeness and reassurance.

**Avoidant (dismissive) attachment.** Deactivation of attachment needs, discomfort with closeness, prioritization of self-sufficiency.

**Disorganized (fearful-avoidant) attachment.** Inconsistent oscillation between anxious and avoidant patterns; typically develops when the caregiver was simultaneously a source of safety *and* fear. Most common in adult survivors of childhood abuse.

**Internal Working Models.** Bowlby's term for the mental representations of self, other, and relationship formed in early attachment that guide adult relational expectations.

**Earned secure attachment.** The clinical phenomenon of moving from an insecure attachment style to secure functioning through therapy, relationships, or reflective practice.

**Age regression (clinical).** A psychological phenomenon — often involuntary — in which a person reverts to thoughts, feelings, or behaviors of a younger age, frequently as a coping response to stress, trauma, anxiety, or overwhelm. Joshua Kaplow has described it as "a willful release of age-appropriate behavior to escape present reality." **Clinical age regression is non-sexual** and is sometimes therapeutically engaged within trauma work; conflation with sexual ageplay is a source of significant harm to age regressors.

**Ageplay (kink).** A consensual adult roleplay in which one or more participants adopts an age different from their own for erotic, playful, or nurturing purposes. May or may not be sexual. Per forensic psychologist Anil Aggrawal, ageplay is unrelated to pedophilia (which requires sexual attraction to actual prepubescent children); ageplay involves only consenting adults (Wikipedia, "Ageplay").

**Age slider / Age fluid.** A little (or regressor) whose accessed "age" varies across sessions or moods rather than being fixed.

**Paraphilic infantilism (autonepiophilia).** Coined by **John Money** (1984). The sexological term for adult sexual arousal and/or emotional comfort from imagining and enacting being an infant. Distinct from CGL/DDlg, which centers a power-exchange caregiver dynamic, not infantile regression.

**ABDL (Adult Baby / Diaper Lover).** A community umbrella covering adult babies and/or diaper-focused arousal. Overlaps with but is distinct from CGL: ABDL centers infancy/diapers, CGL centers power exchange. Hawkinson & Zamboni (2014, *Archives of Sexual Behavior*) is a foundational empirical study.

**Dissociation.** A disruption of normally integrated psychological functions (consciousness, memory, identity, perception). Ranges from mild (highway hypnosis) to clinically significant (DID).

**Depersonalization.** Feeling detached from one's own self, body, or mental processes — "I'm watching myself from outside."

**Derealization.** Feeling the external world is unreal, dreamlike, or distant.

**PTSD.** Post-traumatic stress disorder — DSM-5-TR criteria require exposure to actual/threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence followed by intrusion symptoms, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition/mood, and arousal/reactivity changes.

**Complex PTSD (cPTSD).** Coined by **Judith Herman** (*Trauma and Recovery*, 1992); recognized in ICD-11 (not DSM-5-TR). Results from prolonged, repeated trauma (often interpersonal/developmental) and adds three symptom clusters to PTSD: difficulties in affect regulation, negative self-concept, and disturbed relationships. Pete Walker's *Complex PTSD: From Surviving to Thriving* (2013) is a popular community text.

**Trauma-informed care.** A practice framework (developed in healthcare and social services) recognizing trauma's prevalence and shaping interactions to avoid retraumatization. SAMHSA's six principles: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration; empowerment; cultural/historical/gender issues.

**Inner child / Inner child work.** A therapeutic concept (popularized by John Bradshaw; integrated into many modalities) of engaging with one's younger self as a part still present in adult psychology. Distinct from kink "little" identity but sometimes overlapping; some littles describe little space as access to a healed inner child.

**Internal Family Systems (IFS) / Parts work.** Developed by **Richard C. Schwartz, PhD** in the 1980s. IFS posits the psyche as a system of "parts" (sub-personalities) including *exiles* (carrying pain), *managers* (proactively protective), and *firefighters* (reactively protective), all surrounding a core, undamaged **Self** characterized by curiosity, compassion, calm, and confidence. Schwartz: "All of us are born with many sub-minds — or parts. These parts are not imaginary or symbolic. They are individuals who exist as an internal family within us — and the key to health and happiness is to honor, understand, and love every part" (*No Bad Parts*, Sounds True, 2021).

**Ego state therapy.** A parts-based hypnoanalytic approach developed by **John G. and Helen H. Watkins** (*Ego States: Theory and Therapy*, Norton, 1997), foundational to dissociation and trauma work and a precursor to IFS. Helen Watkins defined an ego state as "an organized system of behavior and experience whose elements are bound together by some common principle."

**Regression in service of the ego.** Coined by **Ernst Kris** (1952) in psychoanalysis. The capacity for adaptive, voluntary access to less-regulated psychological states (play, creativity, sleep, sexual surrender) without losing reality testing. Cited by kink-aware clinicians as a healthy framing for little space and subspace.

**Transference.** The unconscious redirection of feelings, expectations, or relational templates from past significant figures onto a present person (often the therapist). Originated with **Freud** (*Studies on Hysteria*, 1895).

**Countertransference.** The therapist's reciprocal unconscious feelings and reactions toward the client; once viewed as obstacle, now widely seen as clinical information.

**Erotic transference.** The patient's experience of romantic, sensual, or sexual feelings toward the therapist as displaced from earlier figures. Foundational text: **Freud's "Observations on Transference-Love"** (1915, *SE 12*). Modern texts include David Mann, *Psychotherapy: An Erotic Relationship* (1997), and Wrye & Welles, *The Narration of Desire* (1994).

**Projection.** Attributing one's own unacceptable feelings, impulses, or traits to another person.

**Projective identification.** A more interactive form (Melanie Klein) in which the projector behaves toward the other in ways that elicit the projected experience in them.

**Kink-Aware Professional (KAP).** A clinician, healthcare provider, lawyer, or bodyworker listed on the **NCSF's Kink and Polyamory Aware Professionals Directory** (kapprofessionals.org), maintained by the National Coalition for Sexual Freedom since January 2006. Listings are self-declared and divided by awareness level (Friendly / Aware / Knowledgeable); the directory does not endorse but provides a starting point for finding non-pathologizing providers.

**Paraphilia vs. paraphilic disorder (DSM-5/DSM-5-TR).** A pivotal nosological change introduced in DSM-5 (2013): **paraphilia** is "a persistent, intense, atypical sexual arousal pattern, independent of whether it causes any distress or impairment"; **paraphilic disorder** is a paraphilia that *additionally* causes the individual clinically significant distress or impairment, *or* whose satisfaction has entailed personal harm or risk of harm to others (e.g., acting on urges with non-consenting persons). Per the DSM-5 sub-workgroup, this means "you could practice sexual masochism or cross-dressing without having a mental disorder" (DeAngelis, *Psychiatric News*, 2013, "DSM to Distinguish Paraphilias From Paraphilic Disorders").

**Schema therapy.** Developed by **Jeffrey E. Young, PhD** (Schema Therapy Institute) as an integrative extension of CBT. Identifies 18 **Early Maladaptive Schemas (EMS)** — "pervasive self-defeating patterns of memory, emotion, cognition, and bodily sensation" developed in childhood (Young, Klosko & Weishaar, *Schema Therapy: A Practitioner's Guide*, 2003). The **Disconnection/Rejection** and **Impaired Autonomy** domains are particularly relevant to caregiver/dependent dynamics in kink contexts (where they may be either re-enacted or healed).

**Co-dependence vs. healthy interdependence.** Co-dependence (Melody Beattie, *Codependent No More*, 1986) names a pattern of self-sacrificing enmeshment that subordinates one's identity to another's. Healthy interdependence preserves individual selves while allowing mutual reliance. A frequently-asked question in CGL dynamics: when is "I need my Daddy" healthy interdependence vs. co-dependent enmeshment? The answer turns on whether the little's autonomy and capacity for self-care are eroded or supported.

**Limerence.** Coined by psychologist **Dorothy Tennov** in *Love and Limerence: The Experience of Being in Love* (Stein and Day, 1979): "An involuntary interpersonal state that involves an acute longing for emotional reciprocation, obsessive-compulsive thoughts, feelings, and behaviors, and emotional dependence on another person." Distinct from love or healthy attachment.

**Compartmentalization.** A defense mechanism (or, when conscious, a coping strategy) of keeping incompatible aspects of self or experience mentally separated; can be adaptive (closeted kinksters at work) or maladaptive (avoiding integration of trauma).

### E. Specific Daddy/little Dynamic Terminology

**Discipline (DDlg/CGL usage).** The structural/teaching dimension of the dynamic: rules, expectations, accountability, verbal/non-verbal correction. The "boring" backbone work of a dynamic.

**Punishment.** A genuine, non-erotic consequence intended to discourage rule-breaking and modify behavior. Community consensus: should *not* be enjoyable for the submissive; serves to "enforce the dynamic" and "clear the slate."

**Funishment.** Play that performs punishment for erotic effect — the bottom enjoys the "punishment," and both partners know it. Common in DDlg/CGL where bratting earns a sought-after spanking. The key distinction: punishment isn't fun by design; funishment is fun by design (Kinky Curiosity).

**Rules.** Agreed standards of conduct within a dynamic (bedtime, daily check-ins, behavior in public).

**Rituals.** Repeated practices that mark transitions or reinforce the dynamic (morning check-ins, collar placement, bedtime stories).

**Praise kink.** Erotic and emotional arousal/satisfaction derived from receiving verbal praise, particularly from a Dominant. Often overlaps with little dynamics where validation is core to the connection.

**Validation.** The act of acknowledging another's feelings/experience as real and reasonable; in caregiver/little contexts, a central nurturing function.

**Stuffies.** Plush/stuffed animals carried, slept with, or used for comfort during little space. Functional symbol of regression and care.

**Paci (Pacifier).** An adult-sized or standard pacifier used for self-soothing and as a regression cue.

**Sippy (Sippy cup).** A child-style covered drinking cup; symbol of little space and being cared for.

**Onesie.** A one-piece infant- or child-style garment (often snap-crotch) worn during little space.

**Non-sexual vs. sexual little space (debate).** Within the broader CGL umbrella, there is ongoing community debate about whether little space is inherently sexual, can be either, or should be kept separate. CGLre and many non-regression-focused littles assert non-sexual identity; DDlg under the BDSM umbrella explicitly includes (but does not require) sexual integration. The mature position is that *both* are valid and that conflating them (in either direction) causes harm.

### F. Community and Cultural Terminology

**Old Guard.** The romanticized first generation of (predominantly gay male) leathermen forming community in U.S. port cities (NYC, SF, Chicago, LA) from roughly 1945–late 1960s. Characterized in retrospective accounts by **Guy Baldwin** ("The Old Guard," *International Leatherman* #20, 1998) as featuring military-derived protocol, hierarchy, mentorship, motorcycle clubs, and earned leather. Whether the "Old Guard" was a unified tradition or a retrospective construction is contested (Robert Davolt; oldguardleather.men).

**New Guard / New Leather.** The post-AIDS, increasingly diverse evolution of leather culture incorporating women, lesbian leather culture, trans inclusion, and the loosening of strict protocol requirements.

**Leather community.** The historically gay-male-originated subculture organized around leather garments, motorcycle club culture, and BDSM/SM practice; now encompassing many sub-communities (KYNK 101).

**Pat Califia (Patrick Califia).** Influential author and theorist of BDSM (especially lesbian and later trans-masculine perspectives); co-founder of Samois, author of *Sensuous Magic*, *Sapphistry*, *Public Sex*.

**Samois.** First lesbian-feminist BDSM organization in the United States. Co-founded on June 13, 1978 by writer Pat Califia (then identifying as a lesbian), anthropologist Gayle Rubin, and sixteen others in San Francisco; the organization existed from 1978 to 1983 (per Wikipedia, "Leather subculture," and the Leather Hall of Fame).

**The Scene.** Community-internal shorthand for the active local BDSM/kink community in a given city.

**Sex-positive.** A cultural orientation affirming consensual sexuality as a positive force, rejecting shame and pathologization. Associated with Carol Queen, Gayle Rubin's "charmed circle" critique (1984), and the broader sexological tradition.

**Consent culture.** A broader social-movement framing (Kitty Stryker et al., *Ask: Building Consent Culture*, 2017) extending consent principles from sexual contexts into all of life.

**TNG (The Next Generation).** Kink-community groups specifically for younger members (typically ages 18–35), founded in 1999 by Boymeat (Philip Wolf), Kyri, Schelli, and Josh HighTower, beginning with TES-TNG (The Eulenspiegel Society, New York) and a Black Rose TNG (DC).

**Munch (community context).** As above — the entry-level social gathering, considered the safest first step for newcomers.

**TASHRA (The Alternative Sexualities Health Research Alliance).** Non-profit community-based research and education organization founded in 2012 by **Jess Waldura, MD** (UCSF family physician/HIV provider); **Richard Sprott, PhD** (developmental psychologist); and **Anna Randall, DHS, MSW, MPH** (clinical sexologist). Conducts kink-health research (the 2016 National Kink Health Survey, n≈1,400, was the first major national snapshot), educates healthcare providers, and develops kink-friendly clinical practice guidelines. (tashra.org; Sprott & Randall, 2024, *Journal of Humanistic Psychology*.)

**NCSF (National Coalition for Sexual Freedom).** Sex-positive advocacy and education organization founded in 1997 by Susan Wright under the auspices of the New York SM Activists, with five founding coalition partners: Black Rose, The Eulenspiegel Society, Gay Male S/M Activists, National Leather Association International, and the Society of Janus (per Wikipedia, "National Coalition for Sexual Freedom"). Maintains the KAP directory, runs Incident Reporting & Response, and advocated successfully for the DSM-5 paraphilia/paraphilic-disorder distinction.

**FetLife.** A free social-networking platform for BDSM, fetish, and kink communities, launched January 3, 2008 by **John Baku** (John Kopanas) in Montreal and operated by BitLove Inc. With a registered user base exceeding 10 million (per Wikipedia, "FetLife"), it functions more as social network than dating site; munches, local groups, and event listings are commonly organized there.

**Kink Clinical Practice Guidelines.** A document published by **Keely Kolmes, PsyD** and colleagues, hosted by TASHRA, providing clinicians evidence-based standards for working with kink-identified clients without pathologizing consensual practices. Builds on Kolmes & Weitzman's 2010 "A Guide to Choosing a Kink-Aware Therapist."

## Recommendations

For a person exploring or practicing Daddy Dom / little sub dynamics, the staged path that the literature and community consensus most strongly support:

**Stage 1 (orientation, before any play): Read foundational consent literature.** Specifically: Easton & Hardy's *The New Bottoming Book* and *The New Topping Book*; Betty Martin's *The Art of Receiving and Giving*; Williams, Thomas, Prior & Christensen's 4Cs paper (open access via the *Electronic Journal of Human Sexuality*, Vol. 17, 2014); and the NCSF EPP framework. **Threshold to advance:** you can articulate the difference between consent, permission, and acquiescence in your own words.

**Stage 2 (community immersion): Attend munches and CGL-specific socials before any private play with a new partner.** Use FetLife event listings and TNG groups (if age-appropriate) to find local options. **Threshold to advance:** at least 2–3 munches attended; you have a few non-romantic kink acquaintances you could text with questions.

**Stage 3 (dynamic-building): Negotiate explicitly, with written checklists.** Use a yes/no/maybe list, identify hard and soft limits, establish a traffic-light safeword *and* a non-verbal safe signal, and discuss aftercare for both partners (including dom drop). **Threshold to advance:** both partners can name three of each other's limits and one specific aftercare need.

**Stage 4 (clinical scaffolding, if relevant): Find a Kink-Aware Professional via the NCSF KAP directory** if any of the following apply: trauma history that the dynamic touches; difficulty distinguishing healthy interdependence from co-dependence; involuntary age regression that predates and persists outside the dynamic; or recurring sub drop that aftercare doesn't resolve. **Threshold to advance:** the dynamic is supporting your wider functioning, not narrowing it.

**Stage 5 (deepening practice): Build somatic and parts-work literacy.** Learn polyvagal-informed self-regulation (Deb Dana's *Anchored*, 2021); explore IFS-style parts work to understand which part of you is in little space and what it needs; map your erotic blueprint (Jaiya). **Threshold to escalate (e.g., to 24/7 or formal collaring):** you can stay in your window of tolerance during disagreements with your partner, and they with you.

**Signals that should prompt re-evaluation or pause:** the dynamic is being used to avoid rather than process trauma; co-regulation has collapsed into co-dependence (your autonomy is shrinking); aftercare is consistently absent or rushed; protocol has slid from chosen structure into coerced compliance; or in-scene safewords are routinely ignored or punished. Any one of these warrants explicit conversation; two or more warrants outside support.

## Caveats

1. **Contested terms.** "Old Guard" is romanticized and historically contested; "shibari vs. kinbaku" distinctions are largely Western inventions; "Daddy Dom" and "little" identities are evolving and not monolithically defined. Where contested, the glossary flags the contestation.
2. **Clinical vs. community usage diverges meaningfully** for several terms — most importantly *regression*, *fetish*, *sadism/masochism*, and *paraphilia*. The DSM-5-TR's introduction of the paraphilia/paraphilic-disorder distinction is the single most important reform aligning clinical practice with kink-affirmative care, but is not yet uniformly applied by clinicians; the NCSF KAP directory is the most reliable filter.
3. **Polyvagal theory is influential but not uncontroversial.** Critiques by Grossman & Taylor and Neuhuber & Berthoud question its phylogenetic and anatomical specifics. Clinically, the *practical framework* (states of safety, mobilization, shutdown; co-regulation; neuroception) remains widely used and useful even if the underlying neurobiology is revised.
4. **CGL/DDlg is widely misunderstood as pedophilic or incestuous by mainstream observers.** This is empirically and ethically wrong — the practice is between consenting adults — but practitioners should be aware of the stigma and its consequences (especially in family-court and employment contexts; NCSF tracks these incidents).
5. **No glossary is neutral.** The selections, attributions, and framings here reflect choices made by the author. Where a single canonical attribution doesn't exist (primal play, brat dynamics, much of CGL terminology), the entry says so rather than inventing one.
6. **A small number of community concepts (especially within CGL/DDlg) lack peer-reviewed sources** and rely on community glossaries and authoritative practitioners. This is flagged where relevant.
7. **The "munch" origin story** (Kirk's Burger Munch, Palo Alto, April 1992) is community oral history rather than independently documented; Wikipedia confirms the term "burger munch" and a Boston usage by September 1993, but the exact Palo Alto origin date is not independently verifiable.
