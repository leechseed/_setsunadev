But concert and DJ truss is almost always aluminum, not steel, which means TIG welding is required—that's significantly harder to learn (1-3 years to competency) and the equipment costs jump to $1,500-3,000 or more for a capable TIG welder with AC capability.

The real issue is that homemade truss has no load rating documentation, which creates massive liability problems if something goes wrong. Venues, inspectors, and safety officers often reject uncertified rigging entirely. Venues, promoters, and insurers increasingly expect rigging with documented load specs, and a homemade truss has none of that unless a structural engineer signs off on the design — which typically costs $500–$2,000 on its own.

For the welding path, realistic expectations look like this. Learning MIG on steel to a functional hobbyist level takes roughly six months of consistent practice; getting to truly good takes one to two years. Learning AC TIG on aluminum to the level needed for thin-wall structural tube welding is more like two to three years, and the equipment is steeper: an AC/DC TIG machine capable of clean aluminum work runs $1,500–$3,000, plus tank, consumables, PPE, and material. Community college welding programs in Florida typically run $200–$800 per semester and would be the cheapest skilled path. All-in to start practicing seriously: $2,500–$4,000 for steel MIG, closer to $4,000–$6,000 for aluminum TIG, not counting the engineering stamp you would still need for rated rigging.

The cheaper, faster, and more venue-accepted route is buying used Global Truss or equivalent (Tomcat, Tyler, Applied Electronics, Show Solutions). Global Truss F34 is the industry workhorse 12-inch box; F23 and F24 are lighter single- or duo-tube options that fit small DJ rigs. A used F34 6.5-foot section runs roughly $200–$400, and a complete small DJ setup — four totems with a crossbar, or a modest goalpost — typically lands at $800–$2,000 used. The market lives on Reverb, Facebook Marketplace, ProSoundWeb classifieds, audiogear.com, and local production-company estate or downsizing sales. Shipping aluminum truss is actually not as brutal as it sounds because it is light for its size, but local pickup is always cheaper if you can find it.

A local steel fab shop is a valid option only if you specifically need a non-rigging structure — a backdrop frame, a static prop stand, something decorative that does not carry hung weight over people. For actual overhead rigging, even a custom fab shop will (correctly) push you toward engineered specs, and at that point the used-truss market beats them on price by a wide margin.
