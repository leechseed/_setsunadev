With franchising, you pay a large upfront fee plus ongoing royalties, you own and control the unit, and you capture the full upside—but you also bear all the risk. The franchisor gets their fees with minimal capital exposure. With the managing partner equity model, the company owns the restaurant and you're essentially a salaried operator with profit participation. You invest a small buy-in as skin in the game, get a salary plus a percentage of profits, but you don't own the franchise, can't sell it, and your equity stake is limited to that 5-10% participation interest rather than ownership of the entire unit.

So when the user frames this as a "low price point of entry compared to franchises costing millions," they're comparing different things entirely. The advantage is lower capital required and less personal risk, but the tradeoff is you're not building an asset you can sell or pass on—you're an operator with profit sharing, not an owner.

The appeal is clear: you get a guaranteed salary floor so your livelihood isn't entirely dependent on the store's performance, plus real upside through profit participation and a potential liquidity event after five years. You also get corporate support and established systems. But your ceiling is capped at that 5-10% participation, whereas a franchisee keeps everything above their royalties, and you can't build equity in a sellable asset the way an owner can.

**Advantages**
- Almost no capital barrier ($7,500 vs. franchise-level money), so it's accessible to a skilled operator without a war chest.
- Downside protection. You've got a guaranteed $75K base, so you're not betting your livelihood and home on one unit's performance the way a franchisee is.
- You're not personally on the hook for the lease, build-out, or debt — the company carries that risk.
- Real upside if the store performs: 5–10% of profits plus a payout event at year five.
- You operate a proven concept with corporate's systems, supply chain, and marketing already built.

**Disadvantages**
- Your upside is capped. If the store is a monster hit, the company keeps most of it — you keep your 5–10%. A franchisee keeps everything after royalties.
- You're not building a transferable asset. You can't sell "your" Metro Diner or pass it down. The year-five realization is a payout, not selling a business.
- Less autonomy — you run within their menu, pricing, and brand standards, and you answer to corporate.
- It's relationship-gated. Historically they recruited partners from the Bloomin' Brands/Outback network — they pick partners carefully and you have to "earn the right." It's not an open application like buying a franchise.
- No profit means no upside — if the unit isn't profitable, you're just on salary.
