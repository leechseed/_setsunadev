## 🤖 Claude  <sub>2026-08-06 09:26:03</sub>

Intermediate standards are usually expressed as a ratio of bodyweight rather than fixed numbers, since a 150 lb and 220 lb lifter shouldn't be judged on the same bar weight. The commonly cited intermediate 1RM benchmarks (male lifters):

- **Squat:** ~1.5x bodyweight
- **Bench press:** ~1.25x bodyweight
- **Deadlift:** ~1.75–2x bodyweight
- **Overhead press:** ~0.75x bodyweight

For a 180 lb lifter that's roughly a 270 squat, 225 bench, 315–360 deadlift, and 135 press.

For women, intermediate ratios run lower on upper body, closer on lower body: ~1.25x squat, ~0.7x bench, ~1.5x deadlift, ~0.5x press.

The gym shorthand version is the plate milestones — 135 press, 225 bench, 315 squat, 405 deadlift — though hitting all four actually puts you at the upper end of intermediate, bordering advanced. "Intermediate" here generally means stronger than about half of people who train seriously, typically reached after 1–2 years of consistent progressive training.

## 🤖 Claude  <sub>2026-08-06 09:26:50</sub>

Bodyweight ratios get unfair when you're carrying extra fat, because fat inflates the denominator without moving the bar. A 250 lb lifter and a 190 lb lifter with identical muscle mass would need a 375 vs. 285 squat to hit the same "intermediate" label — same engine, harsher grading.

Common workarounds:

- **Use your goal or lean-ish bodyweight** in the ratio instead of scale weight. If you're 250 but would be ~200 at a healthy composition, judge yourself against 200 lb standards.
- **Use absolute numbers instead.** Rough intermediate ballpark for men regardless of size: ~275 squat, ~225 bench, ~315 deadlift, ~135 press.
- **Use powerlifting formulas** like DOTS or Wilks, which scale nonlinearly so heavier lifters aren't expected to add strength pound-for-pound.

Two nuances: extra mass actually helps a bit on bench and squat (stability, leverage, shorter effective ROM), so those ratios sting less than they look. Deadlift at 2x bodyweight, though, gets genuinely brutal past ~230 lbs — almost nobody holds that ratio at high bodyweights.

Honestly, if you're overweight and training, the better scoreboard is absolute progress on the bar plus body composition trending the right way. The ratio standards will take care of themselves as both numbers move.

## 🤖 Claude  <sub>2026-08-06 09:52:41</sub>

The whole advantage is failing safely when you train alone. A full cage surrounds you with four (or six) posts, so the safety bars run the full depth of the cage — solid steel catching the bar no matter where you are in the rep. Miss a squat or bench rep, you just set the bar down on the safeties and crawl out. No spotter needed, ever.

A squat stand or half rack uses cantilevered spotter arms that stick out into open air. They work, but they can flex, tip the whole unit if you dump weight on the end of them, and you can straight-up miss them if the bar drifts forward. Benching heavy alone on spotter arms is sketchy in a way that benching in a cage just isn't.

Secondary perks of the cage: way more stable for band work and rack pulls off the pins, usually more attachment options (dip bars, landmine, spotter straps), built-in pull-up bar, and plate storage pegs that add ballast so the thing never moves.

The rack's advantages are footprint, price, and open access — nothing in your way for walking out or doing overhead work. But if you're training heavy solo — which sounds like your situation — the cage is the move. It's the difference between "I can grind a true max attempt" and "I better leave two reps in the tank because nobody's coming."
