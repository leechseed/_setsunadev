**Practical considerations that will narrow your search faster than price**

Ceiling height is the single most important spec for your use case and the one most listings do not highlight. For suspension work you want a minimum of 12 feet of clear height under the lowest structural member, and 16 to 20 feet is meaningfully better because it gives you working room above the suspension point for the rigging hardware and lighting. When you tour spaces, ask specifically about clear height to the bottom of the bar joists or trusses, not the peak.

Rigging anchorage is the second issue. You cannot drill into structural steel or attach load-bearing hardware to a leased building without written landlord approval, and most standard commercial leases prohibit it outright. The workaround used by film and theater production is a freestanding rigging frame — either a welded steel A-frame or a modular truss system (Global Truss, Applied NN) — that lives on the floor and carries its own load. Budget $2,000 to $6,000 for a rated truss setup. This also makes the rig portable if you relocate.

Lease framing is the third issue and the one most likely to cause problems if handled wrong. Standard Florida commercial leases include use-restriction clauses, and most prohibit adult-oriented businesses by default. The right framing for the lease is "photography and video production studio," which is accurate, defensible, and what the space is being used for in literal terms. Do not volunteer the content category, do not put it on signage, and do not have talent or clients identify the location publicly. Private landlords are generally easier to work with than corporate property management on this — they care that the rent clears, the space is maintained, and there are no police calls. Independently owned light-industrial properties on Capital Circle SW and in the Lake Bradford corridor tend to fall into this category.

Privacy and access matter operationally. You want a unit with its own dedicated entrance rather than a shared corridor, no shared walls with noise-sensitive tenants such as offices or daycares, parking that is not visible from a main road, and ideally a fenced or gated complex if you can find one. End-cap units in multi-tenant flex buildings are better than middle units for both privacy and load-in.

Power and ventilation are workshop considerations. For fabrication work — welding a rigging frame, finishing leather, working with adhesives — you want 220-volt service available and at least one exterior wall where you can install or use existing ventilation. Most flex units have single-phase 110/220 standard; three-phase is rarer and worth asking about only if you plan to run industrial machinery.
