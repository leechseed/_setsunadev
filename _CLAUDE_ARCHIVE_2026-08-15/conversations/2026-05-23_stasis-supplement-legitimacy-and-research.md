 I'm planning to give a comprehensive breakdown of Stasis as a real supplement product, walking through what it actually contains, how the ingredients work, and what the scientific evidence actually supports versus what's just marketing hype.Stasis is a real product, not a vaporware scam — it's sold by a company called Take Stasis (takestasis.com) and on Amazon as a two-bottle Day/Night system designed to be stacked *alongside* a prescription stimulant like Adderall, not replace it. The pitch is that it smooths out the rough edges of stimulants: afternoon crash, jitters, sleep disruption, cortisol spikes, oxidative stress. So the company exists, the bottles ship, the ingredients are listed transparently. That's the easy part. The harder question is whether it actually does what the marketing claims, and that's where things get murkier.

Here's the trick to understanding any supplement like this. There are two very different questions that get conflated on purpose. First: "Are the individual ingredients backed by research?" Second: "Is this specific formula, at these specific doses, in this specific combination, proven to do what the label says?" Stasis lives in the gap between those two questions. The first answer is "mostly yes." The second answer, as of right now, is "not really."

The Daytime formula's headline ingredients are Shoden Ashwagandha (120mg), L-Theanine (200mg), CoQ10, Saffron, Rhodiola Rosea, and B-vitamins (B6, B12, D3). The Nighttime formula adds magnesium bisglycinate, melatonin, Venetron, baicalein, apigenin, and DHH-B (a magnolia bark extract). Every one of those has at least some research behind it in isolation. L-theanine has reasonably solid evidence for reducing acute stress and improving attention when paired with caffeine. Ashwagandha (especially the Shoden extract) has decent clinical data for lowering cortisol. Magnesium glycinate and melatonin are well-established sleep aids. Saffron has some real, if modest, trials for mood. None of that is invented.

But here's the rub. A 2024 systematic review in the National Library of Medicine looking at supplements for ADHD found that L-theanine improved sleep efficiency but not other sleep parameters, caffeine showed no significant benefits (though combining it with L-theanine may help attention), Bacopa monnieri improved inattention and hyperactivity, and Ginkgo biloba reduced symptoms but less effectively than methylphenidate. That's the *honest* state of the literature on the better-studied nootropic ingredients — modest, mixed, never as strong as actual medication. And Stasis's specific combination of ingredients hasn't been published in any peer-reviewed trial.

What about a study on Stasis itself? There is one — and this is the part the marketing leans on heavily — but it's still ongoing. It's a hybrid, two-arm, triple-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized trial evaluating Stasis's effect on oxidative stress, cortisol, and stimulant side effects over three months in adults, and as of 2026 it's still recruiting participants. So when a Stasis ad says "clinically studied," they're partially leaning on the existence of this trial and partially on the individual-ingredient studies — but no published outcome data on the actual Stasis product exists yet. That's a meaningful distinction. The trial *being designed properly* (triple-blind, placebo-controlled) is a good sign that the company isn't avoiding scrutiny, but you can't credit it with a result it hasn't produced.

A few independent reviewers have actually run the product for extended periods. One ran Stasis for 42 consecutive days as a self-test and concluded the gains could've been matched by "a single shot of espresso and a magnesium tablet," and raised two specific concerns worth knowing: high-dose pyridoxine (B6) can induce peripheral neuropathy in susceptible individuals — they had toe tingling that resolved after halving the dose — and repeated high-dose L-theanine may flatten natural dopamine peaks over time, especially in users who avoid caffeine. The B6 issue is a known, documented phenomenon in supplement medicine; it's not paranoia. Worth checking the label dosage against the upper tolerable intake (100mg/day) before long-term use.

So the honest verdict, stripped of hype on either side. Stasis is a legitimate product made by a real company with a real (in-progress) clinical trial, a transparent ingredient panel at non-laughable doses, third-party testing, and a coherent theoretical mechanism — supporting cortisol regulation and dopamine-system recovery around stimulant use. It's not snake oil in the Lipo Gummies sense. But the claim "research supports it" is doing heavy lifting. The research supports the individual ingredients in isolation, mostly at modest effect sizes, and the specific Stasis formula has no published outcome data yet. If you're on a stimulant and the afternoon crash or sleep disruption is wrecking you, Stasis is plausibly worth trying, but the same logic suggests you could build a similar stack cheaper from individual ashwagandha + L-theanine + magnesium glycinate + melatonin and get most of the value. The convenience and the curated dosing is what you're paying the premium for.

One last thing worth saying directly. Anything you stack with Adderall is something to mention to whoever prescribes it, because adaptogens and B-vitamins can interact with how stimulants feel and how your body processes them — not usually dangerously, but enough that your prescriber should know.
