The distinction matters.

The sharpener has two components: convex carbide slots that grind a fresh edge on straight blades, and a **ceramic honing wheel** that refines edges. It hones serrations rather than fully re-grinding them. You run the serrated edge along the ceramic wheel, which refreshes the bite on each tooth and removes the burr without reshaping the scallops.

This is an important limitation to understand. A serrated knife's teeth are individually ground bevels, and properly sharpening them means working each gullet separately — something the Kitchen Edge's fixed honing wheel can only do at a maintenance level. Sharpening a serrated knife is different than sharpening a traditional straight blade because each serration needs to be sharpened separately. For a real restoration, Work Sharp's own guidance is to use a ceramic hone, apply firm pressure perpendicular to the cutting edge, feel for a raised burr on the backside of the blade, and work one serration at a time — sharpening only the ground (beveled) side, then lightly removing the burr from the flat back.

So in practice: the Kitchen Edge will keep an already-decent serrated bread knife biting nicely with quick passes on the ceramic wheel, but it won't rescue a badly dulled or damaged serrated edge. For that you'd want a dedicated tapered ceramic rod (or Work Sharp's belt-driven models like the Ken Onion Edition) that lets you address each tooth.

For your bread and serrated slicers, treat it as upkeep — hone often, expect it to maintain rather than rebuild.
