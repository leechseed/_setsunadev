 I'm pretty sure the concept the user is asking about is the Overton Window—it matches their description exactly. It's a model showing what ideas and policies the mainstream considers acceptable, with positions becoming progressively more extreme and socially unacceptable the further you move from the center of that window.That's the **Overton Window**.

It's a model of which ideas are politically and socially acceptable to discuss publicly at a given time. Picture a spectrum (often drawn as a bell curve of public opinion): the center holds what's currently considered normal "policy," and as you move outward the labels shift to popular → sensible → acceptable → radical → unthinkable. The further an idea sits from the center, the more "edgy" or fringe it reads, and the more social cost there is to voicing it.

Two things Asmongold tends to emphasize when he brings it up:

- **The window moves.** What's unthinkable in one decade can become mainstream in the next (and vice versa). Culture, media, and repeated exposure drag the window in a direction over time.
- **You can shift it on purpose.** By voicing a more extreme position, you make a slightly-less-extreme position look moderate by comparison — so the "acceptable" zone gets pulled your way. That's why he often frames online discourse, controversy, and "saying the quiet part loud" as window-moving tactics.

The term comes from Joseph Overton, a policy analyst at the Mackinac Center, who developed it in the 1990s as a way to describe which policies a politician could realistically support without seeming too extreme to get elected.

One note: people sometimes blend it with the **IQ bell curve / "midwit" meme**, which is a different graph. If the version you're picturing has Wojaks at the low and high ends agreeing with each other, that's the midwit meme — not the Overton Window.
