## 🤖 Claude  <sub>2026-08-05 20:09:33</sub>

The field is **sociolinguistics** — specifically a newer branch called **internet linguistics** or digital sociolinguistics. Best accessible entry point is Gretchen McCulloch's book *Because Internet* (2019), which treats informal internet writing as a legit linguistic system with its own grammar rather than "broken English."

But here's the intellectually honest core: most of what gets labeled "Gen Z slang" is actually **AAVE** (African American Vernacular English). "Ion," "finna," "no cap," "fr," "deadass," "gng," "twin," "asl" — that's a documented dialect with consistent grammatical rules, studied for decades by linguists like William Labov, Lisa Green, and Geneva Smitherman. TikTok just accelerated its diffusion into the mainstream, often stripped of attribution — which linguists study too, under concepts like "crossing" and appropriation.

A few concepts that map exactly onto what we were doing:

- **Eye dialect** — deliberate misspellings ("da," "wat," "kno") that signal casualness and in-group identity, not ignorance
- **Enregisterment** (Asif Agha) — how a bundle of features becomes recognizable as a distinct "style" tied to a social type. "Gen Z brainrot" is a textbook enregistered style
- **Stance and affect** — the nonchalant pivot is a performance of detachment; you signal you care by aggressively pretending you don't
- **Emoji pragmatics** — 💀🥀🧍 function as tone markers, doing the work punctuation and body language do elsewhere

So short answer: sociolinguistics is the field, McCulloch is your gateway drug, and the deeper rabbit hole is AAVE scholarship.
