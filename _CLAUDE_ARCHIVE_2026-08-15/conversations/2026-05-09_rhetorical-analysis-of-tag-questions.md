Let me break down the moves:

**The tag question ("wouldn't it?")** is the load-bearing device. Tag questions don't seek information — they solicit agreement. They convert an assertion into a near-frictionless invitation to co-sign. Saying "yes" is the path of least resistance; saying "no" requires the listener to push back against a frame that's already been gently installed. This is one of the oldest persuasion mechanisms in conversational rhetoric — sometimes called the *yes-ladder* or *coercive co-signing*.

**The subjunctive ("it would be")** keeps the speaker's hands clean. They aren't claiming public talking *is* uncomfortable — only that it *would be*, hypothetically. This lowers the cost of the claim while still planting it. You can't easily attack a hypothetical the way you can attack an assertion.

**The dummy subject "it"** is doing presuppositional work. There's no real referent — "it" smuggles in a whole scenario as if it's already on the table. Compare: "*Talking* to people in public would be uncomfortable" (foregrounded, debatable) vs. "*it* would be uncomfortable *talking* to people in public" (the discomfort is the topic; the activity is demoted to a participial afterthought). The frame is locked before the listener registers it.

**The generic plural "people"** universalizes. Not "those people," not "strangers," not "the crowd at the event" — just *people*. Abstractions are harder to contest than specifics.

**Affective loading ("uncomfortable")** is pure pathos. There's no logos here at all — no reason given, no premise stated. The whole sentence runs on emotional resonance and presumed shared experience.

**Ethos through implied vulnerability.** The speaker positions themselves as someone who feels the same thing the listener (presumably) feels. It's a bid for solidarity. Agreeing isn't just intellectual — it's relational.

**Strategic depersonalization.** Notice the speaker doesn't say "*I'd* be uncomfortable." That would expose them as the source of the feeling. By making it "it would be uncomfortable," they generalize their private state into a quasi-universal truth, then ask you to ratify it.

**What's the purpose?** A few possibilities, in order of charity:
1. Phatic — social bonding through assumed shared experience.
2. Pre-justification — softening the ground before declining to do the thing.
3. Frame-testing — checking whether the listener shares the speaker's worldview.
4. Soft manipulation — getting buy-in for an avoidance the speaker has already chosen.

The skeleton key here: **assertion disguised as invitation.** That's a huge category of everyday rhetoric, and once you spot the tag question + subjunctive + vague referent combo, you'll see it everywhere — in sales, dating, political messaging, parenting, and your own mouth.
