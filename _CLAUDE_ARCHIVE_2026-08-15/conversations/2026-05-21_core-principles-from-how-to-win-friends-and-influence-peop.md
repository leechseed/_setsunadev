11. Never tell someone they're wrong
- Definition: Saying "you're wrong" is a direct attack on their intelligence and judgment. Even if they ARE wrong, this approach shuts down their willingness to consider your view.
- Example: Not "No, that's wrong, it actually works like X" but "I might be missing something - I'd thought of it as X. What's the angle I'm not seeing?"

12. If you're wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically
- Definition: When you beat yourself to the punch on admitting error, you disarm the other person. There's nothing left for them to attack. Plus it builds enormous trust.
- Example: "You know what, I was completely wrong about that. I should have caught it. My bad." - Said before they even finish making their point.

13. Begin in a friendly way
- Definition: A drop of honey catches more flies than a gallon of gall. Coming in hot makes people defensive immediately. Start warm, and the conversation goes better even when you have hard things to say.
- Example: Not "We need to talk about your performance" but "Hey, glad I caught you - got a minute? I want to think through something with you."

14. Get them saying "yes, yes" early
- Definition: Start with points of agreement. Once someone is in "yes" mode, they tend to keep agreeing. Once they say "no," pride locks them in.
- Example: "We both want this project to land well, right?" "Yeah." "And we agreed the deadline matters." "Yeah." Then introduce the hard ask.

15. Let them do most of the talking
- Definition: People convince themselves better when they're the ones speaking. Listening more than talking gives them space to work through their own thinking and makes them feel heard.
- Example: After your opening, ask: "What's your take on it?" Then shut up. Actually shut up. Don't fill the silence.

16. Let them feel the idea is theirs
- Definition: People are far more committed to ideas they think they originated. Plant the seed, let them grow it.
- Example: Not "We should do X" but "I wonder if there's a way to make this hit harder... what do you think would work?" - and if they land near your idea, "Oh that's good, run with that."

17. Try honestly to see it from their point of view
- Definition: Before responding to someone, genuinely try to understand their perspective first. This isn't about agreeing with them—it's about actually grasping why they think what they think.
- Example: Before reacting to a coworker's complaint, put yourself in their shoes: "If I were working their schedule and dealing with their constraints, would I feel the same way?"

18. Appeal to nobler motives
- Definition: People have two reasons for doing things: the one that sounds good and the real one. Appeal to their better self, and they'll often rise to meet it.
- Example: Not "I need this favor because I'm desperate" but "You've always been the kind of person who shows up when it matters—this is one of those times."

19. Dramatize your ideas
- Definition: Facts alone don't stick. You need to make ideas vivid and tangible, show the reality rather than just stating it.
- Example: Not "We waste a lot of time on food prep" but literally timing it in front of them: "Watch this. From ticket to plate—go."

20. Throw down a challenge
- Definition: Get things done by tapping into the desire to excel and prove oneself, not just the desire for money.
- Example: "I bet you can't turn the kitchen over in under 20 minutes" or challenging the team: "We did 80 covers last week—can we hit 90 tonight?"

**LEADING WITHOUT RESENTMENT** (How to change people without offending or arousing resentment)

21. Begin with praise and honest appreciation
- Definition: Ground criticism in genuine recognition first. It softens the message and shows you see the whole person, not just their mistakes.
- Example: "You've been killing it on prep all month, and I want to say that first. There's one thing on plating I want to dial in with you though..."

22. Call attention to mistakes indirectly
- Definition: Use "and" instead of "but"—"but" erases everything before it. Indirect framing lets people keep their dignity while still getting the message.
- Example: Not "You did great on prep, BUT plating is sloppy" → "You did great on prep, and..."

23. Talk about your own mistakes first
- Definition: When correcting someone, admit your own similar failures first. It puts you on equal footing instead of positioning yourself above them.
- Example: "When I started here I was burning sauces constantly—took weeks to dial in the heat. So I get it. Here's what finally clicked for me..."

24. Ask questions instead of giving direct orders
- Definition: Questions invite cooperation while orders invite pushback. Questions also let people keep their agency and contribute their own thinking.
- Example: Not "Rest..." but asking them to think through the solution themselves.

25. Let the other person save face
- Definition: Even when someone clearly messed up, give them a way to walk away with dignity intact. The damage from humiliation costs far more than whatever you gain.
- Example: Not calling out the mistake directly, but finding a graceful way to address it.

26. Praise incremental progress immediately
- Definition: Specific, timely recognition of small improvements motivates people more than waiting for complete transformation. Catch them doing things right.
- Example: "That plate just now - the sauce work was clean. That's the level."

27. Give them a reputation to live up to
- Definition: Treat people as if they're already the version of themselves you want them to become. They'll naturally rise to meet that expectation.
- Example: "You're one of the most reliable guys back here, that's why I'm asking you to handle this" - even if they're not quite there yet, they'll start working toward it.

28. Frame problems as fixable
- Definition: When something feels too difficult to correct, people stop trying. Making the solution seem achievable keeps them engaged and motivated.
