**Subspace, what you experienced:**

The dissociative-blissful-fully-present paradox you're naming isn't actually a paradox neurologically. Subspace sits at an intersection of altered states that resembles both deep meditation and dissociative trance, but with the body fully online and registering sensation. The acid comparison is apt — psychedelics and subspace both produce what's called *ego dissolution lite*: the observer-self gets quieter, the boundary between self and experience thins, and sensation becomes the entire field rather than something happening *to* you.

The sine wave is real and has a name in the literature: it tracks endorphin and endocannabinoid release cycles. Pain or intensity triggers a cascade, you ride the wave up into the dissociated/blissful zone, the chemistry plateaus and starts to clear, you surface into lucidity ("oh, I'm here, I'm a person, this is happening"), and then the next stimulus pushes another release and you're back under. The "captivated by what you're seeing, acceptance, this is what life is right now" — that's the ventral vagal/parasympathetic flip that happens when the body decides it's safe to surrender. Fight-or-flight gives way to what's sometimes called *tonic immobility* in trauma literature, but in a consensual, regulated context it becomes its surrender-bliss cousin.

**The neurochem during the high:**
- **Endorphins** — your body's opioid response to intense sensation. This is the primary driver of the analgesia and euphoria. Same family as morphine, produced endogenously.
- **Endocannabinoids** — anandamide especially. Responsible for the "this is fine, this is beautiful, time is weird" quality. The runner's high molecule.
- **Dopamine** — anticipation and reward, but more modulated than in top space. Dopamine in subs tends to spike around moments of being seen, praised, or used well.
- **Oxytocin** — heavy. The trust-surrender loop with the dom is essentially an oxytocin feedback circuit, which is why subspace is so much harder to reach with someone you don't trust.
- **Adrenaline/cortisol** — present but converted. The arousal system fires, but because the context is safe, the body reads the activation as *thrill* rather than *threat*. This is the same mechanism behind why people pay to ride roller coasters.
- **Reduced prefrontal activity** — your executive/critical/planning brain literally goes quieter. This is the dissociative aspect, and it's why decisions made deep in subspace shouldn't be trusted as binding.

The meditation comparison you made is more than a metaphor. fMRI studies of experienced meditators in deep states and people in flow/trance show overlapping default mode network suppression. You're describing the same phenomenon — the wandering self-narrator goes quiet, and pure experience takes its place. The "measured breathing, get distracted, let it wash over you, bring it back" pattern is *exactly* what trained meditators do, and it's why subs who meditate tend to access subspace more readily and recover more cleanly.

**Sub drop, the reminder:**

Sub drop is the comedown from all of the above, and structurally it's the same event as top drop but with different texture. Where top drop tends to feel hollow, catatonic, "where did the feeling go" — sub drop tends to feel more *emotionally* loaded: weepy, clingy, ashamed, anxious, sometimes a kind of grief that doesn't have an object. Some people get physically symptomatic — chills, achiness, fatigue, like a low-grade flu. It can hit anywhere from a few hours to 2-3 days post-scene, and it sometimes blindsides people on day two when they thought they'd skipped it.

The mechanism: endorphin and oxytocin crash, cortisol spike on the back end, and — critically — a sudden return of prefrontal function. That last part matters. The critical, evaluating, narrating brain comes back online, surveys what just happened to the body, and can produce a kind of dysregulated "what was that, am I okay, do they still want me, am I a freak" cascade even when the scene was perfect and consensual. It's not a moral or rational response, it's chemistry plus a brain trying to reintegrate.

The reason you haven't experienced it in a long time is probably some combination of: not having reached the same depth of subspace recently, better integrated aftercare, or having developed a more secure base where the post-scene reintegration doesn't trigger attachment alarm. Sub drop is significantly worse when there's any ambiguity about the relational ground after the scene.

**The shape of the two drops compared:**

Top drop tends to read as *empty*. Sub drop tends to read as *raw*. Top drop's question is often "where did I go." Sub drop's question is often "am I okay, are we okay." Different chemistry routes to a similar underlying event: the nervous system trying to find baseline after being dramatically displaced from it.

The fact that you've now consciously experienced both sides of this within the same week is genuinely unusual and gives you a kind of stereoscopic view of the dynamic that most people don't get. It also means your aftercare practice needs to account for *both* — your own drop and your partners' — which is a heavier lift than people realize, especially when you're the one running point on the scene.
