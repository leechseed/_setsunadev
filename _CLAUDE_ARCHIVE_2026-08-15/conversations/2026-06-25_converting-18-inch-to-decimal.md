The Kuleshov Effect is a film editing technique and psychological phenomenon discovered/popularized by Soviet film director Lev Kuleshov in the 1920s. 

The basic setup was:
1. A neutral face (an actor with no expression)
2. Followed by different images (like a baby in a coffin, a bowl of soup, a child playing with a doll, etc.)
3. When viewers saw the neutral face followed by these images, they interpreted the actor's expression differently depending on what image came after

So if the neutral face was followed by a sad image (like a dead baby), viewers thought the actor looked sad. If followed by food/soup, they thought he looked hungry. If followed by a child playing, they thought he looked happy or amused.

This demonstrates that emotion perception is influenced by context, not just the face itself.You're thinking of the **Kuleshov Effect**! 

It's a classic film study from the 1920s by Soviet filmmaker Lev Kuleshov. Here's how it worked:

1. **Show a neutral face** — an actor with no clear expression
2. **Follow it with different images** — like a dead baby, a bowl of soup, a child playing, a coffin, cake, etc.
3. **Viewers perceive different emotions** — even though it's the exact same face every time

So the same neutral expression looks "sad" when followed by a crying baby, "hungry" when followed by soup, and "happy" when followed by a child playing.

**The key insight:** People don't interpret emotion from the face alone—they read it based on **context**. The surrounding images influence how viewers perceive the actor's expression, even though the face is identical.

It's a brilliant demonstration of how much our perception is shaped by narrative context rather than what we're actually seeing. Kuleshov used this to show the power of editing in film, and it's still used in psychology and filmmaking to explain how much of emotion recognition is about the bigger picture.
