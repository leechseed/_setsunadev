"That line" hits harder than "this line" because it creates rhetorical distance — it frames the thing as something already established, already out there carrying weight, like you're pointing at it from across the room and letting the audience sit with it. "This" feels like you're still holding it. "That" feels like you already dropped it and it landed.

It's the difference between "this is a problem" (presenting) and "that is a problem" (declaring). "That" has a finality to it, almost confrontational — it says *you already know what I'm talking about.*

## 🤖 Claude  <sub>2026-04-04 02:58:32</sub>

The user is asking about whether "that" and "this" are bad words to use in essays. This is a common writing advice topic. The advice they're referring to is about vague demonstrative pronouns — when "this" or "that" is used without a clear noun after it, it creates ambiguity. But it's not a blanket ban.You're thinking of **vague demonstrative pronouns** — and the advice is real, but more nuanced than a blanket ban.

The problem isn't the words themselves. It's when they float without a noun anchoring them. "This is important" forces the reader to look backward and figure out what "this" refers to. In a long or complex paragraph, that ambiguity kills momentum.

The fix is simple — just attach a noun. "This *pattern* is important." "That *contradiction* undermines the argument." Now the pronoun is doing work instead of gesturing vaguely at the last three sentences.

So in strong academic or persuasive writing, you'll still see "this" and "that" everywhere — they just have a buddy. "This trend," "that claim," "this distinction." It keeps your reader locked in instead of squinting backward trying to figure out what you meant.

The real words to worry about in essays are filler and hedge words — "very," "really," "just," "things," "stuff," "a lot." Those are the ones that quietly drain your writing of authority.
