# How to Win Friends & Influence People — Principles Reference

A practical refresher on Dale Carnegie's core principles, with plain definitions and example phrasings for each.

**The one-sentence compression:**
People are driven by their own sense of importance. Make them feel it — genuinely — and engage their ego instead of fighting it.

## Part 1: Fundamental Techniques in Handling People

### 1. Don't criticize, condemn, or complain

**What it means:** Criticism doesn't fix behavior — it puts people on the defensive and makes them justify themselves harder. It wounds pride and breeds resentment, even when you're objectively right. The person walks away convinced *you're* the problem.

**Instead of:** "You completely screwed up that ticket."
**Try:** "Hey, that ticket got out of pocket — let's reset for the next one."

---

### 2. Give honest, sincere appreciation

**What it means:** Every person craves feeling important. Sincere appreciation — *specific, observed, true* — is one of the most powerful things you can give. The keyword is **sincere**. Flattery is transparent and people smell it instantly.

**Instead of:** "Good job today!" (generic, hollow)
**Try:** "The way you handled that rush when we were down two guys — you didn't break stride. That's not normal."

---

### 3. Arouse in the other person an eager want

**What it means:** People act on *their* motivations, not yours. To influence anyone, frame what you want in terms of what *they* want. Show them how getting you what you need also gets them what they want.

**Instead of:** "I need you to cover Saturday."
**Try:** "You've been saying you want more weekend hours for tips — Saturday's open, you want it?"

## Part 2: How to Make People Like You

### 4. Become genuinely interested in other people

**What it means:** You make more friends in two months being interested in others than in two years trying to make others interested in you. Remember what people tell you. Ask about it later. Pay attention to their life like it matters — because to them, it does.

**Try:** "Last time you mentioned your kid had surgery coming up — how'd that go?"

---

### 5. Smile

**What it means:** A real smile communicates "I'm glad to see you" faster than any sentence can. Costs nothing, changes the chemistry of every interaction. The key is *real* — forced smiles read as creepy. Think of something that actually makes you glad to see this person before you look up.

**Try:** Just do it. When you walk into the kitchen, when you greet a customer, when someone gets to the table. Real one.

---

### 6. Remember and use their name

**What it means:** A person's name is, to them, the sweetest sound in any language. Using it signals they matter as an individual, not as "hey man" or "buddy."

**Instead of:** "Hey man, good to see you."
**Try:** "Hey James, good to see you." And use it again later: "That's a good call, James."

---

### 7. Be a good listener — encourage others to talk about themselves

**What it means:** People love talking about themselves and find a good listener fascinating. The skill is asking questions that open them up — then actually shutting up and paying attention.

**Try:** "How'd you end up in this line of work?" → then follow up: "What was that transition like?" Don't wait for your turn to talk. Actually listen.

---

### 8. Talk in terms of the other person's interests

**What it means:** Find what they care about and steer there. People are pulled toward those who meet them on their own ground.

**Try:** If you know they're deep into Magic: The Gathering — "Did you see the new set? What's the meta looking like?" If they're into cars, lead with cars. Their territory, not yours.

---

### 9. Make the other person feel important — and do it sincerely

**What it means:** The deepest principle in human nature is the craving to be appreciated. Find what's genuinely admirable about someone and acknowledge it openly. Not flattery — recognition.

**Try:** "I always come to you for this kind of question because you actually know what you're talking about — what's your read?"

## Part 3: How to Win People to Your Way of Thinking

### 10. The only way to win an argument is to avoid it

**What it means:** Even if you "win" by logic, you lose — the other person feels humiliated, resents you, and doesn't actually change their mind. They just stop arguing with you out loud.

**Try:** When someone says something you disagree with, instead of countering — "Interesting take. What got you there?" Or sometimes just let it go.

---

### 11. Show respect for the other person's opinion — never say "you're wrong"

**What it means:** "You're wrong" is a direct attack on their intelligence. Even if they *are* wrong, you've now made them defend their position out of pride. You'll never convince anyone this way.

**Instead of:** "No, that's wrong, it works like X."
**Try:** "I might be missing something — I'd thought of it as X. What's the angle I'm not seeing?"

---

### 12. If you're wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically

**What it means:** Beat them to it. When you admit error before they can attack, you disarm them completely. There's nothing left for them to push against. It also builds massive trust over time.

**Try:** "You know what — I was completely wrong about that. I should've caught it. My bad." Said *before* they finish their point.

---

### 13. Begin in a friendly way

**What it means:** A drop of honey catches more flies than a gallon of gall. Coming in hot makes people defensive instantly, and you've lost the conversation before it started. Warm opening, even when you have hard things to say.

**Instead of:** "We need to talk about your performance."
**Try:** "Hey, glad I caught you — you got a minute? Want to think through something with you."

---

### 14. Get the other person saying "yes, yes" immediately

**What it means:** Open with points of agreement. Once someone is in "yes" mode, momentum keeps them agreeing. Once they hit "no," pride locks them in and reversal is nearly impossible.

**Try:** "We both want this project to land, right?" → "Yeah." → "And we agreed the deadline matters?" → "Yeah." → *Now* introduce the harder thing.

---

### 15. Let the other person do a great deal of the talking

**What it means:** People convince themselves better than you can convince them. Let them lay out their thinking. You'll learn what actually matters to them, and they'll feel heard, which is half the battle.

**Try:** After your opening — "What's your take on it?" Then shut up. Don't fill silence. Let them keep going.

---

### 16. Let the other person feel the idea is theirs

**What it means:** People are far more committed to ideas they think they came up with. Plant the seed, then step back and let them grow it. Take none of the credit.

**Instead of:** "We should do X."
**Try:** "I wonder if there's a way to make this hit harder… what do you think would work?" — and when they land near your idea: "Oh that's good, run with that."

---

### 17. Try honestly to see things from the other person's point of view

**What it means:** Before responding, *actually* try to understand why a reasonable person would hold their position. Not as a tactic — as real empathy. This alone solves most conflicts.

**Try:** Before you react to a coworker's complaint, ask yourself: "If I were them, working their shifts, dealing with what they deal with — would I be saying the same thing?"

---

### 18. Be sympathetic with the other person's ideas and desires

**What it means:** Even if you disagree, acknowledge that what they want makes sense to them. Don't dismiss their feelings — meet them. "I'd feel the same way if I were in your spot" is one of the most disarming sentences in the language.

**Try:** "I don't blame you for being pissed about that — I'd be pissed too. Let's figure out what we do from here."

---

### 19. Appeal to the nobler motives

**What it means:** People have two reasons for what they do — the one that *sounds* good, and the real one. Appeal to their better self, and they'll often rise to live up to it.

**Instead of:** "I need this favor because I'm desperate."
**Try:** "You've always been the kind of person who shows up when it actually matters — and this is one of those times."

---

### 20. Dramatize your ideas

**What it means:** Plain statements don't move people. Make it vivid. Visual. Show, don't tell. Stage it.

**Instead of:** "We waste a lot of prep time."
**Try:** Slap a stopwatch on the counter. "Watch this. From ticket call to plated — go."

---

### 21. Throw down a challenge

**What it means:** The way to motivate people (yourself included) is to stimulate competition — not for money, but for the chance to prove something. To win. To beat the previous version.

**Try:** "I bet you can't turn the kitchen over in under 20 minutes." Or to yourself: "Last week we did 80 covers. Can we hit 90 tonight?"

## Part 4: How to Change People Without Offending or Arousing Resentment

### 22. Begin with praise and honest appreciation

**What it means:** Before you criticize, ground it in real recognition. It softens the blow and signals you see the whole person, not just the failure. They'll actually hear the critique.

**Try:** "You've been killing it on prep all month, and I want to say that first. There's one thing on plating I want to dial in with you, though…"

---

### 23. Call attention to people's mistakes indirectly

**What it means:** Use "**and**" instead of "**but**." "But" cancels everything before it ("I love the work BUT…" — they only hear what comes after). Indirect framing lets people save face and stay open.

**Instead of:** "You did great on prep, **but** plating is sloppy."
**Try:** "You did great on prep, **and** if we can sharpen the plating to that same level, we're untouchable."

---

### 24. Talk about your own mistakes before criticizing the other person

**What it means:** When you have to correct someone, lead by admitting your own similar failure first. Puts you on their level instead of above them. They'll listen instead of bristling.

**Try:** "When I started here, I was burning sauces left and right — took me weeks to dial in. So I get it. Here's what finally clicked for me…"

---

### 25. Ask questions instead of giving direct orders

**What it means:** Questions invite cooperation. Orders invite resistance. Questions also let the other person keep their agency and contribute their own thinking.

**Instead of:** "Restock the line."
**Try:** "Hey, how's the line looking — we good on stock for the rush?"

---

### 26. Let the other person save face

**What it means:** Even when someone clearly screwed up, find a way for them to walk away with dignity. The cost of humiliating someone is always higher than whatever you "win" by doing it.

**Instead of:** "You completely blew that order."
**Try:** "Tough ticket — that one was stacked. Let's reset for the next one."

---

### 27. Praise the slightest improvement and praise every improvement

**What it means:** Specific, immediate recognition of incremental progress is far more motivating than waiting for total transformation. Catch people doing things right, especially the small things.

**Try:** "That plate just now — sauce work was clean. *That's* the level."

---

### 28. Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to

**What it means:** Treat people as if they already are the version of themselves you want them to be. People rise to the label you give them.

**Try:** "You're one of the most reliable guys back here — that's why I'm putting this on you." (Even if they're not quite there yet, they'll start being.)

---

### 29. Use encouragement — make the fault seem easy to correct

**What it means:** If a gap feels impossibly large, people give up. Frame it as small, manageable, almost-there. Make them believe they can close it.

**Instead of:** "Your knife skills are way behind, you need a ton of work."
**Try:** "You're like 90% there — it's just the angle on the cut. One small tweak and you're golden."

---

### 30. Make the other person happy about doing the thing you suggest

**What it means:** Frame your ask so they see what's in it for them — growth, recognition, opportunity, status. Tie the action to something they already want.

**Instead of:** "I need you to take on this extra responsibility."
**Try:** "This is the kind of thing that gets noticed — handle this well and it puts you in a different conversation."

## The Underlying Logic

Every principle is a variation on one insight: **arguing with someone's ego is a losing game.** You can't beat their need for importance, dignity, and self-justification — those drives are stronger than any logic you bring. So instead, you align with them. You make their ego your ally rather than your enemy.

That's the whole book.
