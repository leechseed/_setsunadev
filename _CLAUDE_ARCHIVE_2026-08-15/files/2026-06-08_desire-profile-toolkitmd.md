## Part I — Foundations: How Desire Actually Works

### The one principle that runs everything: you are seeking feelings

Your turn-ons are not really about the *acts*. They're about the **feelings the acts produce**. The act is just the delivery mechanism.

This matters because feelings are portable and acts are not. A fantasy might be too extreme, too risky, or something you'd never actually want to live out — but if you know the *feeling* underneath it, you can find safer, more practical acts that produce the same feeling.

The neutral analogy: if "joy" is the feeling you're after, both a plate of cookies and time with a dog can get you there — they're interchangeable routes to the same destination. Eroticism works identically. Once you know the destination (the feeling), you get a whole menu of routes (acts, dynamics, words, clothing, settings) to reach it. That's where the freedom comes from.

So throughout this entire toolkit, every time you find a turn-on, the real question is always: **what feeling is this giving me?**

### The translation chain

This is the spine of the whole system. Everything you discover gets pushed through this chain:

```
TURN-ON              →  FEELING            →  TRIGGER             →  DELIVERY
(the surface thing)     (the real target)     (what evokes it)       (interchangeable acts
                                                                       + partner qualities)
```

- **Turn-on** — the fantasy, memory, image, or content that arouses you.
- **Feeling** — the emotional state it produces (desired, powerful, surrendered, naughty, etc.). *This is the thing you're actually chasing.*
- **Trigger** — the specific element that flips the feeling on (a setting, a power dynamic, a phrase, a particular act, something about the partner).
- **Delivery** — all the different activities **and** partner qualities that can produce that same feeling. Because there are many, you get to mix and match into new experiences.

The payoff: once you've mapped a feeling to multiple triggers and deliveries, you can **engineer** experiences. You're no longer stuck with one specific fantasy — you can recombine activities and partner qualities to hit the same emotional target in countless ways.

### The Erotic Mind (Jack Morin) — core concepts

The framework draws on Jack Morin's *The Erotic Mind*, whose central claim is that the brain — not the body — is the primary sexual organ, converting basic arousal into deep, sometimes transcendent experience via imagination, emotion, and memory. A few of his concepts are worth keeping in your back pocket as decoding tools:

**Core Erotic Theme (CET).** A personal, deeply ingrained pattern of arousal unique to you, built up over a lifetime of experiences, fantasies, and interactions starting in childhood. It's *why* certain things light you up and others do nothing. Morin's view is that the CET often takes something that was once difficult, charged, or unresolved and transforms it into a source of excitement and mastery. When you journal, you're hunting for the recurring themes that point at your CET.

**The Erotic Equation: Attraction + Obstacles = Excitement.** Morin observed that pure attraction alone is often flat; it's the presence of *obstacles* — tension, anticipation, distance, prohibition, risk, difficulty — that amplifies arousal into something intense. If something turns you on more than it "should," look for the obstacle that's charging it. (This also explains why "forbidden" or "taboo" feelings are so common and so strong.)

**The four cornerstones.** Morin identified four recurring sources that show up again and again in people's peak erotic experiences. Use them as prompts when you can't articulate *why* something is hot:
1. **Longing and anticipation** — wanting, waiting, the build-up, the chase.
2. **Violating prohibitions** — the naughty/taboo charge of doing the "not allowed."
3. **Searching for power** — having it, surrendering it, the exchange of it.
4. **Overcoming ambivalence** — the friction of resistance, hesitation, or being "convinced."

If you can spot which cornerstone(s) a given turn-on is built on, you've found a reliable lever you can pull on purpose.

### Moving beyond shame: the "button on the neck"

The reframe that makes the journaling work: imagine there were a button on the side of your neck that, when pressed, flooded you with arousal and all the feel-good chemistry of sex. Almost everyone would press it — and nobody would interrogate where the button came from or whether liking it makes them a good person. It's just a trigger that feels good.

Your turn-ons are that button. You don't have to justify them, trace their origin, or earn them. A turn-on existing in your mind does **not** obligate you to act on it. You can hold even very dark fantasies that you'd never want to live out — and understanding the *feeling* underneath them is still useful, because you can often reach that same feeling through acts you *do* want.

There is no "normal" in eroticism. Even a fetish that very few people share still puts you in a community of others who share it. You're not weird, bad, or broken — you just have a button most people don't. The whole point of this work is to find your buttons and stop apologizing for them.

## Part II — Operating Principles (Your Policy)

Treat these as the standing rules of engagement for everything below. They're what keep the work honest and safe.

1. **Feelings first, always.** Every turn-on gets decoded down to the feeling it produces. The feeling is the target; acts are interchangeable means.
2. **No judgment, no origin-hunting.** Write things down without questioning where they came from or whether you "should" like them. Analysis kills honesty.
3. **A fantasy is not a commitment.** Cataloging a desire never obligates you to act on it. Mind-only is a valid permanent home for some turn-ons.
4. **Honesty over performance.** Never record a desire because you think a partner wants you to have it. That breeds resentment. This is *yours*.
5. **Don't go zero-to-100.** You can approach an intense feeling through a milder act first (e.g., light restraint and dirty talk before anything more extreme). Dial up gradually.
6. **Consent and safety are non-negotiable** for anything you actually act on: explicit agreement, clear limits, the ability to stop, and aftercare. Knowing the *feeling* lets you choose lower-risk routes to it.
7. **The observer stance.** When shame shows up, step "above" yourself and study your eroticism like a foreign object you're curious about. Detachment dissolves shame; you can re-engage warmly once it's named.
8. **Self-compassion throughout.** Celebrate your eroticism as a real part of your identity, not a defect to manage.
9. **It's a living document.** Desires and limits change. Revisit every ~6 months and update.

### Step 1 — The turn-on inventory

- **Thoughts / mental images** — the stuff that drifts through your head during the day, and what's running through your mind during sex.

### Step 2 — Name the feeling

Take one item at a time. Visualize it in detail — the sensory and emotional texture, not just the mechanics. Then ask: **how does this make me feel? What emotions does it evoke?**

These feelings are the whole point — they're what you're actually seeking. A capture/takedown CNC fantasy, for instance, might deliver feelings reachable through light bondage plus heavy dirty talk — same emotional hit, far lower risk. You rarely have to go full-force into a fantasy unless you want to.

### Step 3 — Find the trigger

For that same item, ask: **what specifically is making this arousing?** Look at:

- The **environment / setting**
- **Who** you're with and their attributes
- The **sexual acts** occurring
- Anything else giving it the charge (a phrase, a power move, an article of clothing, a moment of anticipation)

Then widen out: **what *other* activities or partner attributes would trigger this same feeling**, even though they're not in the original image? This is where the menu of options starts to open up.

Repeat Steps 2–3 for every item on your Step 1 list.

### Step 4 — Build out activities + partner qualities

These two columns are the engine of the whole system, so give them real attention. For each core feeling you've identified:

- **Activities that trigger it** — every act, scenario, or situation (sexual *or* not) where you feel this. List them all.
- **Partner qualities that trigger it** — specific things about a partner that flip this feeling on: how they look, dress, talk, move, the personality they project, particular actions they take. (If submission is your thing, an obviously dominant partner triggers it — but get specific about *which* dominant behaviors do it for you.)

Once you have rich lists here, you can **recombine** activities and partner qualities into entirely new experiences that all chase the same feeling. That's the world of eroticism opening up.

## Part V — Tool 3: The Sexual Disclosure

The most intense tool, and optional — but powerful. You enter a safe space with someone you deeply trust and disclose your entire sexual inner world out loud. It does two things at once: it surfaces things you didn't even know were in your own head, and it *trains your capacity to be vulnerable* about sex.

> **Do this only with the right person.** A partner or someone you've built a genuinely safe, non-judgmental container with — and if no one fits that bill, a **therapist or coach**. The risk of the exercise is being judged; if it goes badly you'll never want to do it again, so protect the conditions.

### The three disclosure domains

Set aside an unhurried block (an hour or more) and disclose:

1. **Your sexual history** — past partners and experiences, and crucially *what came up for you* in each: the feelings and thoughts around them.
2. **Your desires** — your turn-ons, kinks, and fantasies (this is where the journal pays off — you'll have language ready).
3. **Your shame and guilt** — anything you carry around past partners, past experiences, or your turn-ons themselves.

### How the listener should hold it

Their job is to be a **mirror, not a mechanic.** They are not there to fix you, advise you, or react — they're there to listen and reflect back what they heard: the turn-ons you named, the shame you named. That reflection is what makes the hidden stuff visible and starts melting the shame.

### Why it works — the reference experience

You'll go through something big, especially the first time. Afterward you may feel a wave of anxiety, shame, or "I can't believe I said that." And then: you'll go home, eat dinner, wake up the next day, and the world won't have ended. You didn't die. That becomes a **reference experience** — proof that sharing this part of yourself is survivable. From then on, sharing bits and pieces with people you want to be close to gets dramatically easier, with far less shame attached. ("I shared this once and they didn't run — it'll be okay to share it again.")

### Disclosure prep checklist

- [ ] Chosen a person who is trustworthy **and** non-judgmental (or booked a therapist/coach)
- [ ] Set the container explicitly: confidentiality, no judgment, no fixing — just listening + mirroring
- [ ] Blocked an hour+ with no time pressure
- [ ] Brought my journal language so I'm not searching for words cold
- [ ] Planned aftercare for *myself* (the post-vulnerability wobble is normal — line up something grounding for after)
- [ ] Journaled the aftermath afterward (what surfaced that I didn't expect?)

## Part VI — Tool 4: The Sex Menu (Yes / No / Maybe)

This is where discovery becomes communication. A sex menu is a long structured list of sexual and BDSM acts, each with a **rating** for how into it you are and a **notes** field for nuance. (Also called a Yes/No/Maybe list or desire menu.) It's usually a spreadsheet so it can be filtered and shared.

It's two tools in one: a **discovery** tool (filling it out, you'll hit acts you've never heard of — look them up, and some will surprise you into "oh… *oh*, that's hot") and a **communication/consent** tool (your partner gets a precise map of what to do, what not to do, and where your boundaries are).

### What makes it powerful

- **Ratings, not just yes/no.** Most of the erotic world lives in gray areas. A good menu uses a scale, e.g.: `Need it · Love it · Occasionally · Try it · Indifferent · Soft limit · Hard limit`.
- **Reference notes per act.** For each item, write what it means *to you* — how you want it done, why you like or dislike it, any conditions. The nuance is where the real communication happens.
- **A top section** for the essentials: the **emotions you want to feel** during sex (pull these straight from Tool 1), plus anything that frames the whole thing.
- **A bottom section** for medical conditions, hard limits, and acts *not* on the list you'd want to add.

### How to do it

1. **Fill it out separately** from your partner, then share. Do your own fully and honestly *before* seeing theirs — it keeps you from shaping your answers to please them.
2. **Discuss with curiosity, not judgment.** For anything one of you isn't up for, get curious about the *feeling* behind it — you can often find an alternative act that delivers the same feeling. (This is the translation chain doing its job again.)
3. **Revisit every ~6 months.** Tastes shift and new boundaries form; keep the menu current as a shared reference.

**Three rules while filling it out:** be honest (you'll never get a desire met that you won't admit to); don't mark things just to please your partner (you'll resent it later); and actually have fun with it — it's a long list, but it's a map to a better sex life, not a chore.

> Rather than building a list from scratch (you don't know what you don't know), use a ready-made one. Brandon's free Google Sheets template has 350+ acts: https://www.brandonthedom.com/resources/sex-menu-template — copy it and rate away.

## Part VIII — Communicating Your Desires

Discovery is only half of it — the original problem is the blank stare when a partner asks "what do you want?" Here's how to close that gap.

**Build the container first.** Good sexual communication only happens inside openness and non-judgment. Before a real conversation, name it: *"I want to talk about what we're each into — no judgment either direction, just curiosity."* The stronger that container, the more honest both of you can be.

**Lead with the feeling, then the act.** The translation chain makes asks land softer and clearer:
> *"I've figured out that I really like [act] — what does it for me is feeling [feeling]. Would you be into trying [specific thing]?"*

**Trade, don't demand.** When a partner names something you're not up for, get curious about the feeling they're chasing and offer an alternative that hits it:
> *"I'm not into [X], but it sounds like you want to feel [feeling] — what if we tried [Y] instead?"*

**Use writing when speech is hard.** If saying it out loud kills the moment or spikes anxiety, write it — the sex menu and a shared doc let you express desires with the act held at arm's length, which makes "yes, I love this" come out without the shame.

**Normalize revisiting.** "Let's redo our menus" every six months keeps desire a living conversation instead of a one-time confession.

- **Erotic Blueprint:** Jaiya's model of five arousal "types" — **Sexual** (direct, genital, intercourse-forward), **Sensual** (full-body, ambiance, all five senses), **Kinky** (taboo, power, ritual, the psychological charge), **Energetic** (anticipation, tease, energy exchange, space between touch), **Shapeshifter** (fluent in and craving all of them).
- **Dominant / Submissive (D/s):** a consensual dynamic of leading vs. yielding control. Distinct from personality — it's a chosen erotic role.
- **Top / Bottom / Switch:** who is "doing" (top) vs. "receiving" (bottom) in a given act; a switch enjoys both and can move between them.
- **Sadism / Masochism:** deriving erotic charge from giving (sadism) or receiving (masochism) sensation/intensity, consensually.
- **Primal:** an instinct-driven, less-scripted mode of play (e.g., chasing, wrestling, biting) — raw rather than ritualized.
- **CNC (Consensual Non-Consent):** a pre-negotiated scene where partners role-play the *appearance* of non-consent while having fully agreed in advance, with safewords and limits. Can be approached gradually via milder acts that deliver the same feeling.
- **Degradation / Praise:** two opposite verbal/psychological styles — being talked down (degradation) or built up (praise) — each chasing different feelings.
- **Bondage:** consensual physical restraint; can be light/playful or elaborate.
- **Hard limit / Soft limit:** a hard limit is an absolute no; a soft limit is conditional — possible only under specific circumstances you define.
- **Aftercare:** the deliberate comedown after an intense scene — reassurance, warmth, grounding — for either or both partners.
- **Container:** the agreed conditions of safety, confidentiality, and non-judgment inside which vulnerable sexual conversation or play happens.
- **Sex Menu / Yes-No-Maybe:** a structured, rated, annotated inventory of acts used to communicate desires and limits.
