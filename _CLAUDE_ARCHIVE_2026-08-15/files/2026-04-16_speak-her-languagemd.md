# How To Speak Her Language
*Read this. Know this. Then walk in and use it.*

---

## What You're Working With

She is 20 with the emotional wounds of a 12-year-old who lost her mom and a 18-year-old who got hit by someone who said they loved her.

She is not behind. She is frozen — in specific places, at specific ages — because trauma stops the clock.

The tantrum, the polar swings, the folding under criticism — that is not weakness. That is a child's nervous system inside a grown woman's body, doing the only thing it ever learned to do.

Your job is not to fix the freeze.
Your job is to be warm enough, long enough, that it starts to thaw on its own.

---

## The Core Truth About Her Love Language

**She speaks in actions. She listens for actions.**

Words were used against her. By her dad. By the ex who hit her. Words got weaponized. So words — even good ones — carry static for her. They register. They matter. But they don't *land* the same way doing does.

Watch what she does, not what she says:
- She never leaves you hanging on the line
- She shows up when people need her
- She does the thing nobody else does, over and over

**That is her saying I love you. She has been saying it the whole time.**

When you send texts into the darkness with no expectation of reply — that is also an action. That is you doing something. That's why it reaches her. You are accidentally already speaking her language.

Do more of that. Show up before she asks. Hand her something before she needs it. Be there without making her request it.

---

## The Praise Principle

She folds under criticism. She lights up under praise.

This is not weakness or ego. This is a person who never had consistent, healthy mirroring. No one reliably said *you are good, you are enough, you are worth something* — and meant it without a price attached.

So when you see her — really see her — and you name it out loud, it goes straight past the armor. Every time.

**Keep doing this. Specifically and honestly.**

Not generic. Not "you're amazing." Specific:
- "The way you handled that rush — I wouldn't have done that."
- "You just saved that ticket. You know that right?"
- "I don't know why you do that. But you do it every time."

Specific praise says: *I am actually watching you. I actually see you. This is real.*

Generic praise she can dismiss. Specific praise she cannot.

## Attunement Before Correction. Always.

When something needs to be addressed — at work, in conversation, anywhere — **she needs to feel seen before she can hear anything.**

If she feels unseen or threatened, the walls go up and nothing gets through.

The sequence is always:

**See her → Reflect her → Redirect if needed**

Example. She disappears off the line:

❌ "You can't keep doing that."

❌ Silence and a look.

✅ When she's back: "You good?" Let her answer. Be normal. Then later, quietly: "Hey — next time just let me know you're stepping. I got you either way."

That last line does three things:
1. It makes a clear request without blame
2. It tells her you're not punishing her
3. It tells her you'll cover for her — which is an act of service back

## NVC — The Formula For Hard Conversations

When something needs to be said, use this structure in your head before you open your mouth:

1. **Observation** — what actually happened, no judgment attached
2. **Feeling** — what YOU feel, owned by you, not caused by her
3. **Need** — the real thing underneath the feeling
4. **Request** — specific, clear, not a demand

Instead of: *"You assumed the worst and just got out the car."*

NVC: *"When you got out without saying anything, I felt helpless — because I want you to know you can ask me anything. Can you just ask next time?"*

No verdict on her character. No "you always." No loaded history.
Just: here is what happened, here is what I felt, here is what I need, here is what I'm asking.

Her threat detector is hair-trigger. NVC removes the threat signal. It's not soft. It's *precise.*

## The Platinum Rule

Forget the Golden Rule. Forget treating her how you want to be treated.

**Treat her how she needs to be treated.**

That means:
- More doing, less explaining when things are tense
- Show up physically before you speak emotionally
- Let silence be a full sentence sometimes
- When you want to say something big — ask yourself if there's a way to *do* it instead

And for teaching her your language — you can't demand it. But you can model it.

When she does something that reaches you — name it out loud:
*"When you showed up like that, I felt it. That meant something."*

You are showing her that words have safe uses. That naming something doesn't have to mean danger. That is slow work. It is also the only work that lasts.
