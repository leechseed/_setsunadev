## Executive Summary

This report proposes and validates the **Trauma Cardinal Vector Model (TCVM)** — a geometric, dimensional framework for mapping the type, source, and severity of traumatic experience along four orthogonal cardinal vectors radiating from a central origin point.

The model operationalizes a key insight from complex trauma literature: trauma is not monolithic. It varies by *type of wound* (omission vs. commission), *relational context* (caregiver vs. event), and *developmental impact*. The TCVM captures this variance spatially, treating each dimension of traumatic experience as a vector with both **direction** (the nature of the wound) and **magnitude** (severity, duration, developmental timing).

The four proposed cardinals are:

| Cardinal | Archetype | Wound Type |
|---|---|---|
| **Neglect** | The Empty | Omission — deprivation of essential care |
| **Abandonment** | The Unwanted | Relational rejection — being discarded or unseen |
| **Violation** | The Shattered | Commission — active harm, overwhelm, direct trauma |
| **Betrayal** | The Unmoored | Trust rupture by a responsible attachment figure |

A person's trauma is plotted as a point on each vector. When connected, these four points form a **Trauma Profile Polygon** — a unique shape that represents their traumatic landscape and can guide intervention.

## The Design Pattern

The TCVM borrows its structural logic from established dimensional design patterns in both psychology and data modeling:

**Radar/Spider Charts** are the closest visual analogue — used in personality assessment (Big Five), sports analytics, and medical profiling. Each axis is independent; the resulting polygon visualizes multi-dimensional data at a glance.

**Cardinal Orthogonality** is the key constraint: each vector must represent a *meaningfully distinct* dimension of traumatic experience. They should not be reducible to each other, and they should collectively account for the major categories of psychological wounding documented in the clinical literature.

**Magnitude as Radial Distance** means that moving outward from the origin along any vector represents increasing severity. This is not binary (trauma/no trauma) but continuous — reflecting the ACE (Adverse Childhood Experiences) research finding that trauma operates on a dose-response curve: more exposure, more severe outcomes.

**The Origin = Regulated Baseline.** The center is not "happiness" or "health" — it is the *theoretically unexposed self*. No vector has been activated. This is a reference state, not a destination, and it avoids the model making normative claims about what a "good" life looks like.

## The Four Cardinals

### 3.1 Neglect

> *"The wound of the empty"* — trauma by omission

**Definition:** The sustained absence of physical or emotional provision that the developing person requires for survival and healthy development. Neglect is a failure to act, not an act itself. This makes it the least visible and, per the literature, often the most prevalent cardinal.

**What it encompasses:**
- Physical neglect (food, shelter, medical care, supervision)
- Emotional neglect (absence of attunement, warmth, mirroring, comfort)
- Cognitive neglect (failure to stimulate, educate, or recognize developmental needs)

**Why it is a distinct cardinal:** The Draijer (2003) dimensional model specifically separates "being overwhelmed" (trauma) from "being unreflected by important others" (neglect), finding they predict distinct outcome profiles — trauma predicts PTSD, while neglect predicts personality and attachment disorders. Emotional neglect is also the single most commonly reported childhood trauma type, with 100% of complex PTSD patients in one cohort reporting it.

**Magnitude drivers:** Chronicity, developmental stage at onset, absence of compensatory attachment figures, and whether the neglect was physical, emotional, or both.

**Associated outcomes at high magnitude:** Attachment anxiety, emotional dysregulation, identity fragmentation, somatic complaints, dissociative symptoms.

---

### 3.2 Abandonment

> *"The wound of the unwanted"* — trauma by relational rupture

**Definition:** The experience — perceived or actual — of being discarded, rejected, or rendered invisible by an attachment figure or social system. Where neglect is the *absence* of care, abandonment is the *withdrawal* or *denial* of belonging. It answers the core developmental question *"Am I wanted here?"* with a decisive negative.

**Why "Abandonment" over "Unwanted":** The clinical literature uses abandonment because it is actionable and encompasses both *active* rejection (being left, expelled, given up) and *passive* forms (emotional unavailability that communicates "you don't matter"). It maps to Bowlby's attachment theory as the prototypical attachment wound and is a diagnostic criterion for Complex PTSD in the ICD-11.

**What it encompasses:**
- Physical abandonment (caregiver death, desertion, institutionalization)
- Emotional abandonment (caregiver presence without emotional availability)
- Social abandonment (ostracism, bullying, systemic exclusion)
- Existential abandonment (being made to feel one is fundamentally unlovable or burdensome)

**Magnitude drivers:** Permanence of the rupture, age at occurrence, whether it was explained or rationalized, and the availability of replacement attachment figures.

**Associated outcomes at high magnitude:** Borderline features, chronic shame, abandonment terror in relationships, identity instability, self-destructive behavior as a locus-of-control shift.

---

### 3.3 Violation

> *"The wound of the shattered"* — trauma by commission

**Definition:** Direct, active harm imposed on the person — the classic "big T" trauma of Herman and Terr. This is the cardinal most associated with traditional PTSD: an event or series of events that exceeds the person's capacity to integrate, fragmenting their sense of safety and bodily integrity.

**What it encompasses:**
- Physical abuse
- Sexual abuse
- Witnessed violence (domestic violence, community violence, war)
- Medical trauma
- Natural disasters, accidents, mass events
- Verbal/psychological abuse as an active pattern of harm

**Why it is distinct from the other cardinals:** Violation is the only cardinal defined by an *external agent actively doing something to the person*. Its neurobiological signature is distinct: it triggers hyperarousal, startle responses, intrusive re-experiencing, and avoidance — the classic PTSD cluster. The other three cardinals are relationally structured (failures of care, belonging, and trust); Violation is structurally different in that it can occur even with fully functional attachment and social systems.

**Magnitude drivers:** Severity, repetition (Type I vs. Type II trauma per Terr), developmental timing, physical proximity, relationship to perpetrator, and whether disclosure was met with support or further harm.

**Associated outcomes at high magnitude:** PTSD, hypervigilance, somatic dysregulation, dissociation, avoidance, chronic fear responses.

---

### 3.4 Betrayal

> *"The wound of the unmoored"* — trauma by broken covenant

**Definition:** The experience of being harmed, failed, or exploited by a person or system in whom trust had been placed and was structurally necessary. Betrayal Trauma Theory (Freyd, 1994) identifies this as a distinct traumatic mechanism: the greater the dependency on the betrayer, the more severe the traumatic impact and the more likely the victim is to suppress awareness of the betrayal to preserve the attachment.

**What it encompasses:**
- Caregiver perpetration of abuse (parent as abuser, not protector)
- Institutional betrayal (schools, churches, medical systems, justice systems that fail or actively harm)
- Partner betrayal (intimate partner violence or infidelity with severe relational dependency)
- Social betrayal (community or cultural systems that gaslight, deny, or invert the victim's reality)

**Why it is a distinct cardinal:** Betrayal is not merely high-magnitude Violation or Abandonment — it has a unique cognitive dimension: the *requirement to not know*. Freyd's research demonstrates that betrayal trauma produces a specific dissociative signature (social amnesia for the harm) that is not found in equivalent non-betrayal trauma. It also maps to the "double bind" that complex trauma literature identifies: the attachment figure is simultaneously the source of terror and the only available source of safety.

**Magnitude drivers:** Degree of dependency on the betrayer, whether the betrayal was acknowledged or denied, institutional vs. individual perpetration, and the presence of gaslighting or reality-denial.

**Associated outcomes at high magnitude:** Complex dissociation, profound trust impairment, reality-testing difficulties, hypervigilance in relationships, complex grief.

## How the Model Works

### 4.1 The Origin

The center of the model — the origin point at magnitude zero on all four vectors — represents the *theoretically unexposed self*. This is a conceptual reference, not an empirical claim that any person exists at perfect zero. It represents the state where none of the four cardinal wounds have been activated at a clinically meaningful level.

Movement away from the origin on any vector represents *wound activation*. The model is agnostic about whether this represents pathology; a small magnitude on the Violation vector might represent a person who experienced a car accident and processed it adaptively. The model describes exposure and wounding, not diagnosis.

### 4.2 Magnitude & Severity Scaling

Each vector can be conceptually scaled 0–10, mapping to the following bands:

| Band | Range | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Subclinical | 0–2 | Minimal or transient exposure; adaptive processing likely |
| Moderate | 3–5 | Meaningful exposure; disrupted but without pervasive functional impairment |
| Severe | 6–8 | Chronic or developmentally timed exposure; significant functional impact |
| Complex | 9–10 | Prolonged, multi-incident, developmentally early; associated with C-PTSD presentation |

Magnitude on any given vector is a function of:
- **Intensity** of the incident(s)
- **Chronicity** (single event vs. sustained exposure)
- **Developmental stage** at onset (earlier = higher magnitude potential)
- **Available protective factors** (compensatory attachment figures, community support, disclosure validation)

### 4.3 The Trauma Profile Polygon

When a person's position on all four vectors is plotted and connected, the resulting quadrilateral is their **Trauma Profile Polygon**. Several properties of this polygon are diagnostically informative:

**Total Area** correlates with overall trauma burden — equivalent to ACE scoring but dimensionally sensitive. A large, roughly symmetrical polygon suggests poly-traumatization across all cardinals (classic complex PTSD profile). A polygon with one or two extreme vectors and low values on others suggests a more specific traumatic etiology.

**Shape Asymmetry** indicates the dominant traumatic mechanism. A person with a massive Neglect vector and minimal Violation and Betrayal vectors has a very different clinical presentation and intervention need than a person with the inverse profile, even if their total polygon areas are similar.

**Expansion Patterns** across treatment can be tracked — ideally showing all vectors contracting toward the origin as healing progresses, allowing visual representation of therapeutic gain.

### 4.4 Quadrant Interaction Effects

When multiple vectors are activated at high magnitude simultaneously, interaction effects emerge that are qualitatively different from single-vector exposure:

**Neglect + Abandonment** (the Relational Deprivation Quadrant): Classic developmental trauma and C-PTSD substrate. The child receives neither care nor belonging. Associated with profound identity disturbances, chronic shame, and attachment disorder presentations.

**Violation + Betrayal** (the Relational Harm Quadrant): The perpetrator is the protector. This is the most dissociation-generating combination per Freyd's BTT. The person cannot integrate that the source of harm is the person they depend on. Associated with structural dissociation and complex grief.

**All Four at High Magnitude**: The *Complex Traumatization Ceiling* — the zone associated with Developmental Trauma Disorder (van der Kolk), Complex PTSD (Herman, ICD-11), and severe personality disorder presentations. The polygon in this state approaches maximum area.

## Validation Against Existing Frameworks

The TCVM is designed to be congruent with, not competing with, established frameworks.

**ACE Framework (Felitti et al., 1998):** The ten ACE categories map directly onto TCVM cardinals. Neglect (physical/emotional) → Neglect vector. Abandonment/loss of parent → Abandonment vector. Physical, sexual, verbal abuse → Violation vector. Caregiver as perpetrator + household dysfunction → Betrayal vector. The TCVM adds dimensional sensitivity (severity scaling) that flat ACE counting obscures.

**Herman's Complex Trauma (1992):** Herman defines complex trauma as "exposure to severe stressors that are repetitive or prolonged, involves harm or abandonment by caregivers, and occurs at developmentally vulnerable times." Each element maps: harm = Violation, abandonment = Abandonment, caregiver context = Betrayal, chronicity = magnitude scaling.

**Draijer's 2D Model (2003):** Directly validates two of our cardinal axes — "trauma (being overwhelmed)" = Violation vector, "emotional neglect (being unreflected)" = Neglect vector — and empirically demonstrates their independence in predicting outcomes. The TCVM extends this to four dimensions by adding Abandonment and Betrayal as orthogonal axes.

**Betrayal Trauma Theory (Freyd, 1994):** Directly validates the Betrayal cardinal as a mechanistically distinct dimension, not reducible to high-magnitude Violation.

**Structural Dissociation Model (van der Hart, Nijenhuis & Steele, 2006):** The model's dissociative predictions align with TCVM quadrant interaction effects — the highest dissociation is predicted when Violation and Betrayal co-occur at high magnitude (caregiver-perpetrated abuse), which maps to the Relational Harm Quadrant.

## Clinical & Experimental Applications

**As an Assessment Tool:** The TCVM can be operationalized as a brief structured instrument — four subscales corresponding to each cardinal, each yielding a severity score. Plotting the resulting polygon gives a clinician an instant visual gestalt of the traumatic landscape, separate from symptom-based diagnosis.

**As a Case Formulation Aid:** The dominant vector(s) can guide treatment modality selection. High Violation → somatic/exposure-based approaches (EMDR, SE). High Neglect → attachment-focused work (ARC, CPP). High Abandonment → schema therapy, IFS. High Betrayal → Betrayal Trauma-informed therapy, EMDR focused on relational processing.

**For Research:** The polygon provides a geometric measure of trauma burden that is more granular than ACE scores and more ecologically valid than diagnosis-based categorization. Polygon area can be used as a continuous predictor variable. Shape ratios can be used to test whether specific cardinal profiles predict specific outcome profiles.

**As a Psychoeducation Tool:** The four-cardinal framework is accessible to clients. Being able to identify *which* wound is most dominant — "I wasn't neglected, but I was profoundly abandoned" — can be clinically meaningful for clients who find traditional diagnostic labels either over-inclusive or stigmatizing.

## Limitations & Open Questions

**Orthogonality assumption:** The model assumes the four cardinals are meaningfully distinct. While this is supported by Draijer's 2D empirical work and Freyd's BTT research, full empirical validation of four-way orthogonality requires factor analysis on large clinical samples.

**Cultural validity:** The definitions of all four cardinals (especially Abandonment and Betrayal) are partly culturally inflected. What constitutes abandonment in one cultural context may be normative in another. The model requires cultural calibration.

**Cardinal completeness:** Four cardinals may not be sufficient. Candidates for a fifth cardinal include **Violation by Systemic/Institutional Force** (racism, poverty, political violence) — which doesn't reduce cleanly to any of the four existing cardinals and may operate by distinct mechanisms. This is an open question.

**Developmental weighting:** The model currently treats magnitude as a single linear dimension. A more refined version might weight magnitude by developmental timing — a 2 on the Neglect vector at age 0–3 may be more damaging than a 6 at age 14, requiring nonlinear scaling.

**Resilience is not represented:** The model maps wounding, not protective factors. A companion model — perhaps concentric rings representing buffering factors (secure attachment figures, social support, therapeutic processing) that constrain outward vector expansion — could make the TCVM a full dual-process framework.
