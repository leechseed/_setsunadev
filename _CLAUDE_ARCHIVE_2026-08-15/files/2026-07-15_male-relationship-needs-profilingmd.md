## 1. Read Me First — What Exists and What Doesn't {#1-read-me-first}

There is no single validated "What a Man Needs in a Relationship" questionnaire. What exists instead is a stack of instruments, each profiling one layer of the system — attachment, temperament, needs, love style, desire, values, relational skills. The complete profile is assembled, not administered. This is the same move as a Hughes/Horney/SDT deck stack: you're layering frameworks that each answer a different question, then reading the composite.

One caveat that governs everything below: **stated preferences predict poorly.** The Eastwick & Finkel speed-dating studies (2008) found that what people *say* they want in a partner barely predicts who they're actually drawn to in the room. So treat every output of this profiling exercise as a hypothesis set, not a spec sheet. The profile tells you what to test against lived experience — it is not the ground truth by itself. The instruments that hold up best are the ones measuring how you *operate* (attachment, desire mechanics, conflict style) rather than what you *claim to want*.

Second caveat: several of the most famous frameworks in this space are pop-psych with weak or absent validation (Love Languages, MBTI, Erotic Blueprints). They're still included below because they're useful as *vocabulary* — shared language for articulating needs — just don't mistake vocabulary for measurement. Validity notes are flagged per instrument.

## 2. The Layer Model — Profile Architecture {#2-the-layer-model}

| # | Layer | Question it answers | Primary instruments |
|---|-------|--------------------|--------------------|
| L1 | Attachment system | How do I bond, and what threatens the bond? | ECR-R, RQ |
| L2 | Temperament baseline | What's my fixed operating temperament? | IPIP Big Five, HEXACO |
| L3 | Core needs | What must a relationship feed for me to stay alive in it? | Harley ENQ, BPNSFS (SDT), NVC needs list, Hughes/Robbins 6-needs |
| L4 | Love expression & receipt | How do I give love, and in what currency do I receive it? | Love Attitudes Scale (Lee), Sternberg TLS, Love Languages |
| L5 | Desire architecture | How does my sexuality actually work? | SES/SIS (Dual Control), SOI-R, SDI-2 |
| L6 | Values & ideals | What long-term alignment do I require, and how flexible am I? | Schwartz PVQ, Fletcher Ideal Standards, dealbreaker inventory |
| L7 | Relational operating system | How do I fight, repair, and build intimacy day-to-day? | Gottman assessments, PAIR, CSI |

Layers 1, 2, and 5 are the most empirically solid — they measure mechanics. Layers 3, 4, and 6 are self-report about wants — useful, but subject to the stated-preference problem. Layer 7 is diagnostic and mostly requires being *in* a relationship to score.

## 3. Instruments by Layer {#3-instruments-by-layer}

### 3.1 Attachment System

**ECR-R (Experiences in Close Relationships — Revised).** Fraley, Waller & Brennan (2000). 36 items, ~10 minutes. Scores two continuous dimensions: attachment *anxiety* (fear of abandonment, need for reassurance) and attachment *avoidance* (discomfort with closeness and dependence). This is the workhorse of adult attachment research. R. Chris Fraley hosts a free interactive version on his research site (yourpersonality.net) that returns your percentile on both dimensions against a large norm sample — one of the few places in this whole space where you get an actual benchmark. Open Psychometrics also hosts an ECR version.

*What it tells you about the other side:* high anxiety → you need a partner who gives consistent, legible reassurance; high avoidance → you need a partner who tolerates space without punishing it. Low both = secure. This layer constrains everything downstream.

**RQ (Relationship Questionnaire).** Bartholomew & Horowitz (1991). Four short paragraphs, you rate how much each describes you. Yields the four-prototype typology: secure, preoccupied, dismissive-avoidant, fearful-avoidant. Fast, crude, good as a gut-check against the ECR-R dimensional score. The book *Attached* (Levine & Heller) popularized this typology and includes its own quiz — fine as an on-ramp, less precise than ECR-R.

**AAI (Adult Attachment Interview).** Clinical, narrative-based, requires a trained coder. Gold standard in developmental research, not practical for self-profiling. Listed for completeness.

### 3.2 Temperament Baseline

**IPIP Big Five (various lengths: IPIP-50, IPIP-120, BFI-2).** Free, public-domain, well-normed. The Big Five traits have the strongest replicated links to relationship outcomes of any personality model — notably, low neuroticism and high agreeableness/conscientiousness predict relationship satisfaction and stability in both self and partner. Your own neuroticism score is arguably the single most predictive personality number for how your relationships will feel.

*What it tells you about the other side:* it profiles your friction surface. High openness → you need novelty and idea-play; high neuroticism → you need a stabilizing, low-chaos partner more than you need an exciting one; low extraversion → you need someone who doesn't read quiet as distance.

**HEXACO-PI-R.** Adds a sixth factor, Honesty-Humility, which is the best personality predictor of infidelity and exploitative relationship behavior. Worth running if trust architecture matters to your profile. Free version at hexaco.org.

**MBTI / 16Personalities.** Massive cultural footprint, poor test-retest reliability, dichotomizes continuous traits. Use only as social vocabulary; don't build the profile on it.

**Fisher Temperament Inventory.** Helen Fisher's 56-item measure behind Match/Chemistry.com — four types (Explorer/dopamine, Builder/serotonin, Director/testosterone, Negotiator/estrogen). Hosted at theanatomyoflove.com. Interesting neurochemical framing, mixed independent validation. Mid-tier: better than MBTI, below Big Five.

### 3.3 Core Needs

This is the layer closest to what you're actually asking for, and it's where the direct "what does a man need" instruments live.

**Harley's Emotional Needs Questionnaire (ENQ).** From *His Needs, Her Needs* (Willard Harley). The most direct "rank what you need from a partner" instrument in circulation. Ten emotional needs, rated and force-ranked: affection, sexual fulfillment, intimate conversation, recreational companionship, honesty & openness, physical attractiveness, financial support, domestic support, family commitment, admiration. Harley's clinical claim is that men most often top-rank sexual fulfillment, recreational companionship, physical attractiveness, domestic support, and admiration — while women most often top-rank the other five. Treat that gender split as a 1980s clinical heuristic, not settled science (it's heteronormative and dated), but the *instrument itself* — force-ranking ten needs — is exactly the profiling move you want. Free PDF on marriagebuilders.com.

**BPNSFS (Basic Psychological Need Satisfaction and Frustration Scale).** The SDT instrument — 24 items measuring satisfaction *and frustration* of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. There are relationship-specific adaptations (La Guardia et al.'s need-satisfaction-in-relationships work) that ask these questions about a specific partner. Since SDT is already a deck in your character stack, this is the same engine pointed at yourself: a relationship that frustrates autonomy will burn you out no matter how much relatedness it supplies.

**NVC Needs Inventory.** The Nonviolent Communication universal needs list (cnvc.org) — not a scored instrument, but the best *checklist* for a needs audit. Categories: connection, physical wellbeing, honesty, play, peace, autonomy, meaning. Run it as a highlight-and-rank exercise.

**Robbins' Six Human Needs.** Certainty, variety, significance, love/connection, growth, contribution — with the core diagnostic question being which two you've made primary. No validated instrument, but as a coarse sorting frame it pairs naturally with the Hughes six-needs map you've already worked (significance, approval, acceptance, intelligence, pity, strength) — Hughes profiles what you *display for*, Robbins profiles what you *organize your life around*. Both are worth a row in the profile even unscored.

**Murray's Psychogenic Needs / EPPS.** The Edwards Personal Preference Schedule operationalizes Murray's need taxonomy (achievement, affiliation, dominance, nurturance, succorance, autonomy, etc.). Proprietary and dated, but Murray's taxonomy itself is public and worth mining — "succorance" (the need to be cared for) and "nurturance" (the need to care for) are two axes most modern relationship instruments ignore entirely. Relevant if you track rescuer/knight dynamics: high nurturance + a partner selected for needing rescue is a profile pattern worth naming explicitly, and it maps cleanly onto Horney's moving-toward vector in your existing stack.
