## 1. How to Use This

Part 2 and 3 are the frame — read them first, they're short. Part 4 (teasing) is the centerpiece and doubles as a dialogue-writing toolbox. Part 5 is a set of separate tools; dip in as needed. Part 7 is the payoff: it turns all of it into concrete models for writing how your BVX characters *act on each other.*

Same discipline as always: **one tool at a time.** Don't try to hold the whole of Keltner in your head. Understand teasing, set it down, pick up awe, and so on. The picture forms when you lay the tools side by side.

## 2. The One Big Idea

Panksepp asks *what's running inside one creature.* Keltner asks a different question, and it's the sentence this whole document rests on: **emotions are not private weather — they are a language, and their job is to manage the space between people.**

Dacher Keltner (UC Berkeley, founder of the Greater Good Science Center) studies emotion as **social signaling.** A blush, a tease, a touch on the arm, a burst of laughter, the small self you feel under a night sky — to Keltner these aren't just inner states. **They're messages.** They tell other people where you stand, how close you want to be, who's up and who's down, whether it's safe, whether you belong. Emotion is how mammals *negotiate their relationships without having to spell everything out.*

Why this matters for BVX: if Panksepp gives you a character's inner engines, **Keltner gives you what happens when two characters are in a room together.** Status, flirtation, bonding, needling, awe, contempt, comfort — the entire between-people layer. That's where scenes actually live. A character alone is a profile; **a character with another character is a *dynamic*, and this is the document about dynamics.**

---

## 3. How This Pairs With Panksepp

Keep this straight and the two docs become one tool:

- **Panksepp = the inside.** The seven engines driving a single character from underneath. What they *want and fear and need*, below thought.
- **Keltner = the between.** What that character *does to and with other characters* — the signals they send, the status they claim, the closeness they offer or deny.

The system guide told you: *you never build one perfect picture; you build simple ones and read them together.* Here it is in practice. **Panksepp is the engine; Keltner is the driving — how the car behaves around other cars.** One character = one Panksepp profile plus a handful of Keltner dynamics. Inside and between. That's a whole person.

They even touch directly at one point: Panksepp's **PLAY** engine (rough-and-tumble joy) is the raw fuel; Keltner's **teasing** is what that fuel looks like once it's grown up and put on social clothes. So we start there.

## 4. Teasing — the deep dive

Teasing looks trivial. It isn't. Keltner's landmark paper (*"Just Teasing," Psychological Bulletin,* 2001) argues that teasing is one of the most sophisticated things people do to each other — a tiny, deniable act that can bond, flirt, correct, or wound depending on how it's built. For a character writer it's almost a cheat code, because **a single well-made tease can show a whole relationship in one line.**

### What a tease actually is

Keltner's definition, worth memorizing:

> **A tease is an intentional provocation accompanied by playful off-record markers that together comment on something relevant to the target.**

Unpack that and you get the machine:

- **Intentional provocation** — you're *poking* at them on purpose. A tease that pokes at nothing isn't a tease.
- **Playful off-record markers** — the "I don't fully mean this" signals wrapped around the poke. (More on these below; they're the heart of it.)
- **Commenting on something relevant to the target** — the poke lands on something *true or near-true* about them. A tease about nothing lands flat; a tease has to touch a real nerve, gently.

**The genius of a tease is that it says a hard thing and takes it back at the same time.** It's provocation and deniability fused into one act. That double nature is exactly why it's such a rich tool for character work.

### The three parts of every tease

Break any tease into three moving parts — this is your build kit:

1. **The provocation** — *what's being poked.* A flaw, a habit, a vanity, a mistake, an attraction, a status claim. ("Oh, *now* you're on time.")
2. **The off-record markers** — *the wrapping that makes it deniable.* Tone, exaggeration, a grin, a nickname. (Said in a sing-song, with a smirk.)
3. **The target-relevant point** — *the real thing it's about.* Their chronic lateness, and the fact that everyone's noticed, and the fact that we're close enough that I can say so.

Change the ratio of these three and you change *everything* about what the tease means. Heavy provocation, thin markers = it stings. Light provocation, thick markers = pure affection. **The mix is the message.**

### Off-record markers: the "just kidding" toolbox

This is the part to steal for dialogue. "Off-record" is a term from politeness theory: saying something *without officially saying it,* so you can deny you meant it. Off-record markers are the signals that flip a sentence from *statement* to *tease.* Keltner catalogs them, and each one is a writer's tool:

- **Exaggeration / absurd overstatement** — claiming something too big to be literal. ("You've been in that bathroom for a *geological age.*")
- **Sing-song voice, stretched vowels, weird stress** — prosody that says *don't take this straight.* ("Sooomebody's in loooove.")
- **Irony and sarcasm** — saying the opposite of what you mean. ("Great parking job.")
- **Repetition / formulaic bits** — "yeah yeah yeah," a running gag, a catchphrase.
- **Metaphorical nicknames** — a name that pokes and endears at once. ("Nice one, *Einstein.*")
- **Mimicry and exaggerated faces** — doing an impression of them.
- **The wink, the smirk, the paradoxical gesture** — a face that contradicts the words.
- **Laughter** — the affiliation stamp: *we're playing.*

**Here's the writer's payoff: the markers are dials, and the setting reveals the relationship.** Thick markers (broad grin, silly voice, obvious exaggeration) = *I'm being warm, this is safe.* Thin markers (flat tone, barely a smile, mostly straight) = *I'm half-serious, and you have to guess how much.* **The thinner the markers, the more dangerous — and the more intimate or hostile — the tease.** You can write two characters' entire history into how thickly they wrap their needling.

### What teasing is for

Teasing isn't one thing; it does at least four jobs, and knowing which job a tease is doing tells you what a scene is really about:

- **Socializing / norm-policing.** Teasing points at a norm violation *without* a formal confrontation. ("Wearing *that* to the meeting?") It's how groups correct their members softly.
- **Flirtation / courtship.** Teasing is a low-risk way to signal interest — provocation you can walk back if it isn't returned. Flirtatious teasing is deniable attraction. (This is *the* engine of romantic tension in fiction.)
- **Status and hierarchy.** Who teases whom, how hard, and who has to take it — that's a live negotiation of rank, happening in real time. (More below.)
- **Bonding and play.** Affectionate teasing is a closeness signal: *I know you well enough to poke you, and we both know I mean nothing by it.* Shared teasing rituals are relationship glue.

### Teasing vs. bullying: the whole difference

This distinction is critical for character morality — get it wrong and a likable rogue reads as a monster. Keltner's line is clean:

> **Teasing carries the off-record markers. Bullying strips them out.**

Bullying is provocation delivered *straight* — the kick, the name-called flatly, the humiliation with no wink and no exit. As one researcher put it: **"teasing can be both playful and malicious, but bullying can never be considered playful."** The markers are the moral hinge. **Same words — "you're such an idiot" — are affection with a grin and a sing-song, and cruelty said flat and cold.** When you write a character who teases, the markers are how the reader knows whether to love them or fear them. Take the markers away and your charming needler becomes a bully in a single beat — which is, itself, a devastating move if you do it on purpose.

### Power changes who teases whom

Keltner found teasing runs *along the status gradient,* and this is pure fuel for character dynamics:

- **High-status people tease more — and more harshly.** Their teases carry *more aggressive provocation and fewer softening markers.* Rank buys the right to poke hard and not wrap it. (His fraternity study: high-status members teased with "more hostile" provocations and fewer politeness softeners than low-status members.)
- **The powerful can afford to be blunt; the less powerful can't.** Low-status people tease more carefully, wrap thicker, hedge more — because for them a tease that misfires is dangerous.
- **Being teased threatens the low-power target more.** The same tease that's fun between equals reads as a threat when it comes down the hierarchy — the low-power target feels more exposed, more "face" on the line.
- **Familiarity turns up the heat.** The closer two people are, the more they tease and the thinner the markers get — intimacy earns the right to poke without cushioning.

**So the *style* of a tease is a status readout.** Show a character teasing hard with no cushion and the room taking it — you've established they're top of the pecking order without a word of exposition. Show a character wrapping every tease in three layers of markers — you've shown they're careful, lower-status, or unsure of their footing.

### How teasing grows up

Teasing is a *developmental achievement* — you have to be cognitively sophisticated to do it well, because you have to hold "I mean this" and "I don't mean this" at once:

- **Ages 5–6:** kids tease, but take things mostly literally — they miss the doubled meaning.
- **Ages 8–9:** they start grasping the contradiction — that a poke can carry two intentions at once.
- **Ages 11–13:** a big leap — they get playful intent and start producing genuinely sophisticated teases (and start wrapping their own hostility in markers to soften it).
- **Older kids** increasingly report the *positive* side — teasing as bonding, not just needling.

For character work this is a gift: **teasing style is an instant age-and-sophistication marker.** A child who teases literally, a teen discovering the deniable art of it, an adult who wields it like a scalpel — each reads as a different developmental place without you stating an age.

## 5. The Rest of the Toolkit

Teasing is one tool. Here are the others, each as its own small picture.

### The Power Paradox

Keltner's book *The Power Paradox* (2016) delivers one devastating, story-shaped finding:

> **You gain power by being good to people — and having power makes you worse at being good to people.**

We *give* power to those who advance the group: the empathetic, the generous, the ones who make others feel seen. But once someone *has* power, it acts on them like a mild brain injury: **the powerful grow less empathic, worse at reading others, more impulsive, ruder, more entitled, quicker to stereotype.** (His lab's studies are famous — powerful people literally take more than their share of a shared plate of cookies, chew with their mouths open, interrupt more, and get worse at reading emotions on faces.) The very traits that earned the power are eroded *by* the power. That's the paradox.

**Why it's gold for BVX: this is a complete character arc in one sentence.** The corruption arc, the fall of the good leader, the founder who forgets the people who lifted them — it's all here, and it's *earned,* not arbitrary. The tragedy isn't that a bad person got power; it's that **power made a good person bad.** Far more affecting.

### Awe

Keltner is the world's leading researcher of awe (*Awe: The New Science of Everyday Wonder,* 2023). His definition has two parts:

> **Awe is what you feel in the presence of something (1) vast, that (2) doesn't fit your current understanding — so your mind has to stretch to take it in.**

The two features are **vastness** (physical, like a canyon, or conceptual, like a huge idea) and **need for accommodation** (it's so big your existing mental frames can't hold it, so they have to expand). Awe's effects are specific and useful: it shrinks the **"small self"** (your ego and daily worries drop away), it makes people **more generous, humble, and connected,** it warps time (moments feel longer), and it shows up in the body as **goosebumps** and a settling of the nervous system.

Keltner maps **eight sources** — "the eight wonders of life": **moral beauty** (witnessing others' courage or kindness — the *most common* trigger worldwide), **collective effervescence** (moving as one with a crowd), **nature, music, visual design/art, spirituality, big ideas,** and **birth and death.**

**For BVX:** awe is your **transformation beat.** The moment a character's small self dissolves and something opens — that's the turning point that makes change believable. And note the #1 source: *watching another person be good.* One character can be another's source of awe. That's a powerful bond to write.

### Compassion and "survival of the kindest"

In *Born to Be Good* (2009), Keltner argues that evolution didn't only make us competitive — **it made us kind, because kindness is what let a fragile, slow-maturing social animal survive.** He reclaims Darwin here (Darwin himself stressed sympathy) and reframes "survival of the fittest" as **"survival of the kindest."** The moral emotions — compassion, gratitude, awe, amusement, even embarrassment — aren't decorations on human nature; they're the load-bearing structure of it.

He ties compassion to the body: the **vagus nerve** (the big nerve running from brainstem to gut) may be a **"nerve of compassion"** — people with stronger vagal tone tend to be calmer, more caring, more connected. **For BVX:** this is the counterweight to the Power Paradox. If power is the fall, compassion is the redemption — and it, too, is *earned* by the biology, not sentimental. A character's capacity for warmth is as real and physical as their capacity for rage.

### Touch as a language

One of Keltner's most striking findings: **a single one-second touch on the forearm — from a stranger you can't see — reliably communicates distinct emotions.** In his lab, people identified **anger, fear, disgust, love, gratitude, and compassion** from touch alone, at accuracy rates matching the face and voice. Touch is a full emotional channel, not a vague one.

And touch *builds* things: in his study of the NBA, **the teams whose players touched each other more early in the season (high-fives, hugs, chest-bumps) cooperated better and won more later.** Touch triggers the body's trust-and-calm chemistry.

**For BVX:** touch is a precision instrument for showing what dialogue hides. *Who touches whom, where, how long, and who pulls away* can carry an entire relationship silently. A character who won't be touched, one who touches too readily, the first time two characters make contact — these are scenes, not stage directions.

### The 27 emotions

With Alan Cowen (*PNAS,* 2017), Keltner mapped the emotions people actually report and found **not six basic emotions, but around 27 distinct categories** — and, crucially, **they don't sit in separate boxes; they blend into each other along smooth gradients** (anxiety shades into fear shades into horror; calm shades into aesthetic appreciation shades into awe).

Two takeaways matter for writing. First, **the real palette is enormous and weird** — it includes states we rarely name: *awe, nostalgia, awkwardness, entrancement, aesthetic appreciation, craving, empathic pain, boredom, adoration, romance, satisfaction.* Second, and notably, **plain "anger" did not show up as one of the core categories** in what these everyday moments evoked — a reminder that the emotions driving real experience aren't the tidy handful we default to. *(Exact membership of the 27 varies a little across write-ups and methods; treat the list as a rich sampler, not scripture. The durable finding is the* gradient *— emotions blend, they don't snap between boxes.)*

**For BVX:** stop writing from the six-crayon box (happy/sad/angry/afraid/surprised/disgusted). **Reach for the 27-color set.** A character isn't "happy" — they're *nostalgic,* or *entranced,* or *awkwardly amused.* And because emotions blend, you can write a character *sliding* from one to its neighbor (unease → dread → horror) rather than snapping between states. That gradient is what emotional realism is made of.

### Inside Out: emotions as characters

Keltner (with Paul Ekman) was the scientific consultant on Pixar's *Inside Out.* He helped decide which emotions to give bodies, and — most importantly — argued that **Sadness had to be essential, not a nuisance.** In the film, Sadness turns out to be the hero: it's what summons others' care and repairs connection.

**Why this belongs in your toolkit:** *Inside Out* is literally this entire document done as character design — emotions given personalities, drives, and roles. The lesson Keltner proved there is the lesson for BVX: **the "negative" emotion is often the most important character in the room.** Sadness, fear, awkwardness, grief — these aren't obstacles to a good character; frequently they *are* the good character. Don't design them out. Design around them.

## 7. Turning Keltner Into Character Models

Four reusable models for BVX, built from the tools above. Where Panksepp set a character's *dials,* these set their *dynamics.*

### Model 1: The Tease Generator

To write a tease between any two characters, set four things:

1. **Provocation** — what real thing are you poking? (a vanity, a flaw, an attraction, a status claim)
2. **Markers** — how thick is the wrapping? (broad-and-silly = safe/warm · thin-and-flat = intimate or dangerous)
3. **Target point** — what's it *really* about underneath?
4. **Intent** — where does it sit on the line from **pure affection ↔ pure aggression?**

Turn the dials and the same poke becomes flirtation, bonding, a status-shove, or cruelty. **A great relationship reveal is often just: change the marker thickness across the story.** Strangers wrap thick; by the end, they needle each other bare — and that thinning *is* the intimacy, shown not told.

### Model 2: The Status Dial

Give every character a **power position** in each scene (high / equal / low), and let it bend how they express *everything:*

- **High power:** teases hard with thin markers, touches freely, interrupts, takes up space, blunt, less busy reading the room.
- **Low power:** teases carefully with thick markers, monitors faces, hedges, minimizes, worried about "face."

**The same character shifts as the room shifts.** Someone who's blunt-and-expansive with subordinates and careful-and-monitoring with a rival isn't inconsistent — that *is* how power reads. Write the status shift and you write a fuller person. (And remember the Power Paradox: a character who *stays* high-power too long should start to *coarsen* — less empathy, more entitlement — if you want the arc to feel true.)

### Model 3: The Relationship-Emotion Map

Every relationship is defined by the **"between" emotions** that mostly pass across it. Name them and you've named the bond:

- Built on **amusement + play + teasing** → the buddy dynamic.
- Built on **awe + admiration** → mentor/hero, or the beloved out of reach.
- Built on **gratitude + compassion** → loyalty, the debt of care.
- Built on **contempt + thin-marker teasing** → rivalry curdling toward cruelty.
- Built on **romance + flirtatious teasing + touch** → the love line.

Write down the two or three emotions that *live in the wire* between two characters, and you know how every scene between them should feel — and you know the arc: **the bond changes when the between-emotions change** (admiration curdles to contempt; teasing warms into romance).

### Model 4: The Arc Engine

Keltner hands you two ready-made, *earned* arcs — the kind that feel inevitable rather than plotted:

- **The Fall (Power Paradox):** a good character gains power → power erodes their empathy → they become the thing they rose against. Tragic *because* they started good.
- **The Opening (Awe / Compassion):** a closed, small-self character meets something vast — often *another person's goodness* — the small self dissolves, and they open. Redemption that's felt, not declared.

Point a character at one of these and the middle of their story fills itself in.

## 8. The Checklist

Do this per relationship (not per character — these are *between*-tools). One page, one pair.

**Setup**
- [ ] Name the **pair** at the top. Two characters.
- [ ] Note each one's **power position** toward the other (high / equal / low). If it's uneven, good — uneven is dramatic.

**Map the bond**
- [ ] Write the **2–3 "between" emotions** that live in the wire between them (Model 3).
- [ ] Decide the **marker thickness** of their teasing right now (thick/warm ↔ thin/charged).

**Pressure-test it**
- [ ] Draft **one tease** between them using the four dials (Model 1). Does it reveal the bond in one line? If not, adjust the intent dial.
- [ ] Find one **touch moment** that could say what they won't say out loud (Model 4/touch).

**Set the arc**
- [ ] Which **between-emotion changes** by the end? (admiration → contempt? teasing → romance?) That shift is the relationship's arc.
- [ ] If power is in play, does anyone **coarsen from holding it too long** (Power Paradox), or **open from awe/compassion**? Pick one if it fits.

**Stop, and pair it up**
- [ ] Lay this beside each character's **Panksepp seven-dial sheet.** Inside (engines) + between (dynamics) = the full picture. Don't merge them into one page — read them together. *(That's the method, every time.)*
