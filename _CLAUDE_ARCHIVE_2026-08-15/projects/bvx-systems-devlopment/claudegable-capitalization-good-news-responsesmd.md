## 1. How to Use This

Read Parts 2 and 3 first — they're short and they're the whole frame. Part 4 is the engine of the thing: four response styles, each one a castable character move. Part 8 is the payoff — it turns those four styles into a fast, deep, and *counterintuitive* tool for showing what a relationship really is on the page.

This is the most compact of the three frameworks — it's basically one elegant grid plus one killer finding. That's a feature. **A small tool you actually reach for beats a big one you admire and never use.**

---

## 2. The One Big Idea

Here's the sentence it rests on: **you find out what a bond is really made of not when things go wrong, but in the tiny, forgettable moment when someone shares good news — and you either turn toward it or you don't.**

Shelly L. Gable (UC Santa Barbara) studies a moment most people never think about: what happens right after someone tells you something *good* happened to them. She calls the act of sharing good news **capitalization** — "the process of informing another person about a personal positive event and thereby deriving additional benefit from it." In plain terms: **good news grows when you tell someone — but only if they receive it well.** Telling the right person doubles the joy. Telling the wrong person kills it.

And then the finding that gives this whole document its title. Gable followed dating couples and watched how partners responded to each other's good news. Months later she checked who was still together. **How you answer someone's good news predicted the survival of the relationship — and did it *better* than how you showed up for them in a crisis.** That's the counterintuitive punch. We all assume relationships are proven in the hard times — the hospital, the funeral, the disaster. Gable's data says the truer test is quieter and happens far more often: *someone you love says "guess what happened," and what you do in the next four seconds is, over thousands of repetitions, the relationship.*

For BVX, this is gold, because it hands you a **cheap, repeatable, revealing scene** most writers never think to use. You don't need a catastrophe to show a bond's health. You need one character with good news and one reaction shot.

## 3. How This Pairs With the Others

Line the three frameworks up and they nest, big to small:

- **Panksepp = the inside.** The seven engines running a single character from underneath.
- **Keltner = the between.** The whole social language that passes between characters — status, teasing, touch, awe.
- **Gable = one moment in the between, put under a microscope.** Of all the signals Keltner catalogs, Gable zooms in on the single most diagnostic one: *the response to a bid.* It's a reaction shot, one second long, and it tells you more about a relationship than a page of dialogue.

And here's where all three touch at once — which is why this framework is the connective tissue of the set. When one character shares good news and another responds, **a Keltner-level social move reaches straight down and moves the sharer's Panksepp-level engines.** Received well, the share *amplifies* the sharer's SEEKING-and-PLAY joy — met delight grows. Stomped on, it hits the **PANIC/GRIEF** engine (the ache of being unseen) or trips **RAGE** (the deflation of being belittled). So the good-news moment is the exact place where "how characters act toward each other" becomes "what characters feel inside." If you're building the BVX system, **this is a seam where the pieces lock together.**

*(Adjacent framework worth a note for later: John Gottman's "bids for connection" and "turning toward vs. turning away" are the same insight from a different lab. Gable is the positive-event, precisely-measured version. When you build the system, they belong on the same page.)*

## 4. The Four Response Styles

Gable borrowed a two-by-two grid (adapted from Caryl Rusbult's model of how people respond to relationship trouble) and pointed it at good news instead. Two dimensions:

- **Active ↔ Passive** — how much energy is in the response. Engaged and loud, or restrained and quiet.
- **Constructive ↔ Destructive** — whether the response lifts the good news up or drags it down.

Cross them and you get four ways to answer a share. Only one of them works. Let's run all four through a single moment so you can hear the difference.

**The share:** a character comes in and says — *"They picked my design. I'm the lead artist on the project."*

### Active–Constructive — turn toward, with energy

The good one. Enthusiastic, engaged, *curious.* You match their energy and you ask the questions that let them relive it.

> *"Wait — lead artist? Tell me everything. How did they tell you? This is exactly what you've been grinding for."*

**What it does:** the joy *grows.* The sharer feels seen, valued, understood. The bond ticks up a notch. And — crucially — **they learn that you're a safe place to bring a win, so they'll bring you the next one too.** This is the only style that predicts relationship well-being. The whole game is here.

### Passive–Constructive — turn toward, quietly

Warm, but small. Genuine happiness that barely leaves the room. A real smile and a few soft words — and then the moment closes.

> *"That's great. Proud of you."* *(warm, and then back to what they were doing)*

**What it does:** not cruel — but a missed chance. The joy is left un-amplified; the sharer got no room to relive it. Here's the part that surprises people: **even this "nice" response correlates *negatively* with relationship quality.** Under-responding is not neutral. A muted "that's nice" quietly tells the sharer their good news wasn't worth much. Do it for years and it reads as mild, chronic indifference.

### Active–Destructive — turn against, with energy

Engaged, but pointed *down.* You find the problem, the risk, the catch. Often dressed up as concern or realism.

> *"Lead? That's a lot more hours. And you'd be managing those people who never listen to you. You sure you want that headache?"*

**What it does:** the joy *curdles.* The sharer deflates, then defends — and files away the lesson that **wins get punished here, so maybe don't share the next one.** Note how it often *feels* caring to the person doing it ("I'm just looking out for you") — which is exactly what makes it such a rich, deniable character move.

### Passive–Destructive — turn away

The quietest killer. You don't engage at all — you ignore the news, deflect, or hijack it back to yourself.

> *"Oh, nice. Hey — did the landlord ever call back? I was on hold all afternoon."*

**What it does:** the sharer feels *erased.* The news evaporates as if it never happened. This teaches the deepest lesson of the four: **your good news doesn't matter to me.** And the sharer's response, over time, is to stop sharing — which is what a dying bond actually looks like from the inside. Not fighting. **Just quietly taking your good news elsewhere.**

## 5. The Master Grid

The whole framework on one card. Notice the shape of it: **three of the four squares are traps, and they fail in different directions.** Only the top-left works.

|  | **Constructive** (lifts it up) | **Destructive** (drags it down) |
|---|---|---|
| **Active** (high energy) | **ACTIVE–CONSTRUCTIVE** — "Tell me everything!" · *joy grows, bond grows* · **the only one that works** | **ACTIVE–DESTRUCTIVE** — "Have you thought about the downside?" · *joy curdles, sharer defends* |
| **Passive** (low energy) | **PASSIVE–CONSTRUCTIVE** — "That's nice." · *joy fizzles, moment missed* | **PASSIVE–DESTRUCTIVE** — "Anyway, about my day…" · *sharer erased, stops sharing* |

| Style | Sounds like | What the sharer learns |
|---|---|---|
| **Active–Constructive** | energy + questions that let them relive it | "My wins are safe with you — here's the next one." |
| **Passive–Constructive** | a quiet, genuine "nice, good for you" | "You're mildly glad, I guess." |
| **Active–Destructive** | finding the catch, the risk, the downside | "Wins get punished here." |
| **Passive–Destructive** | changing the subject, back to themselves | "My good news doesn't matter to you." |

## 6. The Other Half: The Share Itself

Everything above is about the *responder.* But there's a sharer, and their side is just as designable.

**Sharing good news is a bid — a small act of trust.** When a character chooses to tell someone their win, they're saying *I want you in this with me.* That's why capitalization has an **intrapersonal** payoff Gable measured directly: simply telling someone about a good event boosts your positive mood and life satisfaction *"above and beyond the importance of the event itself."* The telling *adds* joy — the event grows in the sharing. (When it's met well. Always that condition.)

Which gives you two quiet, powerful character signals:

- **Who a character runs to with good news reveals where their heart actually lives.** Not who they *say* they're closest to — who they *sprint to tell.* A character who lands the biggest win of their life and calls their estranged sister instead of their spouse has just told you the truth about that marriage, without a word of exposition.
- **A character who stops sharing is a character whose bond is dying.** Withdrawal of good news is one of the most realistic, least melodramatic ways to show a relationship going cold. No fight. They just… start taking their wins elsewhere. On screen or on the page, that's a devastating, understated beat.

## 7. Two Things Worth Knowing Before You Design

**One: the good-news moment is a leverage point — small push, big effect.** This is the payoff of Gable's counterintuitive finding, straight out of the system guide's vocabulary. A four-second reaction to a share, repeated across a relationship, outpredicts the big dramatic crisis. So if you want to show a bond's true state economically, **you don't need the catastrophe. You need the quiet win and the reaction shot.** That's a high-leverage scene hiding in plain sight — most stories spend their relationship-proving budget on conflict and never touch it.

**Two: only one square is safe, and "mild" still hurts.** The trap is thinking there's the bad response (active–destructive) and everything else is fine. Gable's data says otherwise: **passive–constructive and passive–destructive both correlate negatively too.** A lukewarm "that's nice" is not a neutral. This matters for writing because it means a relationship can rot *without a single villainous moment* — just years of under-response. That's a truer, sadder, more useful model of how bonds die than the screaming match.

And the mechanism underneath all of it — Gable and Harry Reis call it **perceived partner responsiveness**, and it has three parts. A good response makes the sharer feel:

- **Understood** — you *get* why this matters to them,
- **Validated** — you think it's genuinely worth being glad about,
- **Cared for** — you're on their side.

**Active–constructive responding is simply how you deliver those three signals in the good-news moment.** That's the whole secret, and it's the direct handshake with Keltner: this is *perceived responsiveness*, the exact currency of his "between." Get those three across and the bond grows; miss any of them and the share falls flat.

## 8. Turning This Into Character Models

Four reusable tools for BVX. Where Panksepp sets a character's inner dials and Keltner sets their dynamics, Gable gives you the single most efficient **relationship-reveal** in your kit.

### Model 1: The Response-Style Dial

Give every character a **default response style** — how they answer good news when they're not thinking about it. It's one of the fastest deep-characterization moves there is, because *how someone receives another's joy* exposes their own insides:

- **Active–Constructive by default** → secure, generous, un-threatened by others' wins. The warm one. (High Panksepp CARE/PLAY; low scarcity.)
- **Passive–Constructive by default** → reserved, undemonstrative, or a little walled-off. Could be temperament, could be avoidance. (Runs cool.)
- **Active–Destructive by default** → critical, competitive, anxious — often someone who experiences another's win as *their own loss.* Their inability to celebrate you is a confession about them. (Envy, scarcity, a RAGE or wounded-SEEKING streak.)
- **Passive–Destructive by default** → self-absorbed, checked-out, or already gone from the relationship. (Low investment, or attention that only points at themselves.)

Set the dial and a character's warmth, security, and hidden wounds all come through in how they take a friend's good day.

### Model 2: The Good-News Litmus Scene

The counterintuitive tool, and the one to actually steal. **To show a relationship's true state, don't write a fight — write a win.** Give one character genuine good news and let the audience watch the other receive it. One reaction shot and the audience *knows* — is this bond warm, cooling, or quietly over? — without a syllable of exposition. It's economical (a single beat), it's realistic (this is how bonds actually reveal themselves), and it's fresh (audiences are braced for the argument, not for the small moment). Perfect for the compressed storytelling of series and animation, and it lands just as hard on the page.

### Model 3: The Bond-Trajectory Tracker

A relationship's arc *is* the drift of its response styles over time. Track it:

- **AC → AD** (warm celebration curdling into finding-the-catch) = a bond souring. You can play this drift across a season and it will feel devastatingly real.
- **PD → AC** (someone who used to change the subject learning, finally, to turn toward) = a redemption arc, earned in small moments rather than grand gestures.
- **Sharing → silence** (a character who used to bring every win, now bringing none) = a bond dying quietly. The most understated breakup beat you can write.

And who a character *brings* their news to, over the course of a story, maps their real allegiances — watch that pointer move and you're watching their heart relocate.

### Model 4: The Revealing Mismatch

A character rarely uses one style with everyone — and the *split* is where the character is. Someone who's **active–constructive with the mentor and passive–destructive with the partner** has just shown you exactly where they're invested and where they've checked out. And a character who is **chronically active–destructive with everyone** isn't a realist — they're carrying a wound (envy, scarcity, a need to be the only one who wins), and *that wound is a whole character.* Pair this with the Panksepp seven-dial sheet: active–destructive responding is very often what a **wounded SEEKING or a scarcity-driven RAGE** looks like from the outside.

*Where this sits in the kit: keep a character's Gable default on their Panksepp sheet (it's an outward tell of their inner dials), and use the Good-News Litmus Scene wherever the Keltner relationship-map says two characters' bond is the real question.*

## 9. The Checklist

Do this per relationship — it's a *between* tool. One page, one pair.

**Setup**
- [ ] Name the **pair** at the top.
- [ ] Give each character their **default response style** to the other's good news (AC / PC / AD / PD).

**Test the bond**
- [ ] Write the **share:** what good news does one bring the other? (Make it something they genuinely care about.)
- [ ] Write the **reaction shot** in their default style. Read it back — does the audience now *know* the state of this bond from that one beat? If not, the style isn't landing; sharpen it.
- [ ] Check the three signals: does the response convey **understood, validated, cared-for** — or miss one? (A missed signal is where the scene gets its ache.)

**Set the drift**
- [ ] Does the style **change** across the story? Mark the start style and the end style. That drift is the relationship's arc.
- [ ] Is there a moment the sharer **stops sharing**? If the bond is meant to die quietly, that's your beat.

**Pair it up**
- [ ] Put this beside each character's **Panksepp seven-dial sheet** (their default style is an outward tell of their inner engines) and their **Keltner relationship map** (this is the highest-leverage moment inside it). Inside, between, and the one moment that tests them — read together. *(The method, every time: separate pictures, laid side by side.)*
