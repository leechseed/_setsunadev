# Understanding a System: A Plain Guide + Checklist

A walkthrough for figuring out what something (like BVX) actually *is* and how it works — without getting turned around in circles. The guide explains the thinking. The checklist at the end is the thing you actually do.

## 1. How to Use This

Read the guide once, slowly. You don't need to memorize anything. Then keep the checklist open and work through it on real paper or a real canvas. The guide is the *why*; the checklist is the *do*. Whenever a checklist step confuses you, the guide section it points to will explain it.

The whole thing should take an afternoon the first time. It gets fast after that.

## 2. The Guide

### The core problem: you can't hold it all at once

When you try to "understand BVX," you're trying to hold several different things in your head at the same time: what it is, how money and work flow through it, where its edges are, how its pieces fit inside other pieces. That is too much to hold at once. Nobody can do it. It's like trying to look at the front, back, top, and inside of a house all in a single glance — the image just won't form, and you end up spinning.

So here is the trick the whole field is built on, and it's the most important sentence in this document: **you never make one picture of a system. You make a few small, simple pictures, each one answering exactly one question.** Each picture is incomplete on purpose. The "real" system is what you see when you lay those simple pictures next to each other. The spinning feeling is the feeling of trying to merge them into one. You stop spinning the moment you let them stay separate.

### Idea 1: A "system" is just a way of looking

A system isn't a special object sitting out in the world. It's just a set of things that affect each other, *plus your decision to look at them together*. The same pile of stuff can be one system or another depending on why you're looking.

Think of a kitchen. A health inspector looks at it and sees a "food safety system." A cook looks at the same kitchen and sees a "get-food-out-fast system." An accountant sees a "cost system." Same room, same equipment — three different systems, because three different people are looking for three different reasons.

So when you ask "what is BVX," the honest answer is: *it depends on why you're asking.* That's not a dodge. It's the actual answer, and once you accept it, a big source of the circling disappears, because you stop hunting for one true answer that was never there to find.

### Idea 2: Where the thing "ends" is a choice you make

The "edge" of a system — what counts as inside it and what counts as outside — is called its **boundary**. Here's the part that trips everyone up: the boundary is not a fact you discover. It's a line *you draw*, and you draw it based on why you're looking.

Example. If your question is "how does BVX make money," then the boundary you draw includes your content, your platforms, your pricing, your audience. Your gaming hobby is *outside* that line. But if your question changes to "how do I keep myself creatively fueled," now your hobbies, your rest, your inputs are *inside* the line, and some money stuff drops out. Same BVX. Different question, different line.

This is why deciding your *purpose* — your reason for looking, right now, today — has to come first. Without a purpose, the boundary floats, and a floating boundary is exactly what makes you dizzy. Pick the purpose, and the line snaps into place.

### Idea 3: Things live inside things

Look at almost anything and you'll notice it's both a *whole thing* and a *part of a bigger thing* at the same time. One piece of BVX content is a complete thing on its own. It's also one part of a series. The series is one part of a property like *The Outliers*. The property is one part of BVX. BVX is one part of the wider market you operate in.

Each of those layers is a complete thing *and* a part. (If you ever want the technical word, each layer is called a "holon" — but you don't need the word, you just need the idea.)

This matters because of a simple, freeing rule: **you cannot understand a layer by staring at only that layer.** To understand any one layer, you look in two directions.

- You look **up**, at the bigger thing it sits inside. The bigger thing gives you your *rules and limits* — your context. (The market tells BVX what's possible; the property tells a series what it's allowed to be.)
- You look **down**, at the smaller things inside it. The smaller things show you *how it actually works* — the mechanism. (The individual pieces of content are how a series actually gets made.)

So when you feel lost — "where do I even stand?" — the answer is: stand on one layer, look up for your rules, look down for your method. You never have to understand everything at every layer at once. You only ever work one layer at a time.

### Idea 4: Why you keep going in circles

There's a name for what you're staring at when you try to understand BVX. Russell Ackoff called it a **mess** — not as an insult, but as a technical term. A mess is a bunch of problems that are all knotted together, where you can't pull one problem out and solve it cleanly because tugging on it moves all the others.

There's an even tougher version, called a **wicked problem**, where you don't even fully understand the problem until you've started trying to solve it — so understanding it and solving it are the same activity, happening at the same time. You literally cannot "fully understand it first, then act." That's not a discipline failure on your part. That kind of problem genuinely cannot be done in that order.

This is why "I'll understand the theory completely, *then* figure out the process" keeps failing you. With a mess, understanding doesn't come before the work — it comes *from* the work. You understand it a little, you draw a little, that drawing shows you the next thing to understand. It's a spiral, not a straight line. And a spiral that's moving forward is fine. The bad kind of circling is when you refuse to put anything on paper until it's all clear in your head — because in your head it will *never* get clear. Paper is where it gets clear.

So the cure for circling is almost rude in its simplicity: **stop thinking, start drawing.** Get the mess out of your head and onto a surface where you can look at it. Which is the next idea.

### Idea 5: Three different questions, three different drawings

You said it yourself, and you were right: not everything is a process. Correct. A system has different *aspects*, and each aspect needs its own picture answering its own question. Trying to answer all of them in one drawing is, again, the circling.

Here are the three questions, and the three separate pictures:

First, **what are the parts, and how are they connected?** This is the *structure* picture. Boxes for the parts, lines for the connections. No time, no sequence — just the parts and how they relate, like a family tree or an org chart.

Second, **how does work move through it, step by step, over time?** This is the *process* picture. A start, a sequence of steps, an end. For BVX, one process might be: idea → script → shoot → edit → publish → earn. Time runs left to right.

Third, **what circles back and affects itself?** This is the *feedback* picture, and it gets its own idea below.

The key move: each of these gets its **own page.** Three questions, three pages. Before you ever start, you do a fourth, messy page — explained in the checklist — where you just dump everything. So four pages total, each simple, none complete. Laid side by side, they *are* your understanding of the system. There is no fifth page that combines them. The combining happens in your eyes when you look across all four. That's the whole method.

### Idea 6: Things that loop back on themselves

A **feedback loop** is just something that circles back and affects itself. There are only two kinds, and they're easy to feel.

A **snowball loop** feeds itself and grows (the technical name is "reinforcing loop"). More BVX content gets you more audience, more audience gets you more motivation and money, which gets you more content. Round and round, getting bigger. Snowball loops are great when they're pushing the right way and brutal when they're pushing the wrong way (no content → no audience → no motivation → even less content).

A **thermostat loop** pushes back toward a target and keeps things steady (the technical name is "balancing loop"). A thermostat senses the room getting cold and turns on heat; it senses too hot and backs off. In BVX, your energy is a thermostat loop — push too hard and burnout forces you to rest, which pulls you back to a sustainable pace.

Knowing your loops matters because of one last idea: a **leverage point** — the spot where a small push creates a big change. In a snowball loop, getting the snowball *started* is a leverage point; one small early push compounds into something huge. Finding those spots is most of the payoff of doing this whole exercise.

## 3. The Checklist

Work through this in order, on real pages. Each step says which guide idea it comes from.

**Setup**

- [ ] Choose **one** system to map today. Just one. Write its name at the top of a page.
- [ ] Get four blank pages or four canvas tabs. Label them: *Mess*, *Parts*, *Process*, *Loops*.
- [ ] Say this out loud and believe it: *each page will be incomplete, and that is correct.* (Idea: core problem)

**Step 1 — Decide why you're looking**

- [ ] Write one sentence: *"Right now I am trying to understand ______ so that I can ______."* (Idea 1 & 2)
- [ ] If you can't fill the second blank, stop and figure that out first. No purpose means no map.

**Step 2 — Draw where the edge is**

- [ ] On the *Mess* page, list 3–5 things clearly **inside** this system. (Idea 2)
- [ ] List 3–5 things clearly **outside** it but touching it.
- [ ] List anything you're **unsure** about. Don't decide — just park it. It often sorts itself out later.

**Step 3 — Dump the whole mess onto one page**

- [ ] On the *Mess* page, sketch **everything**: people, money, content, tools, problems, frustrations. (Idea 4)
- [ ] Use doodles and arrows. Make it ugly. Make it visual, not just words.
- [ ] Draw the parts that feel **stuck or tangled**, not just the tidy parts.
- [ ] Do **not** organize it. When the page feels full, stop. Structure comes later, from this drawing.

**Step 4 — Draw the parts**

- [ ] On the *Parts* page, write the main components as boxes. Aim for 5–9. (Idea 5)
- [ ] Draw lines between boxes that are connected, and write what the connection *is* (owns, feeds, depends on).
- [ ] Add **no** time or sequence here. Just parts and connections. Resist the urge.

**Step 5 — Draw one process**

- [ ] On the *Process* page, pick **one** important flow and draw it start-to-end, left to right. (Idea 5)
- [ ] Mark where it **waits**, **splits**, or **hands off** to someone or something else.
- [ ] Want to map a second process? Good — but on a separate page. One process per page.

**Step 6 — Draw the loops**

- [ ] On the *Loops* page, find one **snowball loop** (more leads to more) and draw it as a circle of arrows. (Idea 6)
- [ ] Find one **thermostat loop** (something that pushes back toward steady) and draw it.
- [ ] Mark any spot where one small push would create a big change. That's a leverage point — circle it.

**Step 7 — Look up and look down**

- [ ] Write: *"This system sits inside ______, which limits or shapes it by ______."* (looking up — Idea 3)
- [ ] Write: *"Inside this system, ______ is itself made of smaller parts: ______."* (looking down — Idea 3)
- [ ] Check that Steps 4–6 all stayed at **one layer**. If a box turned out to be a whole world of its own, that's a *separate* mapping job for another day. Note it, don't do it now.

**Step 8 — Lay it out and stop**

- [ ] Put all four pages side by side. Read them **together** — that overlay is your understanding. (Idea 5)
- [ ] Ask: *did I answer the purpose from Step 1?* If yes — you are done. Actually stop.
- [ ] If a new question appeared, that's a brand-new mapping job. Start fresh at Step 1 with that new purpose. Don't cram it onto these pages.

## 4. Quick Reference

| Page | The single question it answers |
|------|--------------------------------|
| Mess | What does the whole tangled situation look like? |
| Parts | What are the pieces and how do they connect? |
| Process | How does work move through it, step by step? |
| Loops | What circles back and affects itself? |

**If you start spinning again**, it is almost always one of two things: you are trying to answer two questions on one page, or you are mixing two layers (a whole and its inner parts) in one drawing. Stop, separate them, and the spinning stops. One page, one question, one layer.

**The single sentence to remember:** You never build one perfect picture. You build four simple ones and read them together.
