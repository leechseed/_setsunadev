## 1. How to Use This

Read Part 2 and 3 slowly — that's the whole mental model, and it's short. Then treat Part 4 as a reference you dip into, not a chapter you memorize. Part 7 is the payoff: it turns all of this into a **seven-dial control panel** you can set for any character you build in BVX.

The trick from the system guide applies here too: **you don't hold all seven at once.** You look at one engine, understand it, move on. The character appears when you lay the seven settings side by side — not when you try to feel all seven in your head at the same time.

---

## 2. The One Big Idea

Here's the sentence the whole thing rests on: **emotions are not feelings that happen in your head — they're ancient action-systems built into the deep brain, and the feeling is just what the system feels like from the inside.**

Jaak Panksepp (1943–2017) was a neuroscientist who spent his career doing something most people skip: instead of asking humans "how do you feel," he went *into the brain* — stimulating specific deep-brain spots in animals and watching what happened. Same spot, stimulated in a rat, a cat, a human: same emotional behavior, every time. That told him these aren't ideas we learn or words our culture hands us. **They're pre-installed. Standard equipment. Older than thought, older than language, shared across nearly every mammal.**

He found **seven** of them. Seven separate systems, each with its own trigger, its own wiring, its own chemistry, its own behavior, and its own raw feeling. He named them in CAPITAL LETTERS on purpose — not to shout, but as a warning label. The word "fear" in everyday talk is fuzzy and means ten things. **FEAR in capitals means one specific brain system with a known address.** The capitals say: *I mean the machine, not the mood.* Keep that convention and you'll never confuse the two.

Why this matters for BVX: it means a character's emotional core is **not** an infinite fog you have to intuit. It's seven dials. A person — real or written — is largely *which of these seven run hot, which run cold, and which one got wounded early.* That's a designable object. That's the point of this document.

## 3. The Three Floors of the Mind

Before the seven engines, one piece of scaffolding. Panksepp said the emotional mind has **three floors**, and every character lives on all three at once.

**The basement — raw engines (born with).** The seven systems themselves. No learning required, no words, no thought. A newborn already has them running. Panksepp's term is *primary process*. Call it the basement: it's always down there, humming, whether you look or not.

**The middle floor — habits (learned).** What the basement engines got *attached to* through experience. A kid whose FEAR engine fired every time a door slammed now flinches at slammed doors for life. Nothing was decided; it was wired in by repetition. Panksepp's term is *secondary process*. This is habit, conditioning, the automatic reaction. Call it the middle floor.

**The top floor — thoughts and story (thought about thought).** The talking, planning, self-aware narrator. "I get angry because my father was a bully, and I'm working on it." This floor tells stories about the other two — and can sometimes reach down and steer them, but not always, and not easily. Panksepp's term is *tertiary process*. Call it the top floor.

**The freeing part: most of what makes a character *feel real* lives in the basement and middle floor — below their own narration.** Your character's top-floor story about themselves ("I'm easygoing") can flat-out contradict their basement ("RAGE engine idling hot") — and *that gap is where interesting characters live.* Hold onto that; it's a design goldmine, and we'll come back to it.

## 4. The Seven Engines, One at a Time

Each engine below follows the same little template so you can scan it:

- **What flips it on** — the trigger.
- **What it makes you do** — the behavior.
- **What it feels like** — the view from inside.
- **Under the hood** — brain and chemistry, kept light.
- **Stuck on / Stuck off** — what it looks like jammed high or starved. *(This column is where characters are born.)*
- **Character note** — the design hook.

### SEEKING — the go engine

The most important one, so it goes first. Panksepp called SEEKING the "granddaddy" system — **the engine that powers all the other engines.** It's the raw urge to go *out* into the world and get involved with it: hunt, explore, wonder, pursue, want.

- **What flips it on:** an opportunity, a need, something novel, a "what's over there?" — anything that says *there's something to go after.*
- **What it makes you do:** explore, forage, approach, investigate, chase, work-toward. Leaning in.
- **What it feels like:** eager anticipation, curiosity, interest, enthusiasm, the pleasant itch of *wanting.*
- **Under the hood:** the mesolimbic dopamine pathway — deep-brain structures (the ventral tegmental area up to the nucleus accumbens) running on **dopamine.** Here's the twist that matters: dopamine is the *wanting* chemical, **not** the *getting* chemical. SEEKING is the thrill of the chase, not the satisfaction of the catch. **The pursuit itself is the reward.** This is why the hunt is more alive than the trophy — a fact you can build entire characters and stories around.
- **Stuck on:** mania, addiction (wanting that never lands), conspiracy-brain (finding "meaning" and connection everywhere). Endless pursuit, no rest.
- **Stuck off:** depression, apathy, "why bother," the couch you can't get off. When SEEKING dies, the whole dashboard goes dark — nothing feels worth chasing.
- **Character note:** SEEKING is the **drive dial.** High = the restless striver, the obsessive, the adventurer who can't sit still. Low = the flat, the burned-out, the depressive. This is usually the single most important dial for how *alive* a character reads on the page.

### RAGE — the blocked engine

- **What flips it on:** frustration. Being **thwarted, restrained, or blocked** from something you're going after. (Literally: physically holding an animal down fires this. The feeling of being *trapped or restrained* is a pure RAGE trigger.) Also resource competition and bodily irritation.
- **What it makes you do:** push back, attack, strike out, break the thing in the way. "Affective attack" — hot, loud, defensive.
- **What it feels like:** heat, indignation, the red flash, *"get off me / out of my way."*
- **Under the hood:** a column running from the amygdala down through the medial hypothalamus to the PAG (periaqueductal gray, a deep brainstem hub). Chemistry includes substance P and glutamate; testosterone turns up the gain.
- **Stuck on:** chronic hostility, the short fuse, violence.
- **Stuck off:** the pushover, the doormat, the one who can't defend a boundary even when they should.
- **Character note:** RAGE is not villainy — **it's the engine of the blocked.** A character's RAGE tells you what they *can't stand being stopped from.* Note the crucial split: hot RAGE ("get off me!") is a totally different engine from the cold, calm aggression of a predator stalking prey — that cold hunt is **SEEKING**, not RAGE. A calculating villain runs on SEEKING. A wounded, cornered one runs on RAGE. Very different characters.

### FEAR — the danger engine

- **What flips it on:** threat, danger, pain, the cues that reliably came before harm.
- **What it makes you do:** two settings only. Threat far off → **freeze** (go still, don't be seen). Threat close and real → **flee** (run). Trembling, scanning, bracing.
- **What it feels like:** dread, foreboding, the cold drop in the stomach, anxiety.
- **Under the hood:** a flow from the central amygdala through the hypothalamus to the PAG. Anti-anxiety drugs quiet exactly this circuit.
- **Stuck on:** anxiety, phobia, the trauma-wired flinch, the character who reads danger everywhere.
- **Stuck off:** the reckless one, the character with no brakes — thrilling and doomed in equal measure.
- **Character note:** FEAR is the **caution dial**, and it pairs against SEEKING like a seesaw. High SEEKING + low FEAR = the reckless adventurer. Low SEEKING + high FEAR = the anxious shut-in. Where you set these two *relative to each other* defines a character's whole relationship with risk.

### LUST — the mating engine

- **What flips it on:** sexual cues, a desired other, hormonal state.
- **What it makes you do:** pursue, court, seek union.
- **What it feels like:** desire, attraction, longing of the appetite kind.
- **Under the hood:** hypothalamic circuits (notably the preoptic area), driven by testosterone and estrogen, laced with **oxytocin and vasopressin** — the same bonding chemicals CARE uses, which is exactly why desire and tenderness get tangled.
- **Stuck on:** obsession, jealousy, the character ruled by appetite.
- **Stuck off:** the ascetic, the character for whom that whole channel is simply switched off.
- **Character note:** LUST is the **appetite dial** — and the interesting design move is rarely LUST alone. It's how LUST *blends* with CARE (desire fused with tenderness = romantic love) or collides with it (desire with no tenderness = a very different character). Their overlapping chemistry is why you can write love, lust, and their tragic mismatch as three distinct things.

### CARE — the tending engine

- **What flips it on:** vulnerability — offspring, "cuteness," anyone small and in need. Baby signals hit this directly.
- **What it makes you do:** nurture, protect, feed, groom, tend, shelter.
- **What it feels like:** tenderness, warmth, protectiveness, that melt.
- **Under the hood:** hypothalamic circuits soaked in **oxytocin, prolactin, and natural opioids** — the brain's own bonding-and-comfort chemistry.
- **Stuck on:** the smotherer, the martyr who cares past all boundaries, the one who needs to be needed.
- **Stuck off:** coldness, neglect, the parent who can't.
- **Character note:** CARE is the **warmth dial.** It's not weakness — a fierce protector is CARE fused with RAGE (tenderness that will *fight* for what it tends). Some of the most powerful characters are high CARE **and** high RAGE at once: gentle until you threaten what they love.

### PANIC/GRIEF — the alone engine

The one people miss, and often the deepest. Panksepp originally called it PANIC; it's the **separation-distress system** — the raw pain of being *alone.*

- **What flips it on:** separation, loss, rejection, exclusion, isolation. Being left.
- **What it makes you do:** first **protest** (the cry, the call, the reaching-out to restore contact), then, if that fails, **despair** (withdrawal, collapse, giving up).
- **What it feels like:** loneliness, the panic of abandonment, grief, the ache of a "broken heart."
- **Under the hood:** the anterior cingulate and related circuits — and here's the stunner: **social pain runs on the same wiring as physical pain.** The soothing chemicals are natural **opioids and oxytocin.** Panksepp discovered this by noticing that low-dose morphine quieted the distress-cries of puppies. **The reason a broken heart *literally hurts* is that being left triggers the body's actual pain system.** Not a metaphor — the same machine.
- **Stuck on:** grief that won't lift, separation anxiety, the depressive despair-state, the character who cannot be alone.
- **Stuck off:** the loner who genuinely doesn't need anyone — rare, and unsettling to others.
- **Character note:** PANIC/GRIEF is the **attachment dial**, and it is your single best source of a character's **core wound.** "What does being left do to this person?" is often the most revealing question you can ask about them. Most backstory pain lives here.

### PLAY — the joy engine

- **What flips it on:** safety **plus** a companion. Play only switches on when an animal feels *secure and not needy* — no play when you're scared, starving, or alone. It's the "all's well, let's mess around" system.
- **What it makes you do:** rough-and-tumble — chase, wrestle, pin and get pinned, tease, joke. Kids and young animals do this constantly.
- **What it feels like:** joy, delight, social glee, fun, laughter.
- **Under the hood:** deep and subcortical — Panksepp famously found that **rats "laugh"** (a 50-kHz chirp) when tickled, and that even rats with the whole cortex removed still play. So play doesn't come from the thinking brain; it comes from *underneath* it. Runs on opioids and dopamine.
- **What it's FOR:** this is the system that **builds the social brain.** Play is how young mammals learn the rules — turn-taking, limits, how hard is too hard, winning and losing, roles. It's rehearsal for the whole social world.
- **Stuck on:** the manic prankster who can't be serious; Panksepp even linked play-*deprivation* to attention problems in kids.
- **Stuck off:** joyless, stiff, socially awkward, the character who never learned how to be light.
- **Character note:** PLAY is the **lightness dial**, and it's the seat of a character's sense of humor and their ease with others. It also connects straight into the Keltner document — **teasing is PLAY wearing social clothes.** The two frameworks shake hands right here.

## 5. The Master Table

The seven engines at a glance. This table alone is a usable character worksheet — read down the "Stuck on / off" column and you're basically reading a cast of characters.

| Engine | Plain name | Turns on when… | Makes you… | Feels like | Runs on | Stuck ON | Stuck OFF |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SEEKING** | the go engine | there's something to pursue | explore, chase, want | eager, curious | dopamine | mania, addiction | apathy, depression |
| **RAGE** | the blocked engine | you're thwarted/restrained | attack, push back | hot anger | substance P, testosterone | hostility | doormat |
| **FEAR** | the danger engine | threat appears | freeze or flee | dread | glutamate | anxiety, phobia | recklessness |
| **LUST** | the mating engine | desire is cued | pursue, court | attraction | sex hormones, oxytocin | obsession | switched-off |
| **CARE** | the tending engine | something's vulnerable | nurture, protect | tenderness | oxytocin, opioids | smothering | coldness |
| **PANIC/GRIEF** | the alone engine | you're separated/left | cry out, then despair | loneliness, grief | opioids (pain system) | can't be alone | needs no one |
| **PLAY** | the joy engine | safe + a companion | wrestle, joke, romp | delight | opioids, dopamine | manic pranking | joyless, stiff |

## 6. Two Things Worth Knowing Before You Design

**One: SEEKING sits above the other six.** It's the general-purpose "go" that the others aim. FEAR aims SEEKING away from a threat; CARE aims it toward the vulnerable; LUST aims it toward a mate. So when a character feels *dead on the page*, the fix is almost always SEEKING — turn their wanting back on and the other engines have something to push against. **SEEKING is the ignition; the other six are the steering.**

**Two: personality is a *balance*, not a single trait — and this is already a proven idea, not a metaphor.** Panksepp's team built an actual personality test, the **Affective Neuroscience Personality Scales (ANPS)**, that scores a real person on six of these systems (they left LUST out for polite reasons). It works. People really do differ measurably in how hot each engine idles. Which means the move this whole document is building toward — *defining a character as a profile across the seven engines* — isn't a cute trick. **It's how the science already models a real human being.** You're not simplifying personality; you're using its actual chassis.

And the tie-back to the system guide: a "disorder" in Panksepp's world is just an engine stuck too far in one direction — depression as SEEKING-off plus PANIC-on, anxiety as FEAR-on, and so forth. That's the guide's **"stuck loop"** idea in emotional form. A compelling character is very often *a person organized around one stuck engine* — and their arc is that engine finally moving.

## 7. Turning the Engines Into a Character Model

Here's the payoff — the reusable model for BVX. It has one core move and five readouts.

### The core move: set the seven dials

For any character, give each engine a level: **Low · Medium · High · Wounded.**

"Wounded" is the special one: an engine that got hurt early and now misfires — either jammed on (a PANIC-Wounded character reads abandonment into everything) or defensively shut off (a CARE-Wounded character can't let warmth in). **Most characters have exactly one Wounded engine. That wound is their center of gravity.** Everything else orbits it.

That's it. Seven quick settings. From those seven, the character mostly writes itself — here's what each reading gives you.

### The five readouts

Once the dials are set, read them in these five directions:

**1. Default temperature — who they are at rest.** Whichever engine idles highest sets their resting mood. High SEEKING → restless and driven. High FEAR → wary and tense. High PLAY → light and mischievous. High CARE → warm and maternal. High PANIC → melancholy and clingy. High RAGE → coiled and combative. This is the vibe a reader feels the moment the character walks in.

**2. What they chase — their engine of desire.** SEEKING is *that* they want; the dominant engine is *what* they want. SEEKING pointed through CARE = wants someone to protect. Through RAGE = wants a wrong to right. Through PANIC = wants never to be left again. This is their spine — the want that drives the plot.

**3. Their wound — what breaks them.** Find the Wounded dial. That's the pressure point, the thing a scene can press to crack them open. PANIC-Wounded → break them with abandonment. FEAR-Wounded → break them by removing safety. RAGE-Wounded → break them with helplessness/injustice. CARE-Wounded → break them by making them need someone.

**4. How they love and how they fight — the blends.** Love is a *blend*: CARE + LUST + PANIC in some ratio (tenderness, desire, and need — the mix is the whole flavor of the relationship). Fighting is RAGE's style, colored by FEAR (does threat make them attack or freeze?). You don't write "they're in love" — you write *the ratio.*

**5. Their arc — the dial that moves.** A character arc is one engine changing setting across the story. The frozen FEAR-character who finally lets SEEKING pull them into the world. The RAGE-driven one who discovers CARE. The PANIC-Wounded one who learns they can survive being alone. **Pick the engine that moves, and you've got the arc.**

### Casting for chemistry

Two characters *spark* based on how their profiles meet. **Complementary** engines soothe (a high-PANIC character calmed by a high-CARE one — need meets tending). **Clashing** engines strike sparks (two high-RAGE characters; or high-SEEKING recklessness against high-FEAR caution — the classic road-trip odd couple). When you need a duo to crackle, don't write the banter first. **Set both dial-boards, look at where they rub, and the friction writes the banter for you.**

### Worked example (swap in your own cast)

*Character: a retired fixer pulled back for one last job.*

- SEEKING: **Medium** (was High once; the fire's banked, not out)
- RAGE: **High** (fast to anger when cornered)
- FEAR: **Low** (hard to rattle — he's seen it all)
- LUST: **Low**
- CARE: **High but hidden** (protects the young operative he won't admit he's fond of)
- PANIC/GRIEF: **Wounded** (lost someone; can't be close without bracing for the loss)
- PLAY: **Medium**, dry and sardonic (his humor is armor)

Read it out: he's calm-dangerous at rest (low FEAR, high RAGE), pulled back into action because his banked SEEKING got a spark, protective in spite of himself (hidden CARE), and his whole quiet tragedy is the **PANIC wound** — he keeps everyone at arm's length because being left once nearly killed him. **His arc:** the CARE finally overrides the PANIC-wound, and he lets someone close, knowing the cost. You didn't write a backstory paragraph — you set seven dials, and the character, the wound, and the arc fell out of them.

## 8. The Checklist

Do this on a real page, one character at a time. (Straight from the system guide: one page, one thing, don't try to hold it all at once.)

**Setup**
- [ ] Name one character at the top of the page. Just one.
- [ ] Draw seven labeled slots: SEEKING, RAGE, FEAR, LUST, CARE, PANIC/GRIEF, PLAY.

**Set the dials**
- [ ] Mark each engine **Low / Medium / High**.
- [ ] Circle exactly **one** as **Wounded** — the center of gravity. (If two beg to be wounded, pick the deeper one; the other is probably just "High.")

**Read the five readouts**
- [ ] **Temperature:** which engine idles highest? That's their resting vibe.
- [ ] **Chase:** SEEKING pointed *through* which engine? That's their want.
- [ ] **Wound:** what does pressing the Wounded dial do to them?
- [ ] **Blends:** write the love-ratio (CARE/LUST/PANIC) and the fight-style (RAGE/FEAR).
- [ ] **Arc:** which single dial moves by the end? That's the story.

**Check it against the floors**
- [ ] Is there a gap between their **top-floor story** ("I'm fine / I don't need anyone") and their **basement** (the engine that says otherwise)? If yes — good. **That gap is the character.** If the story and the engines agree perfectly, the character is probably flat; find the contradiction.

**Stop**
- [ ] Seven dials set, five readouts read, one gap found. That's a whole character core. Don't add more. Put the page next to your Keltner worksheet (how they *act toward others*) and you've got the inside and the outside both.
